# Glorantha

**Glorantha** is a bronze age mythological fantasy world created by Greg Stafford in 1966 and first published as the setting for RuneQuest in 1978 (with earlier appearance in White Bear & Red Moon board game, 1975). Unlike medieval European-inspired fantasy settings, Glorantha draws from diverse real-world mythologies—Greek, Indian, Native American, Celtic, and others—creating a world where myths are literally true, gods actively involve themselves in mortal affairs, and magic stems from divine relationships rather than academic study. The setting features distinctive elements like ducks (intelligent anthropomorphic ducks as a playable race), broo (chaos-corrupted beast-men), and the God Learners (a destroyed civilization whose hubris in systematizing mythology brought divine wrath). With its emphasis on heroquesting (mythic reenactment to change reality), rune magic (power drawn from cosmic principles), and deeply immersive cultures, Glorantha represents one of RPG gaming's most thoroughly developed and distinctively non-traditional settings.

## History and Development

Glorantha originated in Greg Stafford's personal world-building beginning in 1966, initially inspired by his study of comparative mythology, anthropology, and religious studies at Beloit College. Stafford created Glorantha not as a game setting but as a thought experiment exploring how a world where myths were literally true might function. The setting's core conceits—that gods exist and actively involve themselves in the world, that magic derives from divine relationships, and that cultural beliefs shape reality—stemmed from this anthropological approach.

The first published Gloranthan product was "White Bear and Red Moon" (1975), a board game depicting the conflict between the Lunar Empire and the barbarian tribes of Dragon Pass. This game introduced key Gloranthan elements: the Lunar Way (a sophisticated magical philosophy based on illumination and cyclic time), Orlanth (storm god worshipped by barbarian tribes), and the fundamental conflict between Lunar civilization and Orlanthi freedom. The board game's success suggested a market for Stafford's mythological world-building.

RuneQuest, designed by Steve Perrin with significant input from Greg Stafford and published by Chaosium in 1978, became Glorantha's primary vehicle. The game's mechanics integrated deeply with Glorantha's mythology: rune magic came from cults worshipping specific deities, skills improved through use rather than class-level advancement, and combat was deadly enough that players sought diplomatic or magical solutions rather than assuming combat superiority. The original "RuneQuest" rulebook described Dragon Pass in detail, establishing Glorantha's bronze age technology level, complex tribal cultures, and myth-based magic.

The setting expanded through supplements throughout the late 1970s and 1980s. "Cults of Prax" (1979) detailed the religion of the desert-dwelling nomads, establishing the cult system as Glorantha's central social and magical structure. Each cult description included mythology, holy days, cult skills, rune spells, and social structure, making religion mechanically and narratively central. "Griffin Mountain" (1981) provided a campaign setting in the frontier region of Balazar. "Borderlands" (1982) detailed the city of Pavis and its surroundings, becoming one of the most extensively developed campaign locations.

The "Cults of Terror" (1981) introduced chaos cults and monsters, including the infamous broo—chaos-corrupted, disease-carrying beast-men capable of crossbreeding with anything and everything. The broo became iconic (and controversial) Gloranthan creatures, embodying chaos as genuine cosmic force of corruption rather than mere "evil alignment."

The God Learners, detailed extensively in "Nomad Gods" (1977) and other products, represented Stafford's commentary on systematizing mythology. This ancient maritime civilization (destroyed centuries before the typical campaign era) had approached mythology scientifically, discovering the "true" relationships between gods and systematically exploiting divine power. The gods themselves destroyed the God Learners for their hubris, sinking continents and rewriting history. This narrative warned against reducing Glorantha's myths to mere game mechanics, emphasizing that respectful engagement with mythology was thematically appropriate while mechanistic exploitation risked divine wrath (narratively if not mechanically).

Avalon Hill acquired RuneQuest in 1984, publishing RuneQuest 3rd Edition (often called "Avalon Hill RuneQuest") from 1984-1995. This era expanded Glorantha extensively. "Genertela: Crucible of the Hero Wars" (1988) detailed the northern continent comprehensively. "Troll Gods" (1989) explored troll mythology and culture in depth, making trolls (actually called "uz" in Gloranthan languages) as complex and developed as human cultures. The "Borderlands" and "Pavis" material was extensively revised and expanded.

The setting's metaplot centered on the approaching Hero Wars—prophesied conflicts where heroes would reshape reality through heroquesting and divine intervention. This provided apocalyptic stakes while allowing individual campaigns to participate in or ignore the approaching conflicts.

Chaosium regained RuneQuest rights in 1999, but Avalon Hill retained rights to publish materials using the "RuneQuest" name for existing products. This legal complexity led to the "Hero Wars" RPG (2000), renamed "HeroQuest" for its second edition (2003), using new mechanics while remaining set in Glorantha. HeroQuest emphasized narrative freedom and character-defined abilities over traditional skill lists, creating a radically different mechanical approach to the same setting.

The publication history became fragmented. Mongoose Publishing released RuneQuest (2006) and RuneQuest II (2010), both set in Glorantha but using mechanics derived from the d100 system. The Design Mechanism's "RuneQuest 6th Edition" (2012) used similar mechanics but wasn't explicitly Gloranthan (though easily compatible). Moon Design Publications (later absorbed into Chaosium) published extensive Gloranthan material from 2005-2015, including the massive "Guide to Glorantha" (2014)—a two-volume, 800+ page comprehensive encyclopedia.

Chaosium's "RuneQuest: Roleplaying in Glorantha" (2018, often called "RuneQuest 4th Edition" or "RQG") returned to Stafford's original vision, updating RuneQuest 2nd Edition's mechanics while maintaining its spirit. This edition has received extensive support and commercial success, demonstrating continued market appetite for Glorantha.

13th Age Glorantha (2018) adapted the setting to 13th Age's mechanics, providing an alternative system while maintaining setting fidelity. This demonstrated Glorantha's setting-mechanical flexibility—the mythology and cultures work with various rule systems.

Greg Stafford's death in 2018 marked the end of an era, but his extensive notes and decades of development ensure Glorantha's continuation. Chaosium continues publishing Gloranthan material, with Jeff Richard as creative director maintaining Stafford's vision while allowing careful expansion.

## Geography and Cosmology

Glorantha is not a planet but a cube floating in an infinite sea, enclosed by the Sky Dome above and supported by the underworld below. This cosmology reflects mythological rather than astronomical principles—Glorantha is fundamentally magical, not a science-fictional world with planets and space.

The primary campaign region is Dragon Pass in Genertela (the northern continent). This temperate region of hills, valleys, and mountain ranges is inhabited by Orlanthi barbarian tribes who worship storm god Orlanth and earth goddess Ernalda. The region's name derives from ancient dragons who inhabited it before humans arrived. Dragon Pass contains roughly a dozen major tribal confederations, numerous minor clans, and contested territories.

Sartar is the most detailed kingdom in Dragon Pass, a confederation of tribes unified (before the typical campaign start) by Sartar Lightbringer and subsequently conquered by the Lunar Empire. Sartar's resistance against Lunar occupation provides the default campaign framework for many RuneQuest campaigns. The clans of Sartar—Colymar, Lismelder, Kultain, and others—each have distinct histories, feuds, and traditions.

Pavis is a frontier city on the edge of Prax (the vast desert to the east), built on ancient giant ruins called the Big Rubble. The city serves as a campaign hub where Orlanthi, Praxian nomads, Lunar colonists, and chaos cultists interact. The Big Rubble contains ancient treasures, dangerous ruins, and the nonhumans who inhabit the remnants. Pavis has been extensively detailed across multiple editions, making it one of Glorantha's most developed locations.

Prax is a massive desert inhabited by nomadic beast riders—the Bison Riders, High Llama Riders, Impala Riders, and others, each tribe riding and herding specific beast types. Praxian culture centers on their beasts, with horse-riding considered perverse (horses are carnivorous predators in Prax, eating humans rather than grass). The River of Cradles (later called Zola Fel) provides the only reliable water, with city-states along its banks serving as trading posts.

The Lunar Empire dominates northern Genertela, a sophisticated civilization based on the Red Goddess—a deity who achieved divinity in historical time (approximately 400 years before typical campaigns). The Lunar Way teaches that opposites can be reconciled through illumination, that change is the only constant, and that the Red Goddess embodies this wisdom. Lunar culture is literate, urbane, and magically sophisticated, contrasting with Orlanthi tribalism. The empire's expansionism brings it into conflict with Dragon Pass tribes, driving much campaign conflict.

Other Genertelan regions include the Holy Country (diverse coastal region with multiple cultures and deity-kings ruling different provinces), Ralios (western region of city-states and wilderness), and Fronela (northern feudal kingdoms). Each region has distinct cultures, though Dragon Pass receives most detailed development.

Pamaltela, the southern continent, features distinct mythologies and cultures. Doraddi city-states worship Pamalt (an agricultural deity), while maritime Fonritians and jungle Umathings represent different cultural approaches. Pamaltela receives less detailed development than Genertela but provides adventure opportunities beyond the northern continent.

The cosmology centers on the Gods War—the mythological age when gods directly walked Glorantha, fought tremendous conflicts, and eventually withdrew to the God Time (mythological realm separate from linear time). Mortal history began when the gods withdrew, creating Time and allowing linear progression. However, the gods remain active through their worshippers, granting magic to cultists who maintain proper relationships.

The Underworld exists beneath Glorantha, home to earth goddesses and dark gods. The Sky Dome arches overhead, home to celestial deities. The Middle World (Glorantha's surface) is where mortals live. The God Time exists outside linear time, accessible through heroquesting—ritual reenactment of myths allowing participants to enter mythological events and potentially change their outcomes, thereby changing reality itself.

Runes are fundamental cosmic principles: elemental runes (Water, Earth, Air, Fire, Darkness, Moon), power runes (Death, Fertility, Harmony, Disorder, Illusion, Truth, Stasis, Movement), and form runes (Man, Beast, Plant, Dragonewt, etc.). Every entity in Glorantha embodies certain runes. Orlanth embodies Air, Movement, and Disorder. Ernalda embodies Earth and Fertility. Characters have runic affinities determining personality and magical capability.

## Cultures and Factions

Gloranthan cultures are deeply developed through mythology, daily life, social structure, and religious practice, creating immersive anthropological detail exceeding most fantasy settings.

Orlanthi culture dominates Dragon Pass, organized into clans within tribes within kingdoms. Clan members share bloodlines, lands, and worship at common temples. The clan assembly makes collective decisions, with the chieftain leading in war but consulting the ring (council of notable clan members) for major choices. Honor, hospitality, and cattle raiding are culturally central—cows represent wealth, and raiding neighboring clans for cattle is legitimate competition rather than criminal activity (though murder during raids can trigger blood feuds).

Gender roles in Orlanthi culture are complex. Men typically worship Orlanth and serve as warriors and farmers. Women typically worship Ernalda and manage households, crafts, and agricultural magic. However, numerous deities and cults exist for those who don't fit these patterns: Vinga (Orlanth's daughter, allowing women to be warriors), Nandan (Ernalda's son, allowing men to be weavers and brewers), and others. This creates gender expectations without absolute restrictions, reflecting bronze age cultures' more fluid gender roles than medieval European models.

Lunar culture emphasizes literacy, urban life, and the Lunar Way's philosophical sophistication. The Seven Mothers cult formed the empire's foundation, with seven deities collaborating to birth the Red Goddess. Lunar citizens see themselves as bringing civilization to barbarous regions, offering enlightenment through illumination. The Lunar Empire's moral complexity comes from genuine cultural sophistication combined with imperial conquest and religious intolerance (they hunt chaos, but also suppress non-Lunar religions in conquered territories).

Praxian nomads organize into tribes based on their beasts. The Bison Tribe is the largest and most aggressive. The High Llama Riders are contemplative mystics. The Sable Riders are matriarchal. Each tribe's mythology and culture center on their beast, with the beast providing food, transportation, shelter (hides for tents), and social identity. Praxians despise farmers (called "dirt eaters") and urban dwellers, considering the nomadic life superior.

Trolls (uz) are not evil monsters but a sophisticated culture with complex mythology. Descended from the goddess Kyger Litor, trolls inhabited Glorantha before humans arrived. Troll society is matriarchal, with dark trolls (full-sized) forming the aristocracy, trollkin (small, weak trolls) serving as workers and food, and cave trolls providing muscle. Trolls worship Kyger Litor and her pantheon, practice intricate social hierarchies based on darkness affinity, and consider humans recent interlopers. The "Troll Gods" supplement develops troll culture as thoroughly as human cultures, making them playable with full cultural depth.

Ducks (Durulz in their language) are intelligent anthropomorphic ducks, created when an ancient magical experiment went wrong. Despite appearing comical, ducks are treated seriously within Glorantha—they have their own mythology, worship duck versions of Orlanthi gods, and face prejudice from humans who see them as oddities. Ducks embody Glorantha's willingness to include non-traditional fantasy elements while treating them with cultural respect rather than as jokes.

The Lunars organize into cults and government structures. The Red Emperor rules from Glamour, the Lunar capital. Provincial governors administer conquered territories. The Lunar College of Magic researches sorcerous techniques. The Lunar Army includes professional soldiers, magical support units, and the dreaded Crimson Bat (a chaos monster the empire unleashed and now barely controls). This creates layered antagonists—individual Lunar NPCs might be sympathetic, cultured, even heroic, while serving an empire whose conquest threatens player characters' homes.

Chaos cults represent genuine cosmic threat. Unlike "evil" as mere alignment, chaos is a corrupting force that destroyed gods during the Gods War and continues threatening reality. Thed, the mother of broo, is chaos incarnate. Thanatar teaches vampirism and undeath. Vivamort grants power through cannibalism. These cults offer mechanically powerful magic at the cost of corruption—gradual transformation into chaos horrors. Chaos isn't an organization to negotiate with; it's cosmic entropy to be resisted.

## Distinctive Elements

Glorantha's most distinctive element is mythology-as-literal-truth. The myths aren't ancient stories or metaphors—they're descriptions of actual events in the God Time. When Orlanthi tell how Orlanth killed Ying (emperor of heaven) and brought death into the world, they describe something that genuinely happened. This makes mythology central to understanding the setting rather than decorative background. Characters' religious beliefs aren't faith-based but based on verified divine interaction.

The cult system replaces traditional classes with religious affiliation as primary character identity. Rather than "I'm a fighter," Gloranthan characters say "I'm an initiate of Orlanth Adventurous" or "I worship Seven Mothers." Cults provide skills, rune magic, community, and social role. Advancing in a cult requires upholding its myths, participating in holy days, and serving cult interests. This makes religion mechanically central while creating deep cultural immersion.

Heroquesting allows mortals to enter mythological time and reenact (or alter) myths, potentially changing reality. A successful heroquest might recover a lost magic, create a new myth, or fundamentally alter how the world works. However, heroquesting is dangerous—entering mythological time means confronting gods, walking paths where ancient heroes died, and risking transformation or destruction. Failed heroquests can destroy entire clans. This mechanic makes mythology interactive rather than static background.

Rune magic derives from gods through cult membership rather than individual study. Initiates receive a limited number of rune points (spiritual connection to their deity) allowing them to cast rune spells granted by their cult. Using rune magic temporarily reduces connection to the god, requiring time to regenerate. This makes magic feel like genuine divine favor rather than personal power—characters are channels for their gods' will.

The bronze age technology level distinguishes Glorantha from medieval-fantasy settings. Iron is rare and magical (called "thunderstone" or "starmetal"), with bronze being the standard metal for weapons and tools. Literacy is uncommon, making writing magical and mysterious. Warfare involves spear walls, chariot-riding nobles, and heroic single combat rather than armored knights. This creates a distinctly different aesthetic and set of cultural assumptions.

The setting's moral complexity avoids simple good-versus-evil frameworks. The Lunar Empire is sophisticated and culturally rich but imperialist and oppressive. Orlanthi are freedom-loving and honorable but also cattle raiders and tribal warriors. Chaos is genuinely evil and corruptive, but chaos cultists might have comprehensible motivations. This complexity makes conflicts about cultural values and competing interests rather than alignment teams.

The lack of "evil" races creates nuanced interactions. Trolls, ducks, broo, and dragonewts each have their own mythologies, cultures, and perspectives. Trolls aren't monsters to kill but a civilization to negotiate with, trade with, or fight based on circumstances. This anthropological approach treats non-human cultures as cultures rather than monster categories.

The setting's deep history includes multiple ages: the Gods War (mythological time), the Dawn Age (when time began), the Storm Age (establishing human kingdoms), and the current Third Age. Each age has distinct characteristics, with historical events creating current conflicts and opportunities. The approaching Hero Wars (prophesied conflicts transforming Glorantha fundamentally) provide apocalyptic stakes.

## Mechanical Integration

RuneQuest's mechanics integrate deeply with Gloranthan mythology. The original RuneQuest (1978-1984) established core systems: skill-based rather than class-based character advancement (skills improve through use), percentile-based task resolution, and hit-location-based combat making fights deadly and encouraging non-combat problem-solving.

Rune magic came from cult membership, with initiates gaining access to specific spells based on their deity. Orlanth provides Thunderbolt and Wind Words. Ernalda provides Bless Crops and Heal Body. Each cult's spell list reflects their mythological portfolio, making magic feel integrated with religion. Rune points limit casting, representing spiritual connection to the deity.

Spirit magic (later called battle magic) provides common spells available through shamanic spirits or minor cults, offering utility magic for everyday needs. Bladesharp increases weapon damage, Protection creates magical armor, Detect spells reveal hidden things. Spirit magic uses magic points rather than rune points, being more accessible but less powerful.

Sorcery (introduced in later supplements) represents the God Learners' systematic approach to magic—academic study allowing spell creation and manipulation. Sorcery is powerful but time-consuming to cast, lacks the immediate divine favor of rune magic, and risks divine displeasure. The Lunars use sorcery extensively, making it associated with civilization and magical research.

Characteristic-based skills derive from attributes, creating organic character creation where someone strong naturally has better combat abilities but someone intelligent has better magic potential. Skills improve through successful use and training, creating gradual development rather than level-based jumps.

HeroQuest (2000-2015) radically departed from RuneQuest's mechanics, using keyword-based character creation and narrative resolution. Characters defined themselves through cultural, occupational, and magical keywords, with specific abilities rated from these. Contests resolved through opposed rolls or extended conflicts with narrative stakes rather than hit points. This system prioritized storytelling and character concept over mechanical detail.

RuneQuest: Roleplaying in Glorantha (2018) returned to percentile skills and rune magic while modernizing presentation. Characters have Rune affinities (Air, Earth, etc.) and Passion ratings (Love Family, Loyalty Tribe, Honor, etc.) that both mechanically affect abilities and define personality. Rune affinities can be invoked for skill bonuses or opposed by opponents exploiting weaknesses. Passions can inspire heroic efforts or create emotional compulsions.

Cults receive extensive mechanical and narrative treatment. Each cult description includes mythology, subcults, cult skills, rune spells, holy days, and social structure. Advancement within cults requires meeting skill requirements, performing cult service, and demonstrating appropriate behavior. Becoming a Rune Lord (cult warrior elite) or Rune Priest (cult magical specialist) requires mastery of cult skills and complete dedication.

Heroquesting uses special mechanics where participants enter mythological narratives, facing challenges that require mythological knowledge, appropriate abilities, and often divine intervention. Success brings rewards (magical items, new rune spells, healed lands), while failure means death, transformation, or spiritual damage. The mechanics deliberately create high stakes, making heroquests rare and significant events rather than routine adventures.

## Published Material

The original RuneQuest (1978-1984) line included "RuneQuest" core rules (1st and 2nd editions), "Cults of Prax" (1979), "Trollpak" (1982, later expanded into "Troll Gods"), "Griffin Mountain" (1981), "Borderlands" (1982), and numerous supplements. These Chaosium publications established Glorantha's core regions and mythology.

The Avalon Hill era (1984-1995) produced RuneQuest 3rd Edition and extensive supplements. "Genertela: Crucible of the Hero Wars" (1988), "Troll Gods" (1989), "Sun County" (1988), "Dorastor: Land of Doom" (1989), and many others expanded the setting comprehensively. The "Borderlands" and "Pavis" material received revised and expanded versions.

The HeroQuest era (2000-2015) saw different mechanical approaches to Glorantha. "Hero Wars" (2000), "HeroQuest" (2003), and "HeroQuest 2" (2009) provided increasingly refined narrative mechanics. "Sartar: Kingdom of Heroes" (2009) and "Pavis: Gateway to Adventure" (2009) extensively detailed regions using HeroQuest mechanics.

The "Guide to Glorantha" (2014) from Moon Design Publications was a massive two-volume, 800+ page encyclopedia detailing every region, culture, and deity. This comprehensive reference represents the culmination of decades of development, though its encyclopedic scope makes it more reference than introductory material.

RuneQuest: Roleplaying in Glorantha (2018-present) has received extensive support. The core rulebook, "RuneQuest Gamemaster Screen Pack," "The Smoking Ruin and Other Stories," "The Pegasus Plateau and Other Stories," "The Lightbringers" (campaign), and numerous supplements continue expanding the setting. The "RuneQuest Starter Set" (2022) provides accessible entry point for newcomers.

13th Age Glorantha (2018) adapted the setting to 13th Age mechanics, demonstrating Glorantha's system flexibility. "The Red Cow" campaign volumes explore clan-level play in Sartar during the Lunar occupation.

Fiction set in Glorantha includes Greg Stafford's "King of Sartar" (1992), a historiographical novel presented as future scholarship on the Hero Wars. Other novels and short story collections explore various periods and regions, though Glorantha has less extensive fiction lines than settings like Forgotten Realms or Dragonlance.

## Cultural Impact

Glorantha demonstrated that non-medieval fantasy settings could succeed commercially, influencing later settings that diverged from European medieval defaults. The bronze age technology level, mythological approach, and anthropological depth showed that fantasy gaming could draw from diverse real-world cultures and time periods while creating immersive, playable worlds.

The cult system influenced how RPGs approach religion mechanically. Rather than treating religion as background flavor or generic "clerics worship gods," Glorantha made specific religious affiliation central to character identity and mechanical capability. This influenced games like Pendragon (which adapted the passion system from Glorantha), 13th Age (icon relationships), and others that make philosophical/religious positions mechanically relevant.

Heroquesting as a mechanic influenced how games handle mythological interaction and reality-altering magic. The concept that myths can be entered and changed, that narrative has mechanical weight, and that culture's stories shape reality appeared in later games' approaches to high-level play, mythic paths, and narrative magic systems.

The setting's anthropological depth demonstrated that fantasy cultures could be more than "humans but with different names." Gloranthan cultures have distinct mythologies, daily practices, social structures, and worldviews that affect how characters from those cultures approach problems. This influenced RPG world-building toward greater cultural specificity and away from monolithic racial cultures.

Ducks became iconic despite (or because of) their apparent absurdity. The juxtaposition of anthropomorphic ducks treated with absolute seriousness within the setting created memorable moments. Ducks appeared in other settings (including D&D via the "Duck race in Ravenloft" article), became convention in-jokes, and demonstrated that fantasy gaming could include whimsical elements without becoming comedic.

The setting's complexity created both devoted fans and accessibility challenges. Glorantha's depth rewards extensive study and long-term campaigns where players learn mythologies and cultural details. However, this same depth makes Glorantha intimidating for newcomers compared to more accessible settings with simpler premises. The tension between depth and accessibility remains ongoing in Gloranthan publication.

Greg Stafford's approach to setting-as-mythology influenced how designers think about world-building. Rather than starting with geography and politics, Stafford began with cosmology and mythology, allowing cultures and histories to emerge from mythological principles. This top-down approach created internal consistency and thematic cohesion that bottom-up world-building sometimes lacks.

## Reception and Legacy

Glorantha has maintained devoted fans throughout its 45+ year history, with the setting considered one of RPG gaming's deepest and most thoroughly developed worlds. Fans celebrate the mythological depth, cultural immersion, sophisticated approach to religion and magic, and the setting's willingness to diverge from fantasy conventions. Long-term Glorantha campaigns can span decades of real-time play, with groups exploring specific clans, cultures, or periods in depth.

The setting's complexity and learning curve create barriers for newcomers. Understanding Glorantha requires learning unfamiliar mythologies, bronze age cultures, and complex divine relationships. Unlike settings where players can rely on medieval European or Tolkien-derived assumptions, Glorantha requires genuine cultural study. This rewards investment but frustrates those seeking immediate accessibility.

The multiple mechanical systems (RuneQuest editions, HeroQuest, 13th Age Glorantha) created fragmentation in the fan community. Debates about which system best serves Glorantha continue, with partisans of each approach claiming their preferred mechanics better express the setting's themes. This mechanical plurality demonstrates Glorantha's setting-mechanical flexibility but creates confusion about which version is "definitive."

The metaplot's Hero Wars created both anticipation and frustration. The approaching prophesied conflicts provide dramatic stakes and allow characters to participate in epoch-defining events. However, the extended timeline to reach the Hero Wars (decades of real-time publication advancing the setting toward prophesied events) sometimes felt like indefinite delay. The eventual publication of Hero Wars materials satisfied some fans while others preferred earlier time periods.

Greg Stafford's passing in 2018 marked the end of an era, though his extensive notes and decades of development ensure the setting's continuation. Jeff Richard as Chaosium's creative director has maintained Stafford's vision while allowing careful expansion, preserving Glorantha's distinctive character while making it accessible to new audiences.

The setting's influence extends beyond direct commercial success. Glorantha pioneered approaches to mythology, religion, and cultural world-building that influenced countless designers. The cult system, heroquesting, rune magic, and anthropological approach to fantasy cultures all became templates that later games studied, adapted, or reacted against. Glorantha's legacy is secure as one of RPG gaming's most important settings, demonstrating that fantasy world-building could be as sophisticated and culturally rich as serious speculative fiction.

The setting's mythology and cosmology don't easily connect to other settings' multiverses. While theoretically heroquesting or powerful magic might allow access to other worlds, Glorantha functions best as a standalone setting where its unique mythological principles operate without interference from external cosmologies or divine systems.
