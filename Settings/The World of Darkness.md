# The World of Darkness

**The World of Darkness** is an urban gothic-punk horror setting created by Mark Rein-Hagen for White Wolf Publishing, launched with Vampire: The Masquerade in 1991. Set in a dark reflection of our contemporary world where supernatural beings—vampires, werewolves, mages, wraiths, changelings, and others—secretly exist among humanity, the setting explores themes of personal horror, moral corruption, and the monster within. Unlike traditional fantasy RPGs where players are heroes fighting evil, the World of Darkness casts players as monsters struggling to maintain humanity while navigating supernatural politics, ancient conspiracies, and their own predatory natures. With its sophisticated metaplot, Storyteller System mechanics emphasizing narrative agency, and gothic-punk aesthetic mixing Victorian horror with 1990s counterculture, the World of Darkness became one of RPG gaming's most commercially successful and culturally influential settings.

## History and Development

The World of Darkness emerged from Mark Rein-Hagen's desire to create a horror RPG that differed from existing games like Call of Cthulhu. Rather than playing humans investigating supernatural threats, players would be the supernatural themselves—specifically vampires, exploring the curse of immortality, moral degradation, and eternal political machinations. Rein-Hagen worked with Tom Dowd, Andrew Greenberg, and others at Lion Rampant (which became White Wolf Publishing) to develop the concept.

"Vampire: The Masquerade" launched in August 1991, immediately distinguishing itself through presentation and concept. The book's trade paperback format (rather than hardcover), black-and-white art emphasizing mood over clarity, and dense text discussing philosophy and horror created a distinctive aesthetic. The game positioned itself as "mature" gaming, emphasizing themes of addiction (blood), loss of humanity, and moral choices over combat and treasure-seeking.

The Storyteller System introduced mechanics supporting narrative play. Rather than detailed simulation, the system used dice pools (multiple d10s) for task resolution, with success thresholds and degrees of success creating flexible outcomes. Character creation emphasized concepts and backstories over optimizing statistics. The system deliberately avoided extensive combat rules, encouraging players to solve problems through social interaction, investigation, and politics rather than violence.

Vampire's commercial success exceeded expectations. The game tapped into goth and alternative culture movements of the early 1990s, appealing to audiences who'd never played RPGs. The aesthetic—mixing Anne Rice's vampire novels with goth clubs, conspiracy theories, and punk attitudes—resonated with players seeking darker, more sophisticated gaming than traditional fantasy provided.

The World of Darkness expanded rapidly. "Werewolf: The Apocalypse" (1992) introduced eco-warrior werewolves fighting corporate corruption and spiritual pollution. "Mage: The Ascension" (1993) explored reality-warping wizards in a secret war for humanity's belief and consensus reality. "Wraith: The Oblivion" (1994) detailed the underworld where dead souls struggled with their dark mirrors. "Changeling: The Dreaming" (1995) presented faeries fighting to maintain wonder in a increasingly banal world.

Each game line shared the World of Darkness setting but presented distinct supernatural perspectives. Vampires navigated eternal political conflicts. Werewolves fought apocalyptic environmental destruction. Mages sought transcendence through enlightenment or power. Wraiths confronted death itself. Changelings battled banality. The lines could crossover—vampires and werewolves were ancient enemies, mages and vampires competed for influence—but each maintained distinct themes and mechanical approaches.

The metaplot became increasingly central and complex. The Jyhad (ancient conflict between vampire antediluvians manipulating modern vampires), the Apocalypse (approaching end-times for werewolves), and the Ascension War (mages' secret conflict) created interconnected storylines advancing through supplements. The "Time of Judgment" series (2003-2004) brought apocalyptic conclusions to each line, ending the "Classic World of Darkness" (later called "Old World of Darkness").

"Vampire: The Requiem" launched in 2004 as the first game in the "New World of Darkness" (later rebranded "Chronicles of Darkness"). This setting rebooted the World of Darkness without the extensive metaplot, allowing DMs more freedom while using refined mechanics. The Chronicles of Darkness avoided interconnected metaplot in favor of modular storytelling, reflecting criticism that the Classic WoD's metaplot had become overwhelming.

White Wolf was acquired by CCP Games in 2006, then by Paradox Interactive in 2015. Onyx Path Publishing, formed by former White Wolf staff, licensed rights to continue publishing WoD material. The publishing history became complex, with different companies holding various rights.

"Vampire: The Masquerade 20th Anniversary Edition" (2011) celebrated the game's legacy and revived the Classic World of Darkness. Subsequent "20th Anniversary" editions for other lines followed. "Vampire: The Masquerade 5th Edition" (2018) updated the game for contemporary play, advancing the timeline post-Judgment and introducing new mechanics while maintaining classic themes.

The setting has expanded into other media. The "Vampire: The Masquerade – Bloodlines" video game (2004) became a cult classic despite technical issues. "Vampire: The Masquerade – Bloodlines 2" is in development. The "World of Darkness" MMO was canceled after years of development. Numerous novels, comics, and other media have explored the setting.

The setting's cultural influence extended beyond gaming. The goth and vampire subcultures of the 1990s-2000s were significantly influenced by Vampire's aesthetics. LARP (live-action roleplay) communities formed around WoD games, with Vampire LARPs becoming particularly prominent. The Mind's Eye Theater system formalized WoD LARP rules, creating one of the largest organized LARP communities worldwide.

## Geography and Cosmology

The World of Darkness is Earth, but darker—a gothic-punk version of our contemporary world where supernatural beings hide among humanity. The setting lacks fantastical geography like other worlds; instead, it transforms familiar locations into sinister reflections. New York, London, Berlin, Tokyo, and other real cities exist but contain secret vampire courts, werewolf territories, and occult conspiracies hidden from mortal awareness.

The Masquerade (in Vampire) or Veil (in other lines) represents the supernatural's concealment from humanity. Vampires must hide their nature or risk the Second Inquisition (modern hunters with government resources). This creates urban environments where gothic nightclubs, abandoned warehouses, sewer systems, and corporate boardrooms all potentially host supernatural activity while appearing mundane to mortals.

Vampire geography centers on domains—cities or regions controlled by vampire princes or archbishops. The Camarilla (vampire sect maintaining the Masquerade) rules through princely domains where the eldest or politically skilled vampire claims absolute authority. The Sabbat (vampire sect rejecting human morality) operates through pack territories. The Anarchs (vampire movement opposing both) create free states where younger vampires resist elders' authority. These political divisions create distinct urban character—Chicago might be Camarilla, Detroit could be Sabbat, Los Angeles could be Anarch.

Werewolf geography focuses on caerns—sacred places of spiritual power that werewolves defend against corruption. The material world (where humans live) overlaps with the Umbra (spirit world), with caerns serving as strong connection points. Werewolf territories are invisible to mortals but crucial to werewolves—polluting a caern spiritually damages it, requiring werewolves to fight corporations, banes (evil spirits), and other threats.

The cosmology varies by game line but generally presents a secret supernatural layer over mundane reality. Vampires trace lineage to Caine (biblical figure cursed by God for murdering Abel), creating vampire theology around punishment, redemption, and eternal curse. Thirteen antediluvians (Caine's grandchildren) supposedly sleep beneath cities, manipulating modern vampires through the Jyhad (conspiracy theory or ancient truth, depending on interpretation).

Werewolves serve Gaia (Earth Mother) against the Wyrm (cosmic force of corruption and entropy). The Triat—Wyld (creation), Weaver (pattern), Wyrm (destruction)—once maintained cosmic balance, but the Wyrm went insane, becoming a destructive force rather than natural entropy. Werewolves fight the Wyrm's manifestations: polluting corporations, evil spirits, and vampires (considered Wyrm-tainted by werewolf cosmology).

Mages perceive consensus reality as constructed—humanity's collective belief determines what's "real." The Ascension War is fought between the Technocracy (enforcing scientific materialism as consensus) and the Traditions (nine magical philosophies seeking diverse reality). Mages can perform "vulgar" magic (violating consensus, like throwing fireballs) but risk Paradox (reality backlash). "Coincidental" magic works within consensus, allowing subtle reality manipulation.

Wraiths exist in the Underworld (Shadowlands and Tempest), a realm of death where souls become wraiths bound by Fetters (connections to life) and haunted by their Shadow (dark reflection embodying worst aspects). The Hierarchy (bureaucratic afterlife government) rules from Stygia, while renegades, heretics, and spectres (corrupted wraiths) create factional conflicts. The Underworld has its own geography—necropoli (dead cities overlapping living cities), the Labyrinth (entropic depths), and domains of the Deathlords.

Changelings exist in both mortal world and Dreaming (realm of stories and imagination). Fae souls reincarnate in human bodies, awakening to fae nature while trapped in mortal flesh. The Dreaming is fading as modernity's banality (mundane reality) destroys wonder and creativity. Changelings fight to preserve imagination while navigating both mortal obligations and fae politics.

The metaplot presents approaching apocalypses for each supernatural type. Gehenna (vampire apocalypse when antediluvians rise) threatens to destroy all vampires. The Apocalypse (werewolf end-times when Wyrm wins) approaches as Gaia weakens. Ascension (mages transcending or failing) determines reality's future. The Time of Judgment products detailed potential apocalyptic outcomes, though post-2011 revival treats Judgment as optional or averted.

## Cultures and Factions

World of Darkness factions are supernaturally complex organizations mixing political philosophy, ancient history, and occult power. These groups provide character identity and campaign conflict.

Vampire clans are bloodlines descended from specific antediluvians, each with distinct powers, weaknesses, and cultural characteristics. The Ventrue are noble-blooded leaders and power-brokers, embracing (creating new vampires from) business executives, politicians, and traditional aristocrats. The Toreador are artistic aesthetes and hedonists, creating beauty while struggling with obsession. The Tremere are blood sorcerers and former mages, organized as a rigid magical hierarchy. The Brujah are rebellious idealists-turned-cynics, ranging from genuine revolutionaries to violent thugs. The Nosferatu are hideously deformed information brokers dwelling in sewers. The Malkavian are insane oracles seeing truths others miss through madness's lens. The Gangrel are bestial wanderers, close to their vampiric Beast. Other clans (Assamite assassins, Setite serpent-cultists, Giovanni necromancers) provide additional options.

The Camarilla is a vampire sect maintaining the Masquerade through aristocratic structure. Princes rule cities as absolute monarchs (theoretically), with primogen councils (clan representatives) advising. The Camarilla presents itself as civilized vampire society protecting vampires from mortal hunters through concealment. However, internal politics, elder manipulation, and the Jyhad's ancient conspiracies make the Camarilla as much prison as protection.

The Sabbat rejects the Masquerade and human morality, embracing vampirism as predatory superiority. Organized in packs resembling violent religious cults, Sabbat vampires wage war against antediluvian control, the Camarilla, and humanity itself. They practice horrific rituals (Vaulderie blood-binding, Creation Rites destroying humanity), genuinely believing they're liberating vampires from ancient masters. The Sabbat provides antagonists or player characters in darker campaigns.

The Anarchs movement opposes both Camarilla and Sabbat, seeking vampire freedom from elder tyranny. Composed primarily of younger vampires (neonates and ancillae), Anarchs create experimental social structures—communes, democracies, gang territories—trying to escape the eternal gerontocracy. Anarch philosophy ranges from genuine egalitarianism to simple "might makes right" violence.

Werewolf tribes are cultural-spiritual organizations, not genetic lineages. The Silver Fangs are nobility and natural leaders, though inbreeding and madness plague them. The Get of Fenris are warriors and berserkers. The Children of Gaia seek peace and healing. The Black Furies are female warriors protecting women. The Bone Gnawers are urban survivors and homeless advocates. Each tribe has distinct totems, cultural practices, and approaches to the Apocalypse. The tribes form the Garou Nation, theoretically united against the Wyrm but fractured by ideological conflicts.

Mage Traditions represent nine philosophical approaches to magic and reality. The Order of Hermes practices formal ceremonial magic and hierarchical organization. The Verbena are nature witches and blood magic practitioners. The Dreamspeakers are shamanic traditions connecting to spirit worlds. The Virtual Adepts are techno-mages using computers and information as magical tools. The Celestial Chorus seeks unity with the divine One. Each Tradition offers distinct magical paradigm and cultural approach.

The Technocracy opposes Traditions, enforcing scientific materialism as consensus reality. Divided into Conventions (Iteration X as cybernetics and machines, New World Order as social control, Progenitors as genetic engineering, Syndicate as economics, Void Engineers as deep space), the Technocracy genuinely believes it protects humanity from chaos by maintaining rational reality. Technocracy agents are antagonists but not necessarily villains—they prevent reality from fragmenting into solipsistic chaos.

Wraith guilds organize based on afterlife skills. The Masquers manipulate appearance and identity. The Solicitors serve as lawyers and administrators. The Pardoners offer spiritual counseling. The Hierarchy's Legions (military structure) enforce order. Heretics oppose Hierarchy dogma, while renegades operate outside structure entirely.

Changeling kiths represent fairy types—sidhe are noble fae, trolls are honor-bound giants, pooka are shapeshifting tricksters, satyrs are hedonistic revelers. Houses (noble families) like Liam, Ailil, and Balor engage in political machinations. The Seelie Code emphasizes honor and beauty, while Unseelie Court embraces darker passions. Courts aren't good and evil but different philosophical approaches to fae nature.

## Distinctive Elements

The World of Darkness's most distinctive element is playing monsters rather than heroes. Characters are predators, cursed beings, or corrupted souls navigating supernatural politics while struggling to maintain humanity or succumb to their monstrous nature. This inverts traditional RPG morality—there are no clear heroes, evil is within as much as external, and "winning" might mean merely delaying inevitable corruption.

Personal horror themes distinguish WoD from external-threat horror. Vampire characters fear not just enemies but the Beast—their internal predatory nature that grows stronger as Humanity degrades. Choosing to feed violently, manipulate mortals as pawns, or embrace political ruthlessness provides short-term advantages but erodes Humanity, eventually transforming characters into NPCs controlled by the Beast. This creates mechanical incentive toward monstrosity while narratively exploring moral degradation.

The Humanity system (and equivalent traits in other lines—Gnosis for werewolves, Glamour for changelings) mechanically represents the character's connection to their original nature. Acts violating morality risk Humanity loss through rolls. Low Humanity makes characters increasingly monstrous—frenzy triggers more easily, conscience fades, eventually leading to NPC status. This makes moral choices mechanically significant, creating genuine tension between expedient monstrousness and difficult morality.

The metaplot's complexity created an interconnected narrative across game lines. The Ascension War influenced vampire blood magic (Tremere were mages who achieved vampirism). Werewolves and vampires were ancient enemies (werewolves consider vampires Wyrm-tainted). Wraiths observed living vampires, potentially haunting them. This created a cohesive world where different supernatural types interacted within shared history and cosmology.

The Gothic-Punk aesthetic mixed Victorian gothic horror (vampire lords, haunted manors, eternal curses) with 1990s counterculture (nightclubs, punk attitudes, anti-establishment politics). This created distinctive visual and thematic identity—vampires in leather jackets instead of capes, werewolves fighting pollution instead of peasants, modern cities as gothic landscapes. The aesthetic influenced goth culture broadly, with WoD imagery becoming iconic in alternative music and fashion.

The Storyteller System emphasized narrative over simulation. Rather than detailed rules for every situation, the system provided flexible resolution mechanics and encouraged Storytellers (GMs) to prioritize story and character over mechanical optimization. This "rules-light narrative-heavy" approach influenced later story-focused RPGs like FATE, Powered by the Apocalypse, and others prioritizing fiction.

Secret histories rewrote real history with supernatural conspiracies. The Technocracy assassinated Kennedy. Vampire elders manipulated European politics. Werewolves caused the Black Death fighting Wyrm corruption. This made real history potentially supernatural, allowing campaigns where historical events were secretly supernatural conflicts.

The LARP potential was unusually strong. The urban contemporary setting, emphasis on social interaction over combat, and political intrigue all supported live-action play better than dungeon-crawling fantasy. Mind's Eye Theater formalized LARP rules, creating massive organized LARP communities. Some players only experienced WoD through LARP, never playing tabletop.

## Mechanical Integration

The Storyteller System uses dice pools of d10s, with successes counted (typically 7+ on d10). Character attributes (Strength, Dexterity, etc.) and abilities (skills) combine to form dice pools—a character with Strength 3 and Brawl 2 rolls 5d10 for unarmed combat. Difficulty numbers (how high each die must roll) vary based on task complexity. "1"s rolled subtract successes, potentially creating botches (catastrophic failures).

Character creation uses point-buy across categories: attributes, abilities, advantages (backgrounds, disciplines/gifts/spheres, etc.). Priority dots distribute between physical, social, and mental categories, forcing choices about character focus. This created flexibility but also encouraged optimization gaming ("min-maxing").

Disciplines (vampire powers), Gifts (werewolf powers), Spheres (mage powers), and equivalent systems provided supernatural abilities. Disciplines like Celerity (supernatural speed), Dominate (mental control), and Obfuscate (invisibility) cost blood points to activate. Gifts required Gnosis expenditure. Spheres allowed reality manipulation with Paradox risk. These powers scaled, with elder vampires or powerful mages achieving tremendous capabilities.

The Humanity/Path system tracked moral degradation. Characters started with Humanity ratings (typically 7-8). Acts violating Humanity's hierarchy (killing for convenience, torture, serial killing, etc.) required rolls. Failure meant Humanity loss. Low Humanity triggered easier frenzy, reduced empathy, and eventual NPC status. Alternatively, vampires could follow Paths (alternative ethical systems like Path of Blood or Path of Caine) replacing Humanity with inhuman values.

Combat was deadly compared to heroic fantasy. Characters didn't have massive hit point pools; instead, damage degraded Health levels, with enough damage causing incapacitation or death. This encouraged avoiding combat or ending fights quickly through overwhelming force. Social combat and influence systems provided alternatives, with politics as dangerous as violence.

Backgrounds represented resources and connections. Resources determined wealth, Contacts provided information, Allies offered assistance, Herd represented feeding sources. These backgrounds made characters feel embedded in the world rather than isolated adventurers.

Later editions refined mechanics. "Vampire: The Masquerade 20th Anniversary Edition" (2011) consolidated mechanics from various supplements while maintaining core system. "Vampire 5th Edition" (2018) introduced Hunger dice (special dice representing vampiric thirst) and Convictions (beliefs guiding morality), updating mechanics while preserving themes.

Chronicles of Darkness (New WoD) modified the Storyteller System into "Storytelling System," using similar d10 dice pools but refined mechanics. The "God-Machine Chronicle" (later basis for 2nd Edition Chronicles) introduced Conditions (persistent effects from situations), Beats (minor experience for complications), and other narrative mechanics influencing later RPG design.

## Published Material

The sheer volume of World of Darkness material exceeds most settings. Each game line received core rulebooks, player guides, setting books, and metaplot supplements. The publishing schedule from 1991-2004 saw monthly releases across multiple lines.

Vampire: The Masquerade produced core rulebooks (1st through Revised Editions), "Clanbooks" (one per clan, detailing history and powers), "Guides to the Camarilla/Sabbat/Anarchs," city sourcebooks (Chicago, New York, Berlin, etc.), and metaplot books ("Gehenna," "Time of Judgment"). Over 70 V:tM supplements were published.

Werewolf: The Apocalypse generated "Tribebooks," "Breed Books," setting guides, and apocalypse metaplot materials. Mage: The Ascension produced "Tradition Books," "Convention Books," paradigm explorations, and Ascension War materials. Each game line's publication similarly extensive.

Crossover books detailed interactions between supernatural types. "World of Darkness: Combat," "World of Darkness: Sorcerer," and others provided shared rules. "Dirty Secrets of the Black Hand" explored vampire-werewolf conflicts.

Mind's Eye Theater provided LARP rules for each game line, with "Laws of the Night" (Vampire LARP) becoming particularly popular. These created formal organized LARP communities.

Novels numbered in the hundreds across all lines. The "Clan Novel Saga" (thirteen interconnected vampire novels) and "Tribe Novels" explored characters and metaplot. Quality varied, but successful novels like "Dark Ages: Nosferatu" by Gherbod Fleming attracted readers beyond gamers.

Comics from Moonstone and other publishers adapted game lines. The "Vampire: The Masquerade" comic series explored characters and metaplot.

The Chronicles of Darkness (New WoD) produced parallel publication. "Vampire: The Requiem," "Werewolf: The Forsaken," "Mage: The Awakening," and other lines received comparable support, though without interconnected metaplot.

The 20th Anniversary Editions (V20, W20, M20, etc.) compiled decades of material into comprehensive editions. These massive books (V20 is 500+ pages) included core rules, advanced powers, metaplot summaries, and extensive options.

"Vampire: The Masquerade 5th Edition" (2018-present) continues publication with updated mechanics and setting advancement. Supplements explore modern nights, Anarch uprising, and Second Inquisition.

## Cultural Impact

The World of Darkness became one of RPG gaming's most commercially successful settings, at times outselling Dungeons & Dragons and introducing mainstream audiences to RPGs. The setting's appeal beyond traditional gamers—particularly to goth and alternative culture communities—expanded the RPG market significantly.

The gothic-punk aesthetic influenced 1990s-2000s goth culture, fashion, and music. Vampire imagery from WoD appeared in clubs, fashion magazines, and alternative media. The setting's sophisticated approach to horror and moral complexity demonstrated that RPGs could address mature themes, appealing to adult audiences seeking more than dungeon crawls.

LARP communities formed around WoD games became cultural phenomena. Vampire LARPs in particular attracted hundreds of participants in major cities, creating visible subcultures. The media occasionally covered LARP communities, sometimes positively but often sensationalizing the "people who think they're vampires" angle, creating both publicity and stigma.

The setting's emphasis on personal horror, moral degradation, and playing monsters influenced later dark fantasy and horror games. The focus on internal conflict (the Beast, frenzy, Humanity loss) rather than external threats created template for horror gaming about being the monster. Games like "My Life with Master," "Monsterhearts," and others exploring monstrous protagonists owe debts to WoD's pioneering approach.

The metaplot's extensiveness created both devoted fans and criticism. Fans appreciated the living world advancing through publications, with canonical events providing shared experience and community discussion. Critics argued metaplot constrained creativity, forced unwanted changes on campaigns, and prioritized continuity over playability. This metaplot debate influenced how subsequent settings approached canonical events.

The Storyteller System's narrative focus influenced RPG design broadly. While mechanically the system had issues (dice pools could be unwieldy, success counting tedious), the philosophy of prioritizing story over simulation influenced later narrative-focused systems. The concept of "Storyteller" rather than "Game Master" or "Dungeon Master" emphasized the GM's role as collaborative narrator rather than referee.

Video games like "Vampire: The Masquerade – Bloodlines" (2004) introduced hundreds of thousands to the setting. Despite buggy release, the game became cult classic for its atmosphere, storytelling, and capturing VTM's themes. The upcoming "Bloodlines 2" demonstrates ongoing commercial interest.

The setting's treatment of vampire mythology influenced popular culture's vampire depictions. The emphasis on political intrigue, eternal ennui, and tragic curse rather than simple predation appeared in subsequent vampire fiction. Some attribute the 2000s vampire romance boom (Twilight, etc.) partly to WoD popularizing sympathetic vampire protagonists, though this connection is debated.

## Reception and Legacy

The World of Darkness received both commercial success and critical examination. Fans celebrated the mature themes, sophisticated setting, personal horror mechanics, and rich metaplot. The setting provided something genuinely different from heroic fantasy or Lovecraftian horror, creating space for exploration of moral complexity and dark themes.

Critics raised concerns about the setting's treatment of violence, moral degradation, and transgressive content. The focus on playing predators potentially romanticizing harmful behavior generated controversy. Some communities had negative experiences with players using "it's what my character would do" to justify disruptive or disturbing behavior, creating the "that guy playing Malkavian/Sabbat" problem.

The metaplot's complexity became both feature and bug. Long-term fans appreciated the interconnected narrative and advancing timeline. New players found the extensive backstory intimidating, requiring significant reading to understand references. The Time of Judgment's apocalyptic endings disappointed some fans invested in continued campaigns, though others appreciated definitive closure.

The mechanics received mixed response. The Storyteller System's flexibility enabled narrative focus but sometimes felt arbitrary. Dice pool mechanics created table clutter, and success counting slowed play. The system's emphasis on narrative over simulation was ahead of its time but not fully realized mechanically, requiring Storytellers to fill gaps with rulings.

The 5th Edition updates have generated debate. Changes to clan powers, Humanity mechanics, and setting advancement pleased some (modernizing dated material) while upsetting others (losing familiar elements). The incorporation of modern politics and social issues created controversy, with some players appreciating relevance while others preferred escapist fantasy.

The Chronicles of Darkness (New WoD) had limited commercial success compared to Classic WoD. While mechanically refined and allowing greater DM freedom, Chronicles lacked Classic's cultural cachet and devoted fanbase. The 20th Anniversary Editions' success demonstrated that Classic WoD retained stronger brand identity and player investment.

The setting's legacy is secure as one of RPG gaming's most important settings. It demonstrated that RPGs could succeed with mature themes, that horror gaming could focus on internal rather than external threats, and that settings could support extensive metaplot. The influence on goth culture, LARP communities, and horror gaming broadly ensures WoD's cultural relevance extends beyond tabletop gaming.
