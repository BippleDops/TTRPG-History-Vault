# Greyhawk

The **World of Greyhawk**, often simply called **Greyhawk**, is the original campaign setting for Dungeons & Dragons, created by Gary Gygax in the early 1970s. Set on the planet Oerth, with primary focus on the continent of Oerik and the region known as the Flanaess, Greyhawk represents the foundation upon which much of D&D's design philosophy and world-building conventions were established. From its humble origins as Gygax's home campaign to its publication as the first official D&D setting, Greyhawk has maintained a devoted following for nearly five decades, influencing countless fantasy worlds that followed.

## History and Development

Greyhawk began in 1972 as Gary Gygax's personal campaign world, originally called "Castle Greyhawk and Vicinity." The setting developed organically through play, with Gygax creating material as needed for his players, who included Rob Kuntz, Terry Kuntz, Ernie Gygax, and others. The legendary Castle Greyhawk dungeon, built beneath the ruins in the Cairn Hills near the Free City of Greyhawk, became one of gaming's most famous megadungeons, though its original form was never officially published.

Throughout the mid-1970s, Greyhawk existed primarily as Gygax's home game and was referenced obliquely in D&D products. Many of the spells in the Player's Handbook bore names from Greyhawk NPCs—Mordenkainen, Bigby, Tenser, Otto—immortalizing Gygax's players and their characters. Similarly, artifacts like the Tome of Strahd and the Rod of Seven Parts, and monsters like the beholder and mind flayer, had their origins in Greyhawk play sessions.

The first commercial Greyhawk product, the "World of Greyhawk Fantasy Game Setting," was published in 1980 as a folio edition. This groundbreaking boxed set included a large full-color map of the Flanaess, a 32-page gazetteer, and details on gods, politics, and geography. It established the Common Year (CY) dating system, setting the "present day" as 576 CY. The folio was revolutionary, showing gamers that a campaign setting could be more than just a collection of dungeons—it could be a living, breathing world with history, politics, and interconnected storylines.

In 1983, TSR released the expanded "World of Greyhawk Fantasy Game Setting" boxed set, often called the "Gold Box" edition. This version pushed the timeline forward to 576 CY and greatly expanded the setting details, including more information on the Flanaess's nations, deities, and power groups. The glossography provided brief descriptions of every major nation and city, while maintaining the "sandbox" approach that allowed DMs maximum creative freedom.

The setting evolved significantly with the "From the Ashes" boxed set in 1992, which advanced the timeline to 585 CY following the cataclysmic Greyhawk Wars (detailed in a 1991 hardcover). This controversial product fundamentally changed the political landscape of the Flanaess, destroying some nations and creating new power dynamics. Iuz the Evil, a demigod and one of Gygax's original campaign villains, conquered much of the northern Flanaess, while the Scarlet Brotherhood emerged from centuries of isolation to threaten southern lands.

When Wizards of the Coast acquired TSR in 1997, Greyhawk became the default setting for D&D 3rd Edition (2000-2008). The Living Greyhawk organized play campaign, which ran from 2000 to 2008, became the most expansive organized play campaign in RPG history. It featured regional play where players' home locations determined which Flanaess regions they could adventure in, generating thousands of adventures and advancing the timeline to 598 CY by the campaign's conclusion.

Greyhawk has received less official support in recent editions, with the Forgotten Realms becoming D&D's default setting for 4th and 5th Editions. However, it remains beloved by old-school gamers and continues to influence D&D's core assumptions, with many iconic elements—from the City of Greyhawk itself to the Gygaxian deities—still present in the game's core materials.

## Geography and Cosmology

The World of Greyhawk is set on the planet Oerth, which orbits the sun Solnor. The primary focus is the continent of Oerik, specifically the eastern portion known as the Flanaess, so named because it lies east of the Crystalmist and Barrier Peaks mountain ranges. The Flanaess stretches from the Baklunish Basin in the northwest to the Tilvanot Peninsula in the south, and from the Solnor Ocean in the east to the impassable mountain ranges in the west.

The Flanaess contains dozens of nations, city-states, and wilderness regions, each with distinct cultures and political alignments. Major power centers include the Great Kingdom of Aerdy (once the dominant empire, now fragmented and decadent), the Theocracy of the Pale, the Shield Lands, the Duchy of Tenh, the County of Urnst, the Free City of Greyhawk, the Kingdom of Furyondy, the Yeomanry, the Hold of the Sea Princes, and many others. The diversity of nations allows DMs to run campaigns with vastly different flavors—from high chivalry in Furyondy to mercantile intrigue in Greyhawk to desperate survival in Iuz-occupied territories.

The Free City of Greyhawk itself serves as the setting's iconic urban center. Located on the Selintan River between the Nyr Dyv (a vast inland sea) and the Wild Coast, Greyhawk is a cosmopolitan trading hub ruled by an oligarchy of guild masters and nobles. The city contains numerous districts, from the wealthy High Quarter to the notorious slums of the River Quarter, and serves as a base for adventurers exploring nearby ruins, including the legendary Castle Greyhawk. The city's Directed Council, guided by its Lord Mayor (historically Nerof Gasgal), manages the complex politics of maintaining neutrality amid surrounding conflicts.

Greyhawk's cosmology follows the Great Wheel model that became standard for AD&D, with the Prime Material Plane surrounded by the Inner Planes (Elemental Planes of Air, Earth, Fire, and Water, plus Positive and Negative Energy) and Outer Planes (the seventeen alignment-based planes, from Lawful Good Mount Celestia to Chaotic Evil the Abyss). The deities of Greyhawk, collectively called the Oerth pantheon, reside on various Outer Planes according to their alignments.

Notable geographic features include the Bright Desert (home to ancient Suloise ruins), the Sea of Dust (the devastated remains of the Suel Imperium), the Hellfurnaces (volcanic mountains), the Abbor-Alz (mysterious badlands), the Barrier Peaks (where Expedition to the Barrier Peaks introduced a crashed spaceship into fantasy gaming), and the Vast Swamp (lair of numerous monsters). The Underdark equivalent, called the Underoerth or Deepoerth, contains drow cities, duergar strongholds, and stranger things.

The setting's history stretches back millennia, through great empires and catastrophes. The ancient Suel Imperium and Baklunish Empire destroyed each other in mutual magical annihilation (the Invoked Devastation and Rain of Colorless Fire), sending Suloise refugees fleeing eastward over the mountains into the Flanaess. The Great Kingdom of Aerdy rose to dominance, then declined into decadence and evil. The legendary hero Vecna achieved lichdom and eventually godhood, though not before his lieutenant Kas betrayed him, leaving only the Hand and Eye of Vecna as artifacts of terrible power.

## Cultures and Factions

Greyhawk features exceptional cultural and ethnic diversity, reflecting the setting's development through actual play. The major human ethnic groups—Baklunish, Oeridian, Suel, and Flan—each have distinct characteristics, histories, and cultural traits, with most nations containing mixed populations from these groups.

The Flannae are the oldest human inhabitants of the Flanaess, with bronze skin and dark hair. Once dominant, they were displaced by successive waves of migration but maintain strong cultural identities in regions like the Rovers of the Barrens and the Duchy of Tenh. Flan culture emphasizes druidic traditions, earth magic, and reverence for ancient spirits.

The Suel people, descendants of the destroyed Suel Imperium, have pale skin, blonde or white hair, and often striking blue or violet eyes. The Scarlet Brotherhood represents Suel supremacists who advocate returning to ancient Suel purity, while the barbarian tribes of the Thillonrian Peninsula (Fruztii, Schnai, and Cruski) maintain Suel heritage without the Brotherhood's sinister ideology. Suel culture values personal achievement, magical innovation, and often, racial hierarchy.

The Oeridians, with light brown to olive skin and varying hair colors, came from the western lands and established the Great Kingdom of Aerdy. Oeridian culture emphasizes military prowess, feudal hierarchy, and centralized authority. Nations like Furyondy and Nyrond maintain Oeridian cultural traditions, though they've diverged from the decadent Great Kingdom.

The Baklunish people, with golden-bronze skin and various hair colors, inhabit the western Baklunish Basin and regions of the Flanaess. Baklunish culture draws inspiration from Middle Eastern and Central Asian traditions, emphasizing scholarship, elemental magic (particularly fire and air), and complex social codes. The Baklunish states include the Caliphate of Ekbir, the Sultanate of Zeif, and various Paynims tribes.

Beyond ethnic divisions, Greyhawk features numerous powerful factions that drive plots and conflicts. The Circle of Eight, originally formed by the archmage Mordenkainen, comprises powerful wizards dedicated to maintaining balance in the Flanaess—sometimes through direct intervention. Members have included Bigby, Otiluke, Tenser, Rary, Drawmij, Nystul, and others, though betrayals and deaths have changed the roster over time.

The Scarlet Brotherhood operates as a secretive network of assassins, monks, and infiltrators dedicated to Suel supremacy. Based on the Tilvanot Peninsula, the Brotherhood maintains cells throughout the Flanaess, manipulating politics and eliminating opponents. Their emergence in the Greyhawk Wars shocked the other nations, as centuries of isolation had led most to forget the Brotherhood existed.

The Old Faith represents the druidic hierarchy that maintains nature's balance, particularly important to Flan cultures. Led by the Grand Druid, the Faith operates across political boundaries, concerning itself with ecological disasters and supernatural threats to the natural world. The hierophant druid is often a powerful NPC who can provide guidance or missions to player characters.

The Knights of Holy Shielding (later the Knights of the Watch) serve as paladins and champions of good-aligned deities, particularly in fighting the forces of Iuz, Vecna, and other evil powers. The Horned Society, conversely, served as a nation of evil humanoids and sinister humans before Iuz absorbed it during the Greyhawk Wars.

## Distinctive Elements

Greyhawk's most distinctive characteristic is its deliberate incompleteness—what later designers called its "sandbox" approach. Gary Gygax provided broad strokes for the setting, establishing politics, major NPCs, and general geography, but intentionally left vast areas undefined or only briefly described. This allowed individual DMs to develop regions according to their own campaigns' needs without contradicting established canon. The approach contrasted sharply with later, more detailed settings like the Forgotten Realms.

The setting's moral ambiguity distinguishes it from more heroic fantasy worlds. Nations aren't simply good or evil; they're complex political entities with realistic motivations. Furyondy might be generally good-aligned, but it has its corrupt nobles and political prisoners. The Great Kingdom's descent from noble empire to decadent tyranny illustrates how power corrupts. Even adventurers themselves exist in a morally gray area—they're essentially treasure hunters who loot ancient tombs and ruins, accumulating wealth through violence.

Greyhawk pioneered the concept of historical depth in fantasy gaming. The setting has thousands of years of history, with lost empires, legendary heroes, and ancient mysteries. The Suel-Baklunish Wars, the rise and fall of Vecna, the migrations of peoples, and the expansion and decline of the Great Kingdom provide context for current events. Ruins scattered across the Flanaess represent tangible connections to this history—adventurers exploring ancient Suel crypts or lost Flan temples directly interact with the setting's past.

The self-referential nature of Greyhawk spells and magic items created a unique relationship between game mechanics and setting. When players cast "Mordenkainen's Faithful Hound" or use a "Heward's Handy Haversack," they reference actual in-setting characters. This approach made the setting feel lived-in and connected to the game's mechanical elements in ways that purely invented names couldn't match. Players knew these were spells developed by previous adventurers, perhaps even other player characters from Gygax's original campaign.

The setting's deities form a robust pantheon that became standard for D&D. Rather than having one or two major gods, Greyhawk features dozens of deities representing various portfolios, alignments, and cultural groups. The Suel pantheon (including Lendor, Wee Jas, Kord, Pholtus, and others), the Oeridian pantheon (Heironeous, Hextor, St. Cuthbert, Procan, and more), the Flan pantheon (Beory, Pelor, Nerull, Obad-Hai), and the Baklunish pantheon (Al'Akbar, Istus, Geshtai, Zuoken) each serve their cultural groups while being accessible to anyone. Non-human pantheons include the elven Seldarine, the dwarven Mordinsamman, and others.

The emphasis on dungeons and ruins as adventure sites reflects Greyhawk's origins in the earliest days of D&D. While later settings often focused on wilderness exploration, planar travel, or political intrigue, Greyhawk maintains that classic "there's a dungeon, go explore it" approach. Castle Greyhawk, the Temple of Elemental Evil, White Plume Mountain, the Tomb of Horrors, and countless other sites exist primarily as places to adventure, with world-building serving the game rather than overwhelming it.

## Mechanical Integration

Greyhawk shaped AD&D's mechanical assumptions because it was the setting Gary Gygax had in mind while writing the core rules. The alignment system, cleric turning undead, the spell lists, magic item creation rules, and dungeon ecology all reflect Greyhawk's medieval-fantasy-with-dungeons approach. When AD&D describes a "typical" campaign, it's describing Greyhawk, even if not explicitly named.

The setting introduced several mechanical innovations that later became standard. The "From the Ashes" boxed set included rules for domain management and warfare that expanded on the AD&D Dungeon Master's Guide's basic guidance. The Living Greyhawk campaign developed extensive rules for regional character creation, with characters from different Flanaess regions gaining mechanical benefits reflecting their homelands.

Greyhawk's deity system established the framework for cleric mechanics in D&D. Clerics choose deities with specific alignments, domains, weapons, and spheres of influence, gaining powers reflecting their patron's portfolio. This moved beyond the generic "cleric" of early D&D to create meaningful mechanical differentiation. A cleric of St. Cuthbert (lawful neutral god of wisdom and retribution) plays differently from a cleric of Trithereon (chaotic good god of liberty) or Nerull (neutral evil god of death).

The setting's magic items often have specific origins and histories. The artifacts described in the AD&D Dungeon Master's Guide—the Crook of Rao, the Invulnerable Coat of Arnd, the Heward's Mystical Organ—are Greyhawk items with connections to the setting's history and NPCs. Campaign-specific magic items include Iuz's various artifacts of evil, Vecna's hand and eye, and items created by famous wizards of the Circle of Eight.

For D&D 3rd Edition, Greyhawk served as the default setting, and the core rules assumed a Greyhawk-like world. The deities in the Player's Handbook were Greyhawk gods. The spells remained named after Greyhawk personalities. The example organizations in the Dungeon Master's Guide drew from Greyhawk factions. This mechanical integration meant that any D&D 3E campaign was implicitly Greyhawk-compatible unless specifically stated otherwise.

## Published Material

Greyhawk has generated an enormous body of published material across multiple decades, though publication has been sporadic. The original folio (1980) and boxed set (1983) established the foundation. The From the Ashes boxed set (1992) and Greyhawk Wars (1991) significantly advanced the timeline and setting details. The Player's Guide to Greyhawk (1998) provided a final 2nd Edition overview of the setting.

Numerous adventure modules are explicitly set in Greyhawk, forming a canonical adventure path through the setting. Early classics include T1 The Village of Hommlet, T1-4 The Temple of Elemental Evil, S1 Tomb of Horrors, S2 White Plume Mountain, S3 Expedition to the Barrier Peaks, S4 The Lost Caverns of Tsojcanth, G1-3 Against the Giants, D1-3 Descent into the Depths of the Earth, and Q1 Queen of the Demonweb Pits. These adventures established iconic locations and villains—the Temple of Elemental Evil, Acererak the demilich, the giant chiefs, and Lolth herself.

Later adventure series continued Greyhawk's story. The Slavers series (A1-4) dealt with slave-taking humanoids. The Scourge of the Slavelords supermodule combined these. The City of Greyhawk boxed set (1989) detailed the Free City itself, while Greyhawk: The Adventure Begins (1998) provided a 2nd Edition update. The Adventure Path format used extensively in 3E often featured Greyhawk locations, with Adventure Paths like "Age of Worms" and "Savage Tide" set in or near the Flanaess.

During the Living Greyhawk campaign (2000-2008), thousands of adventures were written for organized play. While most were convention-only play, they expanded Greyhawk lore substantially. Regional triads (three-person volunteer teams) developed their assigned regions extensively, creating a depth of material that dwarfed any previous setting. When Living Greyhawk concluded, many regions published their adventures online, creating an enormous fan resource.

Dragon Magazine and Dungeon Magazine published substantial Greyhawk material over the years. The "From the Ashes" series of articles updated regions post-Greyhawk Wars. "The Adventure Path" articles in Dungeon often used Greyhawk as their default setting. Gary Gygax's "Gord the Rogue" series of novels, while not game products, explored Greyhawk through fiction, particularly the multiverse-spanning conflict involving Tharizdun and other dark powers.

Third-party publishers have released unofficial Greyhawk material. Troll Lord Castles & Crusades products sometimes reference Greyhawk-compatible content. The fan community maintains extensive online resources, including detailed timelines, regional gazetteers, and conversion guides for newer editions. Canonfire.com serves as a central hub for Greyhawk fans, featuring articles, adventures, and ongoing community development of the setting.

## Cultural Impact

Greyhawk's influence on tabletop roleplaying extends far beyond its direct publication history. As the first D&D campaign setting, it established conventions that became industry standards: fantasy settings should have maps, multiple nations with different cultures, a pantheon of gods, defined history spanning millennia, and adventure sites integrated into the geography. Every fantasy RPG setting since, from the Forgotten Realms to Golarion to Eberron, follows patterns Greyhawk pioneered.

The concept of the "adventurer" as a profession—people who explore dungeons for treasure and glory—originates with Greyhawk. The Free City of Greyhawk explicitly contains an economy built around adventuring, with guilds, equipment shops, temples offering healing, and establishments catering to adventurers. This became the default assumption for fantasy gaming, where professional adventuring makes social and economic sense rather than being seen as grave robbing or criminal behavior.

Greyhawk's spell names created a unique linguistic convention in fantasy gaming. Saying "I cast Tenser's Floating Disc" or "Do you have Mordenkainen's Magnificent Mansion prepared?" references Greyhawk whether players realize it or not. This connected game mechanics to narrative in ways that felt organic—these were actual spells developed by actual (fictional) wizards. Later settings adopted this convention, with the Forgotten Realms renaming some spells to Faerûnian wizards (Elminster's Evasion, Aganazzar's Scorcher) while keeping many Greyhawk originals.

The Living Greyhawk organized play campaign pioneered aspects of organized play that influenced later campaigns. The concept of regional play, where players could only adventure in regions corresponding to their real-world location, created invested local communities. The three-tier adventure structure (local, regional, core) balanced local control with campaign-wide consistency. While subsequent organized play campaigns (Living Forgotten Realms, Pathfinder Society, Adventurers League) didn't adopt all of Living Greyhawk's innovations, they learned from its successes and challenges.

Greyhawk's influence appears in unexpected places. The computer RPG Temple of Elemental Evil (2003) directly adapted the classic adventure module. The Slavers series was adapted for 3.5 edition. The Tomb of Horrors has been republished, updated, and referenced in multiple editions, becoming perhaps D&D's most famous death-trap dungeon. Acererak appears as a recurring villain even outside Greyhawk, most notably in Tomb of Annihilation (5E).

The setting's aesthetic—European medieval fantasy with dungeon-delving as the primary activity—defined "default D&D" for decades. When people imagine D&D, they often unconsciously imagine something Greyhawk-like: adventurers based in a Free City, exploring nearby ruins, fighting humanoid tribes and evil wizards, seeking ancient treasures, and gradually becoming involved in regional politics. This aesthetic proved so durable that even when later settings tried to differentiate themselves, they often defined themselves in relation to the Greyhawk baseline.

## Reception and Legacy

Greyhawk has maintained devoted fans throughout its history, though its cultural position has shifted over time. In the 1980s, it was the default D&D setting, with modules and supplements assuming its use. The 1990s saw it overshadowed by newer, more detailed settings like the Forgotten Realms and Planescape, which offered more published support and more defined metaplots. The 2000s brought renewed prominence through Living Greyhawk and 3rd Edition's adoption of it as the default setting. Since 4th Edition, Greyhawk has occupied an honored but inactive status—respected as D&D's original setting but receiving minimal new official support.

Many fans appreciate Greyhawk precisely because of its "sandbox" approach. The deliberate incompleteness that some critics saw as a weakness allows DMs maximum creative freedom. Unlike settings with extensive metaplots and detailed timelines, Greyhawk provides a framework without overwhelming detail. DMs can develop regions, create NPCs, and advance the timeline without contradicting mountains of established canon. This approach aligned with the Old School Renaissance movement's values, making Greyhawk popular with OSR enthusiasts.

Critics have noted that Greyhawk can feel dated or generic. Its medieval European baseline, while standard for 1970s fantasy, lacks the distinctive hooks of later settings. Why adventure in Greyhawk when Eberron offers pulp noir magic-technology, or Planescape offers multiverse philosophy, or Dark Sun offers brutal post-apocalyptic survival? Defenders counter that this "genericness" is actually flexibility—Greyhawk accommodates many campaign styles without forcing a particular approach.

The setting's political complexity appeals to DMs who enjoy intricate faction play and diplomatic campaigns. The dozens of nations, each with realistic motivations and conflicts, support campaigns where politics matter. The Greyhawk Wars demonstrated how interconnected the Flanaess's nations were, with actions in one region cascading across the continent. Living Greyhawk exploited this complexity, with regional storylines affecting each other through core adventures that advanced the metaplot.

Greyhawk's treatment has sometimes frustrated fans. The lack of new official material since 3rd Edition ended feels like abandonment to devoted followers. The controversial decisions during the Greyhawk Wars—killing beloved NPCs, radically altering the political landscape, having Rary betray the Circle of Eight—alienated some fans. The shift to the Forgotten Realms as D&D's default setting for 4th and 5th Editions relegated Greyhawk to secondary status, mentioned respectfully but not actively developed.

Despite limited recent support, Greyhawk maintains cultural relevance. The 5th Edition Dungeon Master's Guide includes Greyhawk among its example settings. Ghosts of Saltmarsh (2019) adapted classic nautical adventures, many originally set in Greyhawk. The setting's deities appear in 5E materials, and the spell names remain unchanged. Gygax's original creation continues influencing D&D at a foundational level, even as newer settings claim the spotlight.
