# Action Economy

## Overview

Action economy represents the systematic limitation and categorization of what characters can accomplish during their turn in combat or other time-structured scenes. At its most basic, action economy asks "What can you do on your turn?" and "What are the opportunity costs of those choices?" These seemingly simple questions create some of the most important tactical and balance considerations in combat-focused TTRPGs.

The term "action economy" emerged from optimization-focused D&D communities but describes a design element present in nearly all games with structured turns. The core insight is that actions are a resource: characters have a limited budget of actions each turn, and choosing how to spend that budget creates meaningful tactical decisions. A character who can make three attacks per turn has better "action economy" than one who can make only one, all else being equal. A spell that incapacitates multiple enemies damages the opponents' action economy by preventing them from acting effectively.

Action economy systems vary dramatically in complexity and philosophy. Some games feature simple structures like "one significant action per turn" while others create elaborate taxonomies of action types (standard, move, minor, free, swift, immediate, reaction) with detailed rules about what falls into each category and how they can be combined. These choices profoundly affect gameplay, from the speed of combat resolution to the depth of tactical decision-making to the balance between different character types.

Modern action economy design recognizes that the system must balance several competing concerns: providing meaningful tactical choices without creating analysis paralysis, allowing character differentiation without permitting overwhelming action advantages, maintaining reasonable turn length, and creating intuitive categories that players can easily understand. The evolution of action economy across game editions and systems reveals ongoing attempts to find optimal balance points among these sometimes-conflicting goals.

The action economy framework also extends beyond individual turns to shape overall combat dynamics. Effects that grant extra actions, deny opponents' actions, or allow acting outside normal turn order all represent forms of action economy manipulation that can dramatically shift combat effectiveness. Understanding action economy as a resource helps both game designers create balanced systems and players make effective tactical decisions.

## Historical Development

The history of action economy in TTRPGs reflects evolving understanding of combat structure, game balance, and tactical depth. Early designs inherited assumptions from miniature wargaming without fully considering how those structures would function in roleplaying contexts, while later designs deliberately engineered action economies to create specific gameplay experiences.

**Chainmail (1971)** and **Original D&D (1974)** featured rudimentary action structures inherited from wargaming: units could move and attack during their phase, with some special actions (spellcasting, charging) replacing or modifying the standard action set. The concept of "segments" in AD&D attempted to create simultaneous resolution with speed-based initiative, but in practice most tables resolved turns sequentially with characters taking "their action" each turn. The action economy was implied rather than formally defined: you could do one significant thing (usually an attack or spell) plus move, with some activities (like drinking a potion) taking your whole turn while others (like speaking) were free.

**AD&D 1st Edition (1977)** formalized some action categories through the segments system and weapon speed factors, but interpretation varied wildly between tables. The rules attempted granular time-tracking (combat rounds as one minute, with multiple attacks possible for high-level fighters) but the execution was unclear. The action economy existed but lacked systematic categorization, leading to constant adjudication of what counted as "an action" versus what could be done freely.

**AD&D 2nd Edition (1989)** simplified but didn't fundamentally reconceptualize action economy. The standard turn structure was move + attack, with some actions (like drawing weapons or switching targets) either free or consuming your attack depending on interpretation. The lack of formal action categories meant balance issues: could you move before and after attacking? Could you drink a potion and attack? Different tables resolved these questions differently.

**The d20 System and D&D 3rd Edition (2000)** revolutionized action economy through systematic categorization. [[Jonathan Tweet]], [[Monte Cook]], and [[Skip Williams]] created a formal taxonomy: standard actions (major activities like attacking or casting most spells), move actions (repositioning), full-round actions (activities requiring your entire turn like full attacks), swift actions (quick activities), immediate actions (out-of-turn reactions), and free actions (speaking, dropping items). This system made action costs transparent and consistent, enabling sophisticated tactical play and character optimization.

The 3e action economy's elegance came from its composability: a turn consisted of one standard action and one move action, OR one full-round action. This simple framework could express complex options while maintaining clarity. Swift and immediate actions added tactical depth without overwhelming the core structure. The formalization also made action economy a balancing tool: designers could cost abilities appropriately by assigning them action types, and players could evaluate options by comparing action costs.

However, 3e's action economy also revealed problems. The gap between single attacks (standard action) and full attacks (full-round action) created the "five-foot step paradox" where movement dramatically reduced damage output, leading to static combat. Swift actions became highly valuable, creating optimization pressure around abilities that used them. The proliferation of action types occasionally confused new players.

**D&D 4th Edition (2008)** streamlined the system into standard actions, move actions, and minor actions, with one of each per turn. This elegant structure reduced complexity while maintaining tactical depth. The system explicitly balanced powers by action cost, with minor action attacks deliberately designed as weaker. 4e also introduced "action points" allowing extra actions, making action economy manipulation a core gameplay element. Opportunity attacks became more structured, creating additional tactical considerations around positioning and movement.

**D&D 5th Edition (2014)** simplified further to actions, bonus actions, and reactions. The streamlined system improved accessibility while preserving tactical choice. However, the bonus action category created unexpected balance issues: characters with consistent bonus action uses had better action economy than those without, and the "one spell per turn" rule interacted oddly with the action economy. The 5e approach prioritized simplicity and speed over tactical depth, reflecting different design values than 3e or 4e.

**Pathfinder 2nd Edition (2019)** innovated with a three-action economy: each turn you get three actions to spend however you want, with activities costing one, two, or three actions. This elegant system maximizes flexibility while maintaining balance through action costs. Moving twice is possible but means you can't attack as many times. The standardization makes it easy to evaluate abilities and prevents the "dead" turns that can happen in systems where you've used your standard action but have nothing useful to do with your move.

**13th Age (2013)** introduced action escalation: the escalation die increases each round, giving an attack bonus but also encouraging aggressive play. Quick actions allow minor tactical choices without complex action categorization. The system balances accessibility with meaningful decisions.

**Lancer (2019)** features sophisticated action economy with full actions, quick actions, free actions, and reactions, plus a special "protocol" system for ongoing effects. The mech combat system requires careful action management, with heat and other resources creating additional economy considerations. The depth supports tactical mech combat without becoming incomprehensible.

**Icon (2024)** by [[Massif Press]] experiments with limiting actions through ability cooldowns and resource generation rather than pure action types, creating a different feel for action economy where planning across turns matters as much as optimizing individual turns.

## How It Works

Action economy systems function through several interconnected mechanisms that structure turn-based play:

### Action Types and Categories

**Single Action Systems**: The simplest approach grants one significant action per turn. Players choose their most impactful option, creating clarity but limiting tactical complexity. Early RPGs often used this model implicitly.

Example: Basic D&D essentially operated on "one thing per turn"—you attack, or you cast a spell, or you use an item, plus some amount of movement. The simplicity kept combat moving but offered limited tactical depth.

**Binary Systems (Major + Minor)**: Most traditional designs split actions into two categories: major actions (significant activities like attacking or spellcasting) and minor actions (less impactful activities like moving or drawing items). Players typically get one of each per turn.

Example: D&D 5e's action + bonus action structure. Your action is typically an Attack, Cast a Spell, Dash, Disengage, Dodge, Help, Hide, Ready, Search, or Use Object. Your bonus action is only usable if you have a specific ability that grants one. This creates clear categories while keeping turn structure simple.

**Multi-Tier Systems**: More complex designs create three or more action categories with different power levels and costs, allowing finer-grained activity costing and more varied turns.

Example: D&D 3.5's standard action + move action + swift action (+ free actions and immediate actions). This allowed turns like: swift action to activate an ability, standard action to attack, move action to reposition. The additional granularity supported more tactical options and character abilities.

Example: D&D 4e's standard + move + minor action structure gave everyone something useful to do with each action type, reducing "wasted" parts of turns and encouraging dynamic play.

**Point Pool Systems**: Instead of categorical action types, characters receive action points to spend on activities with varying costs. Maximizes flexibility and makes action costs transparent.

Example: Pathfinder 2e's three-action system. Each turn you get three actions. Stride (move) costs one action. Most single attacks cost one action. Some spells cost two actions. Activities explicitly list their action cost, making it easy to evaluate options and construct turns. You might Strike twice and Raise Shield, or Stride twice and Strike once, or cast a two-action spell and Stride.

Example: Lancer's structure: one full action (major attack, tech action, boost) and one quick action (minor abilities, movement, protocols) plus free actions. The categorization supports mech combat's tactical depth while remaining manageable.

**Cooldown Systems**: Rather than limiting actions per turn, abilities have cooldown periods or resource costs. This shifts economy from per-turn action budgets to multi-turn resource management.

Example: Icon uses abilities with cooldowns (once per combat, once every few rounds) and resource generation (combo points, etc.) rather than strict action categorization. Your tactical choices involve setting up future turns as much as optimizing the current turn.

### Movement and Positioning

Action economy must address how movement interacts with other actions:

**Integrated Movement**: Movement is part of your general action allotment, costing actions that could otherwise be spent on other activities. Creates meaningful choice between mobility and offense.

Example: Pathfinder 2e's Stride action costs one of your three actions, directly competing with Strikes, spells, and other activities. Moving across the battlefield means fewer attacks.

**Separate Movement**: Characters get movement distinct from their main action, allowing repositioning without sacrificing offensive capability. Encourages dynamic combat but can reduce positional tactics' importance.

Example: D&D 5e gives you movement (typically 30 feet) separate from your action. You can move before, after, or during your action, splitting your movement freely. This keeps combat mobile but means movement rarely costs offensive power.

**Movement as Action Option**: Movement is one choice among many for your main action, but doesn't consume it unless you choose to prioritize mobility.

Example: D&D 5e's Dash action uses your action to double your movement. Normally you move and act, but if you need extra mobility, you can sacrifice your action for it.

### Action Advantage and Multiplication

Advanced action economy includes mechanics for gaining extra actions or denying opponents' actions:

**Extra Actions**: Abilities that grant additional actions represent powerful action economy advantages. Balanced games make these rare, costly, or situational.

Example: D&D 5e's Haste spell grants one extra action (limited to Attack, Dash, Disengage, Hide, or Use Object). The limitation prevents it from being overpowered while still improving action economy.

Example: D&D 4e's Action Points let you take an extra action once per encounter. This emergency button creates memorable moments without breaking balance.

**Action Denial**: Effects that prevent, delay, or limit opponents' actions damage their action economy, often more effective than direct damage.

Example: Stunning conditions typically prevent all actions for their duration. A stunned enemy contributes zero actions to their side, effectively removing them from combat temporarily—often more impactful than dealing damage.

Example: D&D 5e's Slow spell limits targets to either an action OR bonus action, not both, and prevents reactions. This roughly halves their action economy.

**Opportunity Attacks and Reactions**: Out-of-turn actions create additional tactical considerations and reward positional play.

Example: D&D 5e opportunity attacks trigger when enemies leave your reach, punishing careless movement and creating "sticky" melee zones. Characters must choose between provoking attacks or spending actions (Disengage) to move safely.

### Multi-Target Actions

Action economy interacts with how abilities affect multiple targets:

**Area Effects**: Spells or abilities affecting multiple targets provide better "action economy" by impacting multiple enemies with one action.

Example: Fireball hits all creatures in a 20-foot radius. If it damages four enemies, you've effectively used one action to impact four opponents' worth of hit points—much better economy than a single-target attack.

**Multi-Attack**: Abilities granting multiple attacks per action improve action economy but must be balanced against versatility.

Example: D&D 5e's Extra Attack feature lets fighters attack twice with one Attack action. This doubles their damage potential but locks that action into attacking rather than allowing tactical flexibility.

### Turn Duration and Balance

Action economy systems must balance turn length with tactical depth:

**Quick Turns**: Fewer action types and simpler decisions keep turns short, maintaining pacing and player engagement. Risk: potentially shallow tactics.

**Complex Turns**: More action types and options create tactical depth and character expression. Risk: analysis paralysis and slow combat.

Good design finds the sweet spot for the intended gameplay experience, matching action economy complexity to player expertise and genre expectations.

## Design Philosophy

Action economy design reflects fundamental choices about what kind of combat experience the game aims to create and how it balances competing design values.

**Tactical Depth vs. Accessibility**: More complex action economies create richer tactical gameplay with more meaningful choices per turn. Three action types with various combinations produce more decision points than a single action per turn. However, complexity increases cognitive load, especially for new players, and can slow combat to a crawl as players calculate optimal choices.

Designers must decide whether their game prioritizes depth for experienced players (3.5e, Pathfinder 2e) or accessibility for broader audiences (5e, 13th Age). The "right" answer depends on target audience and design goals. A game emphasizing tactical combat as its core appeal justifies complex action economy, while a game emphasizing story and character might prefer streamlined combat that resolves quickly.

**Character Differentiation**: Action economy provides a powerful axis for character differentiation. Classes or builds that manipulate action economy feel distinctly powerful, while those with poor action economy feel weak. A rogue with Cunning Action (bonus action Dash/Disengage/Hide) has better mobility and tactical flexibility than a class with no bonus action uses.

This creates design tension: action economy advantages feel good but can create balance problems if not carefully managed. Classes with consistent bonus action uses have inherent advantages over those without. Abilities that grant extra actions risk becoming overpowered. Designers must either ensure rough parity in action economy across character types or accept that action economy manipulation is a legitimate power axis with appropriate costs.

**Movement and Positioning**: How action economy treats movement shapes combat dynamics profoundly. Systems where movement competes with offense encourage static "stand and deliver" combat, while systems with free movement encourage mobility and dynamic positioning.

The "right" approach depends on desired tactical flavor. Games emphasizing positioning (4e, Pathfinder 2e) often make movement costly to create meaningful positional choices. Games prioritizing fast-paced action (5e, 13th Age) often separate movement from offense to keep combat mobile. Neither is inherently superior; they create different tactical textures.

**Balance Through Action Costs**: Action economy provides an elegant balancing mechanism. Powerful abilities can cost more actions (two or three actions in Pathfinder 2e, full-round actions in 3.5e) while weaker abilities cost fewer. This allows diverse power levels within a single system while maintaining balance through opportunity cost.

The transparency of action costs helps players evaluate options. "Is this two-action activity better than using two one-action activities?" becomes an answerable question. Good design ensures costs align with power: two-action abilities should be worth giving up two one-action options.

**The Action Economy Arms Race**: Games with complex action economies risk optimization spirals where system mastery means maximizing actions per turn. Finding ways to gain extra actions, use all action types efficiently, and deny enemy actions becomes central to effective play. This creates rewarding depth for optimization-focused players but can leave casual players feeling ineffective.

Designers can embrace this (3.5e, Pathfinder), minimize it (5e's simplification), or build around it (Icon's cooldown approach). Each choice attracts different player types and creates different play cultures.

## Variations Across Systems

**D&D 3.5/Pathfinder 1e**: Standard action + move action + swift action + free actions + immediate actions. Full-round actions replace standard and move. This complex system enables sophisticated tactical play and optimization but has learning curve. The "full attack" problem (moving prevents full attacks) leads to static combat, while swift action optimization becomes crucial. The system's depth rewards system mastery but can overwhelm new players.

**D&D 4th Edition**: Standard + move + minor action, with action points granting extra standard actions once per encounter. Extremely clean structure where everyone has useful options for each action type. Powers explicitly designed around action costs. Encounter powers and dailies create multi-turn resource management. The system's elegance supports tactical depth without unnecessary complexity, though some found it too game-like rather than simulationist.

**D&D 5th Edition**: Action + bonus action + reaction + movement. Radical simplification improves accessibility and speeds combat. Bonus actions only usable with specific features creates some balance concerns (classes with consistent bonus action uses have better economy). The streamlining succeeded in making D&D more approachable but sacrificed some tactical depth. Movement separate from actions keeps combat mobile.

**Pathfinder 2nd Edition**: Three actions per turn, spend however you want. Activities cost 1-3 actions as labeled. Elegant, flexible, and intuitive. Makes action costs transparent and enables varied tactical choices. Moving twice or attacking three times are equally valid. The uniform structure simplifies evaluation and balancing. Arguably the most successful modern action economy design.

**13th Age**: Standard action + move action + quick action, with quick actions being minor tactical choices. The escalation die creates tempo, encouraging offense. Simpler than 3.5 but more structured than pure narrative approaches. Strikes a balance between tactical crunch and accessibility.

**Lancer**: Full action + quick action + reactions + free actions. Full actions are major offensive or tactical choices (attacks, tech actions, boosts). Quick actions are supplementary (minor movement, activating systems). The structure supports detailed mech combat while remaining comprehensible. Heat and other resources add economy layers beyond pure actions.

**GURPS**: No formal action types; instead, all activities have time costs in seconds. Attacks, movements, and other actions cost time, with faster characters acting more frequently. Simulationist approach creates realistic pacing but requires careful timing tracking.

**Savage Worlds**: Each turn you can move your Pace and take an action (plus free actions). Simple structure speeds play. Multi-Action penalty allows multiple actions with cumulative penalties, creating flexibility without complex action categories.

**Genesys/FFG Star Wars**: One action + one maneuver per turn. Maneuvers are minor actions (movement, drawing weapons, etc.). Can take second maneuver by suffering strain. Simple two-tier system with meaningful choices.

**Shadowrun**: Complex Pass system where character initiatives determine how many passes (complete action sets) they get per turn. Enhanced characters act multiple times while normal characters act once, creating dramatic action economy differences. Supports cyberpunk's speed-enhanced combatants but creates balance challenges.

## Impact on Play

Action economy profoundly shapes combat experience, from moment-to-moment tactical decisions to overall combat pacing and character power evaluation.

**Tactical Decision-Making**: Rich action economies create decision trees each turn. Pathfinder 2e players constantly evaluate: "Should I Strike three times, or Strike twice and Raise Shield, or Stride and Strike twice, or use this two-action ability?" These choices make combat engaging and reward tactical thinking. Simpler systems reduce decision paralysis but can feel straightforward or obvious.

**Combat Pacing**: Action economy directly affects how long combat takes. More action types per turn mean longer turns as players consider options. Free-form systems risk analysis paralysis. Well-designed economies balance depth with speed, keeping turns meaningful but not excessively long.

**Power Evaluation**: Understanding action economy helps players evaluate character options. An ability usable as a bonus action is more valuable than one requiring an action if you can use both. Extra attacks granted as part of an action are more efficient than those requiring separate actions. This knowledge becomes crucial for character optimization.

**Movement and Positioning**: How movement costs actions determines combat's spatial dynamics. Games where movement competes with attacks create tactical positioning puzzles. Games with free movement create mobile, dynamic fights. Neither is wrong, but they feel dramatically different.

**The "Dead Turn" Problem**: Poor action economy design can create turns where players have actions left but nothing useful to do with them. 5e characters without bonus action abilities have "dead" bonus actions. Good design ensures all players have meaningful choices for their action allotment.
