# Flashback Mechanics

**First Appeared**: [[Blades in the Dark]] (2017)
**Designer**: [[John Harper]]
**Category**: Narrative Device / Planning Abstraction

## Overview

Flashback Mechanics, pioneered by John Harper in Blades in the Dark (2017), revolutionized heist gaming by allowing players to retroactively establish preparation rather than front-loading tedious planning sessions, creating Ocean's Eleven-style revelations ("Ah, but I already prepared for that!") through mechanical framework where players spend Stress to narrate flashbacks showing earlier preparation, solving heist gaming's perennial problem: extensive planning often proves irrelevant when execution goes awry, while pure improvisation feels inconsistent with playing skilled professionals.

The mechanic works elegantly: during the score (heist/mission), when players need something they should have prepared (lockpicks, disguises, bribed guards, researched blueprints), they declare flashback, spend Stress (0-2 based on complexity), and narrate how they prepared earlier. This jumps play directly to exciting action while maintaining feel of careful preparation—you don't plan beforehand; you reveal planning retroactively when relevant.

**Revolutionary Impact**: Flashbacks became one of RPG design's most influential recent innovations, appearing in numerous Forged in the Dark games and adopted/adapted beyond FitD for any game involving planning, heists, or preparation. The mechanic demonstrated how eliminating boring but thematically important gameplay (planning meetings) could enhance rather than diminish authenticity—competent thieves WOULD plan, but watching planning is tedious; flashbacks give you planning's benefits without its pacing problems.

**Stress Cost**: Flashbacks cost Stress (resource also used for resisting consequences), creating meaningful choice—spend Stress to have been prepared, or accept being unprepared and face consequences. This resource tension prevents unlimited retroactive preparation while enabling enough flexibility for competent professional feel.

## Historical Context

Before flashbacks, heist gaming faced structural problem: realistic planning required extensive sessions where players discussed approaches, contingencies, equipment needs, and escape routes. This planning often:
- Consumed entire sessions without advancement
- Became irrelevant when first contact with enemy changed situations
- Encouraged player paranoia and analysis paralysis
- Felt tedious even when necessary for competent thief portrayal

Games handled this poorly through:
- **Pure Planning**: Spending sessions planning missions that go differently anyway
- **GM Railroading**: Plans succeed as outlined, removing player agency when things go wrong
- **Pure Improvisation**: Professionals somehow unprepared, breaking verisimilitude
- **Abstraction**: Planning happens "off-screen" but players can't reference it

John Harper, designing Blades in the Dark, recognized this structural flaw and drew inspiration from heist films' narrative technique: movies don't show thieves planning beforehand—they jump to action, then reveal preparation through flashbacks showing "oh, they already accounted for this." Ocean's Eleven, The Italian Job, Leverage—all use this structure because it maintains pacing while showcasing competence.

Harper's genius was recognizing this narrative technique could become mechanical system: make flashbacks cost resources (Stress), and you create meaningful choice architecture while solving pacing problems. The mechanic tested extensively during Blades development, refining Stress costs and establishing limits preventing abuse.

The 2017 Blades release introduced flashbacks to gaming community. Initial reactions varied—some immediately recognized brilliance; others felt it broke immersion or seemed too meta. However, actual play proved the mechanic's value: groups using flashbacks reported dramatically improved heist pacing, better player competence feel, and more exciting sessions.

The mechanic's adoption accelerated as Forged in the Dark movement emerged. Every FitD game (Scum and Villainy, Band of Blades, Wicked Ones, etc.) included flashbacks, refining and adapting the core concept for different contexts. Beyond FitD, designers adapted flashbacks for various planning-heavy scenarios—military operations, detective investigations, con games, corporate espionage.

By 2020s, flashbacks had become standard tool in narrative gaming's mechanical toolkit, comparable to PbtA's "Moves" or FATE's "Aspects"—a specific innovation so successful it transcended its origin game becoming recognized universal mechanism.

## Mechanical Framework

### Basic Flashback Structure

**When Needed**: Player realizes they need something not established earlier

**Declaration**: "I have a flashback to earlier..." and describe preparation scene

**Stress Cost**:
- **0 Stress**: Simple preparation anyone would do (brought lockpicks)
- **1 Stress**: Moderate preparation requiring some effort (bribed a guard)
- **2 Stress**: Complex preparation requiring significant work (forged documents, elaborate disguise)

**Narration**: Player describes flashback showing preparation

**Resolution**: If flashback involves risky action, may require roll; otherwise it simply establishes fact

**Return**: Play resumes in present with established preparation

### Stress as Limiting Factor

Flashbacks cost Stress (Blades in the Dark's primary resource also used for resisting consequences). This creates meaningful economy:

**Strategic Choices**: Spend Stress on flashbacks (preparedness) or save for resisting consequences (surviving danger)?

**Prevents Abuse**: Can't flashback infinitely—Stress limits how much retroactive preparation possible

**Differential Costs**: More complex flashbacks cost more, preventing "I prepared for everything"

**Resource Tension**: Multiple uses for Stress create interesting allocation decisions

### GM Responsibilities

The GM (or equivalent) must:
- **Set Fair Costs**: Determine Stress cost based on preparation complexity
- **Honor Flashbacks**: If player pays cost, flashback happened—don't negate it
- **Maintain Fiction**: Flashback must be plausible given established facts
- **Balance**: Allow creative preparation without letting it trivialize challenges

### Variations Across Games

Different FitD and flashback-using games adjust the framework:

**Scum and Villainy** (space opera FitD): Flashbacks for off-screen planning during jobs

**Band of Blades** (military FitD): Limited flashbacks (military operations require actual planning)

**Leverage RPG**: Extensive flashback use matching TV show's structure

**Non-FitD Adaptations**: Various games adapting flashback concept with different resource costs

## Design Considerations

### When Flashbacks Work

Flashbacks excel for:
- **Heist Gaming**: Planning robberies, cons, infiltrations
- **Professional Protagonists**: Playing competent specialists
- **Pacing**: Avoiding tedious planning sessions
- **Competence Feel**: Showing characters are skilled without perfect foresight
- **Reveals**: Creating dramatic "I already planned for that" moments

### When Flashbacks Don't Fit

Less appropriate for:
- **Improvised Action**: When characters shouldn't have planned (sudden ambush)
- **Horror Gaming**: When preparation undermines vulnerability
- **Mystery**: When not-knowing is central to experience
- **Simulationist Play**: When realistic timeline matters
- **Low-Competence**: Playing amateurs or desperate characters

### Common Pitfalls

**Abuse**: Players trying to flashback out of every problem
- Solution: Stress costs limit this; GM can set high costs for excessive prep

**Immersion Breaking**: Some players find meta-game element jarring
- Solution: Frame as character remembering earlier actions not player invention

**Timeline Confusion**: When did flashback occur?
- Solution: Establish when it happened relative to score start

**Over-Reliance**: Using flashbacks when present action would be more exciting
- Solution: Flashbacks aren't mandatory—sometimes present improvisation better

## Thematic Purpose

Beyond mechanical utility, flashbacks serve themes:

**Competence**: You're professional; of course you prepared

**Genre Emulation**: Heist films use flashbacks; so do heist games

**Trust**: Flashbacks can reveal characters prepared things secretly, creating suspense

**Collaboration**: Players riff on each other's flashbacks, building collective competence

**Pacing**: Maintains momentum by skipping boring prep for exciting action

**Leverage RPG**: "Flashback to the briefing" as core mechanic

**Heist Games**: Nearly all modern heist RPGs include flashback variants

**Military Games**: Tactical briefings as flashback-style preparation

**Detective Games**: "I already investigated that" flashbacks

**Non-Heist Contexts**: Adapted for any planning-heavy scenarios

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Solves heist gaming's pacing problem brilliantly
- Creates dramatic reveals matching genre
- Maintains competence feel without tedium
- Simple elegant mechanics
- Widely applicable beyond Blades

**Criticism**:
- Some find it too meta/breaks immersion
- Can feel like retconning rather than planning
- Stress cost sometimes feels arbitrary
- Requires player buy-in to work well
- Doesn't suit all game types

## Legacy and Influence

**Universal Adoption in FitD**: Every Forged in the Dark game includes flashbacks

**Design Tool**: Became standard solution for planning-heavy scenarios
