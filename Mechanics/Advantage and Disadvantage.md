# Advantage and Disadvantage

**First Appeared In**: [[Dungeons & Dragons Fifth Edition (2014)]]
**Year**: 2014
**Complexity**: 1/5
**Popularity**: 5/5

## Overview

Advantage and Disadvantage represent D&D 5th Edition's elegant solution to situational modifiers, replacing countless +1/+2/-1/-2 bonuses and penalties with a simple binary system: roll two d20s and take the higher (advantage) or lower (disadvantage) result. This deceptively simple mechanic creates approximately +4/+5 average bonus/penalty while maintaining dramatic dice-rolling moments, eliminating arithmetic overhead, and speeding gameplay significantly. When circumstances favor you—attacking prone enemies, receiving magical help, or acting from advantageous positions—you roll with advantage. When circumstances hinder you—attacking while prone, suffering curses, or acting while restrained—you roll with disadvantage.

The genius of advantage/disadvantage lies in maintaining d20 drama while improving probability. A single d20 has equal 5% chance for each result—totally random, often frustrating. Rolling two d20s and taking the highest/lowest creates a weighted probability curve favoring extreme results without eliminating the possibility of any outcome. Advantage gives you roughly 9.75% chance to roll 20 (nearly doubled), while disadvantage reduces your natural 20 chance to only 0.25% (nearly eliminated). This mathematical elegance rewards favorable circumstances substantially while preserving the excitement of dice rolling.

Advantage and Disadvantage solved one of D&D's most persistent problems: modifier accumulation. Previous editions required tracking stacking bonuses from flanking, high ground, magical aid, inspiration, favorable conditions, and dozens of other factors. Players calculated "+2 flanking, +1 high ground, +2 Bless, +1 from Bardic Inspiration equals +6 total" before every attack. This arithmetic overhead slowed play dramatically. By replacing most modifiers with advantage (which doesn't stack—you either have it or you don't), 5th Edition streamlined play without sacrificing tactical depth. The system has proven so successful that it's been reverse-engineered into older editions, adopted by indie games, and become the most influential RPG mechanic of the 2010s.

## Historical Context

Before advantage/disadvantage, D&D accumulated modifiers extensively. Third Edition codified systematic bonuses for flanking (+2), high ground (+1), charge attacks (+2), cover (up to +8 AC), concealment (miss chances), invisibility (+2 attack, target loses Dex to AC), and countless other situational factors. Players maintained modifier checklists, asked "do I get +2 for flanking?" before every attack, and spent significant playtime calculating cumulative bonuses.

Fourth Edition attempted to address this through simplified bonus types and unified mechanics, but still required extensive modifier tracking. The edition reduced bonus variety and standardized progression, but combat remained number-heavy with players calculating attack bonuses, defense modifiers, and situational adjustments constantly.

During 5th Edition's playtest (D&D Next), designer Mike Mearls faced a critical challenge: how to maintain tactical richness while dramatically reducing mathematical overhead? The playtest explored various approaches—simplifying modifiers, eliminating circumstantial bonuses, creating new frameworks—but nothing satisfied simultaneously simple, tactically meaningful, and emotionally satisfying criteria.

The breakthrough came from recognizing that players enjoyed rolling dice far more than calculating modifiers. What if favorable/unfavorable circumstances changed the dice rolled rather than adding numbers? Roll-and-keep systems in games like Legend of the Five Rings (roll multiple dice, keep highest) demonstrated this approach's appeal, while dice pool systems showed how multiple dice created interesting probability curves.

The development team initially tested advantage/disadvantage as one option among many modifier replacements. Playtest feedback overwhelmingly favored it—players loved the tactile satisfaction of rolling two dice, the dramatic tension of seeing both results, and the speed of simply taking higher/lower without arithmetic. The mechanic tested so well that the designers progressively expanded it, eventually replacing most numerical modifiers throughout the system.

Advantage/disadvantage represents a fundamental design philosophy shift: prioritizing play speed and emotional satisfaction over mathematical precision. Previous editions pursued simulation accuracy through precise modifiers (+1 for this, +2 for that, -1 for this condition). Fifth Edition recognized that approximate bonuses generating faster, more enjoyable play outweighed numerical accuracy. This philosophy—elegant simplicity over comprehensive simulation—defines 5E's approach and explains its mainstream success.

## Mechanical Function

### Basic Resolution

When you have advantage:
1. Roll two d20s simultaneously
2. Take the higher result
3. Add your normal modifiers to that result
4. Compare to target DC or AC

When you have disadvantage:
1. Roll two d20s simultaneously
2. Take the lower result
3. Add your normal modifiers to that result
4. Compare to target DC or AC

When you have neither:
1. Roll one d20 normally
2. Add your normal modifiers
3. Compare to target DC or AC

**Example (Advantage)**:
- Rogue attacks prone orc (attacking prone enemies grants advantage)
- Rogue rolls 2d20, gets 11 and 17
- Takes 17, adds +5 attack bonus = 22 total
- Compares to orc's AC 13 = hit

**Example (Disadvantage)**:
- Fighter attacks while poisoned (poisoned condition imposes disadvantage)
- Fighter rolls 2d20, gets 14 and 7
- Takes 7, adds +6 attack bonus = 13 total
- Compares to enemy AC 15 = miss

### Critical Hits

Advantage/disadvantage affects critical hits dramatically:

**Advantage**: If either die shows 20, it's a critical hit (even if you don't use that die). Advantage nearly doubles critical hit chance from 5% to 9.75%.

**Disadvantage**: Both dice must show 20 for a critical hit. Since you're taking the lower result, disadvantage reduces critical hit chance from 5% to 0.25%—twenty times less likely.

This asymmetry creates tactical significance beyond average bonuses. Advantage-seeking becomes especially valuable for critical-fishing builds (Champions, Rogues), while disadvantage devastates critical-dependent strategies.

### Stacking and Cancellation

**Advantage and disadvantage don't stack**. Having multiple sources of advantage doesn't provide additional benefits—you still roll just two d20s. Similarly, multiple sources of disadvantage don't compound.

**Advantage and disadvantage cancel**. If you have both advantage and disadvantage simultaneously (attacking prone enemy while you're also prone), they cancel completely regardless of how many sources you have. Even one source of each negates both, resulting in a normal single d20 roll.

This elegant cancellation rule eliminates edge case tracking. You don't need to count sources—simply determine whether you have advantage, disadvantage, or neither.

### Common Sources of Advantage

**Combat Situations**:
- Attacking prone enemies (melee only; ranged has disadvantage)
- Attacking restrained, paralyzed, stunned, or unconscious targets
- Attacking invisible enemies when you can detect them
- Flanking (optional rule in DMG)
- Pack Tactics (ability where attacking with ally nearby grants advantage)
- Reckless Attack (Barbarian feature granting advantage in exchange for enemies getting advantage against you)

**Spells and Magic**:
- Faerie Fire (affected creatures are attacked with advantage)
- Guiding Bolt (next attack against target has advantage)
- Bless (adds d4 to attacks/saves, but similar spells grant advantage instead)
- True Strike (next attack has advantage—notoriously inefficient use of action)

**Conditions and Features**:
- Help action from allies
- Knocking enemies prone then attacking in melee
- High ground (optional rule)
- Inspiration (player reward mechanic allowing advantage)
- Class features like Barbarian's Reckless Attack, Rogue's Sneak Attack enabling conditions

**Ability Checks**:
- Proficiency in relevant tools
- Favorable circumstances (picking a crude lock, researching in comprehensive library)
- Guidance cantrip

### Common Sources of Disadvantage

**Combat Situations**:
- Attacking while prone
- Attacking long range with ranged weapons
- Attacking targets with heavy cover
- Attacking while restrained, poisoned, or frightened
- Attacking in darkness without darkvision
- Attacking invisible enemies

**Spells and Conditions**:
- Being poisoned (widespread condition from many sources)
- Exhaustion (level 1 imposes disadvantage on ability checks)
- Bane spell
- Hexblade's Curse (attacks against the warlock)

**Situational**:
- Darkness without darkvision
- Heavy obscurement
- Underwater combat with inappropriate weapons
- Improvised weapons
- Attempting tasks far beyond normal capability

### Mathematical Properties

Advantage/disadvantage creates distinct probability curves:

**Probability to Roll X or Higher**:
- Normal d20: 5% per number increment
- Advantage: Dramatically better odds on high numbers
  - Rolling 11+: 75% (vs 50% normal)
  - Rolling 16+: 51% (vs 25% normal)
  - Rolling 20: 9.75% (vs 5% normal)
- Disadvantage: Dramatically worse odds on high numbers
  - Rolling 11+: 25% (vs 50% normal)
  - Rolling 16+: 9% (vs 25% normal)
  - Rolling 20: 0.25% (vs 5% normal)

**Average Bonus**:
- Advantage equals approximately +4.5 to +5 average bonus (varies by target number)
- Disadvantage equals approximately -4.5 to -5 average penalty
- Most effective against medium DCs (advantage helps more against DC 12 than DC 5 or DC 20)

**Dramatic Ranges**:
- Against low DCs (need to roll 5+), advantage barely matters—you'd likely succeed anyway
- Against medium DCs (need to roll 11+), advantage provides substantial improvement
- Against high DCs (need to roll 18+), advantage helps but success remains unlikely

This curve means advantage rewards competent characters facing reasonable challenges most dramatically. Already-impossible tasks remain nearly impossible; trivial tasks remain trivial; but challenging tasks become notably more achievable.

## Design Philosophy

### Cognitive Load Reduction

Advantage/disadvantage eliminates mental arithmetic during play. Instead of "okay, +2 flanking, +1 high ground, +2 Bless equals +5 total," players simply note "I have advantage" and roll two dice. This reduces cognitive load from calculation to simple dice comparison.

The psychological impact exceeds the mechanical simplification. Players experience less decision paralysis when considering tactical options, spend less time consulting rule references for specific modifier values, and maintain better narrative immersion without constant mathematical interruption.

### Bounded Accuracy Synergy

Fifth Edition's bounded accuracy philosophy limits numerical bonuses to prevent mathematical escalation. Attack bonuses cap around +11-13 at high levels; AC rarely exceeds 19-20. This keeps d20 results mattering at all levels—even 20th-level characters can miss, and low-level threats can potentially hit legendary heroes.

Advantage/disadvantage perfectly complements bounded accuracy. By avoiding numerical bonuses, it prevents modifier inflation while still rewarding tactical play. Players gain substantial benefit from favorable positioning without breaking bounded accuracy's probability curves.

### Dramatic Dice Rolling

Rolling two d20s feels more dramatic than rolling one d20 and adding +2. The physical act of rolling multiple dice, the suspense of seeing both results, and the moment of comparison creates emotional engagement. Players want both dice to roll high (advantage) or low (disadvantage), creating doubled excitement compared to single die rolls.

This dramatic element proves surprisingly important. Playtest data showed players enjoyed advantage/disadvantage sessions more than equivalent numerical bonus sessions, despite mathematically similar outcomes. The emotional satisfaction of rolling multiple dice outweighed numerical precision.

### Tactical Simplicity

Advantage/disadvantage creates binary tactical decisions: "Do I have advantage? No? Then how do I get it?" This simplifies tactical thinking compared to optimizing multiple +1/+2 bonuses. Players ask: "Should I knock this enemy prone (granting advantage) or attack normally?" rather than calculating whether +2 flanking plus potential +1 high ground justifies repositioning.

The binary nature paradoxically increases tactical creativity. Since advantage doesn't stack, pursuing the first source of advantage is valuable, but pursuing additional sources provides no mechanical benefit. This encourages creative problem-solving for that crucial first source rather than mechanical optimization stacking bonuses.

### Universal Application

Advantage/disadvantage applies to all d20 rolls—attacks, saves, ability checks. This universal framework means players learn one mechanic that applies everywhere, reducing rules memorization. Want to help your ally? Grant advantage. Environmental hazard? Impose disadvantage. The same solution applies consistently across contexts.

## Evolution Over Time

### D&D 5th Edition (2014)

Advantage/disadvantage debuted in 5th Edition as a revolutionary core mechanic. The designers progressively expanded its role throughout playtesting, eventually making it the primary modifier system.

Key design decisions:

**Non-Stacking**: Advantage doesn't stack, preventing bonus accumulation that plagued previous editions. This controversial choice prioritized simplicity over simulation but proved crucial to maintaining bounded accuracy.

**Cancellation**: Advantage and disadvantage cancel completely, creating elegant binary state (advantage, disadvantage, or normal). This eliminates tracking multiple sources.

**Critical Hit Interaction**: Either die showing 20 counts for advantage; both must show 20 for disadvantage. Creates asymmetrical critical hit probability adding tactical depth.

**Inspiration System**: Introduced player-reward mechanic granting advantage when roleplaying well, connecting narrative play to mechanical benefit.

The mechanic's success exceeded design expectations. Community reception was overwhelmingly positive, with advantage/disadvantage frequently cited as 5E's best innovation.

### Retroactive Application

The mechanic proved so popular that fans reverse-engineered it into earlier editions. AD&D 2nd Edition retroclones incorporated advantage/disadvantage. Pathfinder 1st Edition players house-ruled advantage/disadvantage replacements for situation modifiers. The OSR community embraced it enthusiastically despite the movement's general preference for classic mechanics.

This backward adoption is extremely rare in RPG history. Mechanics typically flow from old systems to new, not vice versa. Advantage/disadvantage's reverse propagation demonstrates its exceptional design success.

### Influence on Other Games

The mechanic influenced countless games beyond D&D:

**Direct Adoption**: Many indie games explicitly use "roll 2d20, take higher/lower" with explicit D&D attribution.

**Inspired Variants**: Games created similar "roll multiple dice, select one" mechanics adapting the concept to different dice types or selection criteria.

**Design Philosophy**: Even games not using advantage/disadvantage adopted its philosophy—replace mathematical modifiers with dice manipulation for faster, more dramatic resolution.

The speed of influence (2014-2020) demonstrates the mechanic's immediate impact on game design thinking.

### Criticism and Refinement

Despite widespread praise, advantage/disadvantage received specific criticisms:

**Non-Stacking Controversy**: Some players and designers argue multiple advantages should provide additional benefit. Various house rules emerged granting +2 bonus with multiple advantage sources, or allowing additional dice (roll 3d20, keep highest with double advantage).

**Mathematical Flatness**: A +5 magical weapon provides identical attack benefit to prone enemy (both grant advantage)—the distinction between overwhelming superiority and minor tactical edge feels lost.

**Numerical Modifiers Still Exist**: While many modifiers became advantage/disadvantage, some numerical bonuses remain (+1/+2/+3 magic weapons, proficiency bonus, ability modifiers), creating partial but not complete simplification.

**Edge Cases**: Situations combining advantage sources with numerical penalties create ambiguity—attack with advantage but -5 penalty (Sharpshooter feat) requires tracking both dice manipulation and modifier.

Despite criticisms, the mechanic remained unchanged through 5E's lifetime. The designers determined its benefits outweighed limitations, and any refinements would add complexity undermining the core simplicity.

## Cultural Impact

### Most Influential Modern Mechanic

Advantage/disadvantage is arguably the most influential RPG mechanic of the 2010s. Its adoption speed and breadth exceed any contemporary mechanic, appearing in games across all genres, scales, and design philosophies within years of 5E's release.

The mechanic became a design template: "Can we replace these modifiers with advantage/disadvantage-style dice manipulation?" Game designers routinely evaluate whether advantage/disadvantage approaches suit their systems, even when ultimately choosing alternatives.

### "Rolling with Advantage"

The phrase "rolling with advantage" entered gaming vernacular beyond D&D contexts. Players in non-5E games say "I'm at advantage here" or "that gives me disadvantage" despite their games using different mechanics. The terminology became metaphorical shorthand for favorable/unfavorable circumstances.

Video games, board games, and other media reference "advantage" as mechanical concept even when not using the specific mechanic. This linguistic adoption demonstrates cultural penetration beyond D&D's immediate community.

### Accessibility Contribution

Advantage/disadvantage significantly contributed to 5E's mainstream success by lowering entry barriers. New players grasp "roll two, take higher" instantly, while previous editions' modifier calculations intimidated non-gamers. This accessibility attracted audiences outside traditional gaming demographics, expanding the hobby substantially.

The mechanic's contribution to D&D's 2010s renaissance cannot be overstated. While multiple factors drove 5E's success, advantage/disadvantage's elegant simplicity made the game approachable to mainstream audiences previously deterred by D&D's mathematical reputation.

### Design Philosophy Shift

Beyond the specific mechanic, advantage/disadvantage represented philosophical shift in RPG design: prioritizing play experience over simulation precision. This philosophy influenced the broader industry—designers increasingly evaluate whether mechanical precision improves actual play or merely satisfies simulation completeness.

The "does this need to be a +2 or can it be advantage?" question became a design heuristic. This shift toward simplification without sacrificing depth influenced numerous games throughout the 2010s.

## Strengths and Weaknesses

### Strengths

**Extreme Simplicity**: Rolling two dice and comparing is trivially easy. Eliminates arithmetic overhead and accelerates play significantly.

**Dramatic Satisfaction**: Rolling multiple dice feels exciting. Players enjoy the physical act and suspense more than adding numbers.

**Universal Framework**: Applies consistently to all d20 rolls. Learn once, use everywhere.

**Tactically Meaningful**: Substantial benefit (~+5 average) rewards tactical thinking and positioning without breaking bounded accuracy.

**No Stacking Complexity**: Can't accumulate overwhelming bonuses. Maintains bounded accuracy and prevents optimization arms races.

**Fast Adjudication**: DM determines "advantage, disadvantage, or normal" instantly without consulting specific modifier tables.

**Elegant Cancellation**: Advantage and disadvantage cancel regardless of sources, eliminating edge case tracking.

**Scalable Benefit**: Advantage helps more against challenging DCs than trivial ones, creating natural difficulty scaling.

**Critical Hit Impact**: Dramatically affects critical hit probability, creating additional tactical consideration.

**Accessibility**: New players understand immediately, lowering entry barriers substantially.

### Weaknesses

**Loss of Granularity**: All favorable circumstances equivalent to advantage regardless of whether they're minor (+1) or major (+5) factors. Feels overly simplified to simulation-focused players.

**Non-Stacking Frustration**: Multiple tactical advantages provide no additional benefit beyond the first. Coordinated party tactics don't accumulate mechanically.

**Binary State**: Only three states (advantage, disadvantage, normal) exist. Can't represent increasingly favorable/unfavorable circumstances incrementally.

**Numerical Modifiers Persist**: Magic items, proficiency, abilities still use numerical bonuses, creating partial but not complete simplification. System uses two different modifier types inconsistently.

**Mathematical Equivalence**: +5 sword and prone enemy both grant advantage, feeling mathematically identical despite representing vastly different circumstances.

**High/Low DC Limitations**: Advantage barely matters against very easy or very hard DCs. Most effective in medium difficulty range, feeling less impactful at extremes.

**Critical Fishing**: Advantage dramatically increases critical hit chance, potentially creating critical-focused builds that dominate through probability manipulation rather than diverse tactics.

**Inspiration Implementation**: The inspiration system (DM-granted advantage for good roleplaying) often goes unused in practice, wasting the mechanic's player-engagement potential.

**Edge Case Ambiguity**: Combining advantage with numerical penalties (Sharpshooter, Great Weapon Master) or advantage with disadvantage from different sources creates occasional adjudication questions.

## Modern Usage

### Best Practices

Contemporary advantage/disadvantage implementation wisdom:

**Grant Liberally**: Reward creative problem-solving, environmental usage, and teamwork with advantage. The non-stacking nature prevents abuse.

**Use for Drama**: Save advantage/disadvantage for dramatically interesting moments rather than routine actions. Maintains special feeling.

**Explain Narratively**: State why advantage/disadvantage applies. "You have advantage because the enemy is distracted" creates narrative immersion.

**Inspiration Consistency**: If using inspiration (DM-granted advantage rewards), establish clear criteria for earning it. Inconsistent inspiration feels arbitrary.

**Minimize Numerical Modifiers**: Resist adding +1/+2 bonuses. Use advantage/disadvantage instead whenever possible to maintain system elegance.

**Situational Disadvantage**: Use disadvantage to represent environmental challenges, creating tension without needing complex subsystems.

**Tactical Communication**: Explicitly discuss advantage-granting tactics during combat. "If you knock that enemy prone, I'll have advantage on my attack."

**Balance Sources**: Ensure both advantage and disadvantage appear regularly. If players always have advantage, it loses special meaning.

**House Rule Carefully**: If implementing non-stacking variations or multiple advantage rules, maintain simplicity. Don't reintroduce the complexity advantage/disadvantage eliminated.

### Variants and House Rules

Common advantage/disadvantage modifications:

**Super Advantage**: When multiple sources grant advantage, roll 3d20 and take highest. Rewards stacking favorable circumstances while maintaining dice-based resolution.

**Numerical Hybrid**: Multiple advantage sources provide +2 bonus after first. Combines advantage's drama with stacking rewards.

**Elven Accuracy**: Official feat allowing re-roll of one die when you have advantage, creating "even better advantage" for specific character concepts.

**Degrees of Advantage**: Some homebrew systems create advantage/super advantage/supreme advantage tiers with 2d20/3d20/4d20 dice. Adds complexity but represents scaling circumstances.

**Advantage Points**: Banking advantage for future use, converting advantage/disadvantage into spendable currency. Increases tactical depth but adds resource tracking.

**Conditional Advantage**: Advantage applies only to specific roll aspects (damage but not hit, hit but not damage). Adds granularity but complicates adjudication.

### Beyond D&D 5E

Advantage/disadvantage-inspired mechanics in other games:

**Shadowdark RPG**: OSR retroclone using advantage/disadvantage despite otherwise classic AD&D structure.

**Advantage-based indie games**: Numerous small press games explicitly use "roll 2d20, take higher/lower" with attribution to 5E.

**Dice manipulation**: Games use similar "roll multiple dice, select based on circumstances" applied to d6, d10, or other die types.

**Fate's Invoke**: While predating advantage/disadvantage, Fate's aspect invocation for +2 or reroll performs similar function—circumstantial benefits without permanent modifiers.

**Genesys Advantage**: While mechanically different (adding success die), serves similar purpose of representing favorable circumstances simply.

**[[d20 System]]**: Advantage/disadvantage integrates with core d20 resolution, modifying probability while maintaining roll+modifier structure.

**Bounded Accuracy**: Complementary design philosophy limiting numerical progression to maintain roll relevance.

**Inspiration**: Player reward mechanic granting advantage for good roleplaying, connecting narrative engagement to mechanical benefit.

**Critical Hits**: Advantage/disadvantage dramatically affects critical hit probability, creating tactical consideration beyond average bonus.

**Proficiency Bonus**: Numerical bonus that stacks with advantage/disadvantage, representing character expertise alongside circumstantial factors.
