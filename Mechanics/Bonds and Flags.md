# Bonds and Flags

**First Appeared In**: **[[Apocalypse World (2010)]]** (Hx/History), formalized as Bonds in **[[Dungeon World (2012)]]**
**Year**: 2010
**Complexity**: 2/5
**Popularity**: 3/5

## Overview

Bonds and Flags are character-to-character relationship mechanics that transform abstract "I know these people" into concrete narrative hooks with mechanical weight. Bonds are short statements describing specific relationships between player characters: "Marcus saved my life during the goblin raid," "I don't trust Sarah's judgment in combat," "The priest owes me a debt they can never repay." Flags are character elements (desires, vulnerabilities, beliefs, goals) that signal to other players "create situations involving THIS"—they're explicit invitations for dramatic engagement. Together, these mechanics systematize relationship play, ensuring that party members aren't just mechanically-linked murder hobos but actual characters with complex, evolving interpersonal dynamics that drive stories forward.

The genius of Bonds lies in forcing players to articulate specific relationships rather than vague abstractions. Not "we're friends" but "I've seen Marcus at his worst and I'm helping him become better." Not "I know Sarah" but "Sarah thinks I'm reckless, but I'm going to prove her wrong." These concrete statements create immediate story hooks—the Bond itself suggests future scenes, conflicts, and developments. When Bonds provide mechanical benefits (bonus dice, advantage, XP for resolution), players gain incentive to engage with relationships actively rather than treating party members as interchangeable tactical units.

Flags work differently but complementarily. Instead of defining existing relationships, Flags signal desired dramatic content: "My character is haunted by their violent past—please create scenes forcing them to confront it," "I want my character to struggle with faith—put religious challenges in my path," "I'm interested in romance—introduce potential love interests." Traditional RPGs left content preferences implicit, forcing GMs to guess what players wanted. Flags make desires explicit, transforming GMing from mind-reading into responding to clear signals. This creates collaborative storytelling where players actively request dramatic content and GMs deliver it, resulting in more satisfying, player-driven narratives.

## Historical Context

Early RPGs treated party relationships minimally. D&D characters were murder-hobo adventuring parties united by treasure-seeking. Alignment conflicts provided rudimentary party tension, but interpersonal relationships were player-improvised roleplaying, not mechanical systems. Characters might develop relationships through play, but systems offered no support, incentive, or structure.

The indie RPG movement (2000s) questioned this absence. Games like Sorcerer (2001) introduced Kickers—player-authored plot hooks launching campaigns. Dogs in the Vineyard (2004) used relationship dice—dice added to pools when relationships mattered, mechanically rewarding engagement with NPCs and fellow players. My Life with Master (2003) structured play entirely around dysfunctional relationships between master and minions. These experiments demonstrated that relationship mechanics could generate dramatic content and incentivize character-focused play.

Vincent Baker's Apocalypse World (2010) introduced Hx (History) mechanics—numerical values tracking how well characters knew each other, improved through shared experiences and highlighted moments. Hx started as simple familiarity ratings but evolved through play into mechanical benefits—adding Hx to rolls when helping/interfering with allies, gaining XP for highlighted relationships, and modifying moves based on relationship depth. This created mechanical incentive for relationship development beyond pure roleplay.

Dungeon World (2012) refined Hx into Bonds—specific narrative statements with mechanical effects. Instead of abstract numerical familiarity, Bonds described concrete relationships: "Aventyr has my back when things go wrong," "I worry that Kasper is too reckless," "Nora and I have a con going." These statements felt more narratively engaging than numbers while maintaining mechanical benefits. Resolving Bonds (when relationships changed significantly through play) granted XP, incentivizing relationship evolution and character development.

The concept of Flags emerged from online RPG theory discussions (circa 2010-2012) as designers recognized a common pattern: successful games happened when GMs understood player interests and created content matching those interests. Vincent Baker articulated this as "say what your character wants and the GM will push on it." Various games implemented explicit Flag mechanics—character aspects, beliefs, goals, drives—that communicated content preferences mechanically rather than requiring out-of-character meta-conversations.

Burning Wheel (2002) used Beliefs (character convictions), Instincts (automatic reactions), and Traits (personality descriptors) as proto-Flags—statements guiding play and earning rewards when challenged. The Quiet Year (2013) used community desires and conflicts as collective Flags. Hillfolk (2013) structured entire play around emotional conflicts mapped through relationship web. These diverse implementations explored how explicit content signaling could improve play experience.

## Mechanical Function

### Bonds (Dungeon World Style)

**Structure**: Short statement describing relationship between two PCs

**Creation**: During character creation, each player writes Bonds to other PCs based on playbook prompts:
- Fighter: "\_\_\_\_ owes me their life, whether they admit it or not."
- Wizard: "\_\_\_\_ will play an important role in the events to come. I have foreseen it!"
- Thief: "I stole something from \_\_\_\_."

**Mechanical Benefits**:
- **Aid/Interfere**: When helping or hindering another PC, roll +Bond (number of Bonds with that PC)
- **Class Moves**: Some moves trigger based on Bonds or grant benefits when Bonds exist
- **Advancement**: Resolving Bonds (marking them off when relationships fundamentally change) grants XP

**Resolution**: When Bond no longer represents current relationship, mark it resolved and write new Bond reflecting evolved relationship:
- Old: "Marcus saved my life during the goblin raid"
- Resolved after Marcus betrays party: "I can never trust Marcus again"
- XP gained for resolution

**Example Usage**:
Player helps ally with Bond "I've sworn to protect Sarah with my life"
- Roll 2d6+2 (has 2 Bonds with Sarah)
- On 10+, Sarah gets +3 to their roll
- Success reinforces protective Bond narrative

### History/Hx (Apocalypse World Style)

**Structure**: Numerical value (-3 to +3 typically) representing how well you understand another PC

**Creation**: During first session, each player answers character-specific questions determining starting Hx:
- Battlebabe: "Who's watched you fight and respects what they saw? Give them Hx+2"
- Brainer: "Who's brain have you been inside? Give them Hx+3 and tell them something you learned"

**Mechanical Benefits**:
- **Help/Interfere**: Roll 2d6+Hx when aiding or hindering another PC
- **Read Person**: When reading another PC, roll+Hx instead of +Sharp
- **Experience**: At session end, highlighted character whose Hx changed most during session grants XP to players

**Adjustment**: Hx increases through:
- Highlighted moments: When dramatically significant interaction occurs, increase Hx by 1
- Reset: When Hx reaches +4, resets to +1 and marks experience (represents relationship deepening beyond simple familiarity)

**Example**:
Driver has Hx+2 with Hardholder. When Driver helps Hardholder negotiate, rolls 2d6+2. Strong history means Driver understands Hardholder well enough to assist effectively.

### Flags (Multiple Implementations)

**Structure**: Explicit signals about desired content, implemented various ways:

**Beliefs** (Burning Wheel):
- Statement of character conviction: "The old gods must be honored or disaster will befall us"
- GM creates situations challenging Belief
- Successfully pursuing Belief in the face of consequences grants Artha (fate points)

**Keys** (Lady Blackbird):
- Character drives with XP triggers: "Key of the Daredevil: XP when you take unnecessary risks, bonus XP when risk leads to trouble, buyoff: refuse a dangerous opportunity"
- Keys explicitly tell GM what scenes/content the player wants
- Buyoff option allows retiring Keys when character development makes them inappropriate

**Drives** (Mouse Guard):
- Character goals for session: "Find out who betrayed the patrol," "Protect my mentor's reputation"
- Completing Drive grants rewards
- Explicitly communicates player's session priorities

**Issues** (Smallville RPG):
- Personal challenges/vulnerabilities: "Haunted by my father's legacy," "Suspicious of authority"
- Other players/GM use Issues to create complications and dramatic scenes
- Issues as explicit invitations for drama

**Motivations** (various):
- Character ambitions, fears, relationships
- Engage with Motivations = mechanical benefits
- Essentially Flags formalized as character sheet elements

### Using Flags in Play

**GM Response**:
1. **Observe Flags**: Note character Flags (Beliefs, Keys, Drives, Issues, etc.)
2. **Create Relevant Content**: Design scenes, NPCs, challenges that engage Flags
3. **Apply Pressure**: Create situations where pursuing Flag-aligned goals requires sacrifice or difficult choices

**Example**:
Character has Key of the Protector: "Gain XP when you protect innocents, bonus XP when protection causes personal harm"
- GM introduces innocent villagers threatened by monsters
- Character must choose: pursue original mission or protect villagers?
- Protecting villagers grants XP (Flag engagement rewarded)
- Complications arise from delay/exposure
- Flag transforms potential side-quest into character-defining moment

**Player Communication**:
Players use Flags to communicate interests to table:
- "My Belief is about revenge against the Red Duke—I want that in the spotlight"
- "I've taken Key of Romance—please introduce potential romantic subplots"
- "My Drive is discovering my mentor's fate—that's my priority this session"

### Bonds and Flags Synergy

Bonds and Flags work together to create character-focused play:

**Bonds** define existing relationships, creating interpersonal drama and mechanical cooperation incentives.

**Flags** signal desired future content, guiding GM and fellow players toward satisfying storylines.

**Example Character**:
- **Bonds**: "I owe Marcus a life-debt" and "Sarah questions my honor"
- **Flags** (Keys): "Key of Brotherhood: XP when helping fellow warriors" and "Key of Vengeance: XP when pursuing those who wronged me"

This character wants:
- Scenes with Marcus where life-debt matters (Bond-driven)
- Scenes with Sarah where honor is tested (Bond-driven)
- Combat helping allies (Flag-driven)
- Revenge opportunities (Flag-driven)

The GM and players can create content satisfying these desires, ensuring the player gets spotlight moments aligned with their interests.

## Design Philosophy

### Explicit Content Negotiation

Bonds and Flags formalize content preferences that traditionally remained implicit. Instead of GMs guessing what players want, Flags explicitly communicate desires. This respects player agency—their stated interests receive spotlight—and reduces GM burden by providing clear content direction.

Traditional GMing requires reading player reactions, inferring preferences from character choices, and hoping content lands well. Flag-based design makes preferences explicit: "I want revenge storylines" or "I want to explore my character's faith crisis." The GM delivers requested content confidently rather than guessing.

### Mechanical Incentive for Roleplay

Traditional systems reward combat/progression mechanically while treating relationships as pure roleplay. Bonds/Flags reverse this by offering XP, mechanical bonuses, or other rewards for relationship engagement. This incentivizes character-focused play for players who respond to mechanical rewards, expanding who engages with narrative content beyond "natural roleplayers."

**Example**: Player focused on tactical optimization might ignore relationships without mechanical incentive. When Bond resolution grants XP equivalent to defeating encounters, they engage with relationships because they're mechanically rewarding, discovering enjoyment in narrative play through mechanical gateway.

### Collaborative Storytelling

Flags enable distributed narrative authority. Instead of GM creating all content, players signal desired content through Flags, effectively collaborating on story direction. The GM facilitates rather than dictating, responding to player-communicated interests.

This shifts GMing from "create adventure, hope players enjoy" to "observe what players want (via Flags), create accordingly." It creates responsive, player-driven storytelling where content emerges from character interests rather than predetermined plots.

### Relationship Evolution as Advancement

Resolving Bonds treats relationship development as character advancement, equivalent to leveling or gaining abilities. Characters grow not just through power increases but through relationship evolution—learning to trust former enemy, recognizing ally's fatal flaw, evolving from partnership to romance. This positions interpersonal development as mechanically significant, not secondary to combat prowess.

### Safety and Consent

Flags can incorporate safety tools—players Flag content they DON'T want alongside content they do. "Key of the Protector" signals desired content; absence of "Key of Romance" signals romantic subplots shouldn't target that character without discussion. This creates implicit consent framework where Flags communicate boundaries alongside interests.

## Evolution Over Time

### Early Relationship Mechanics (2000-2008)

**Sorcerer Kickers** (2001): Player-authored plot hooks launching play, proto-Flags communicating desired content.

**My Life with Master** (2003): Structured play entirely around master-minion relationships, demonstrating relationship-as-mechanics viability.

**Dogs in the Vineyard** (2004): Relationship dice adding to pools when relationships mattered, mechanically rewarding NPC connections.

**Polaris** (2005): Formalized relationships between players (Heart, Mistaken, New Moon) with mechanical implications and defined narrative roles.

### Apocalypse World Hx (2010)

Introduced numerical familiarity tracking with mechanical benefits and XP rewards for relationship development. Refined through playtest into elegant +Hx mechanics that:
- Started numerical (abstract familiarity)
- Improved through highlighted moments
- Granted mechanical bonuses when helping/interfering
- Provided XP for relationship depth

Influenced entire PbtA lineage, with most PbtA games using Hx or variants.

### Dungeon World Bonds (2012)

Transformed numerical Hx into narrative statements:
- More accessible to traditional RPG players
- Clearer dramatic content than numbers
- Maintained mechanical benefits and XP rewards
- Created template for future relationship mechanics

Bonds' success demonstrated narrative statements worked better than numerical values for many groups, spawning countless variations.

### Burning Wheel Beliefs (2002, revised 2005)

Parallel development of proto-Flags:
- Beliefs as character convictions
- Instincts as automatic reactions
- Traits as personality descriptors
- Artha rewards for pursuing Beliefs despite consequences

Created sophisticated Flag system rewarding character-consistent play and providing GM with clear content direction.

### The Quiet Year (2013)

Collective Flags representing community desires:
- Community members want different things
- Discover opportunities raising Flags about potential content
- Collective Flag negotiation creates emergent storytelling

Demonstrated Flags needn't be individual character-focused—could represent group desires.

### Modern Implementations (2014-Present)

Contemporary games use sophisticated relationship/Flag combinations:

**Masks** (2016): Influence mechanics tracking how other characters shape your self-image, with relationship moves triggering based on Influence levels.

**Dream Askew** (2018): No dice, only narrative tokens. Relationship mechanics entirely narrative-based without numerical tracking.

**The Veil** (2016): Emotional state tracks serve as Flags—"I'm feeling Peaceful/Joyful/Angry"—signaling current character state and inviting relevant content.

**Fellowship** (2015): Bonds create explicit mechanical synergies between characters, encouraging coordinated actions and relationship-based tactics.

## Cultural Impact

### Relationship Mechanics Normalization

Bonds/Flags normalized mechanical relationship support in indie RPGs. Pre-2010, relationship mechanics were niche experiments; post-2015, they're expected elements in narrative-focused games. Designers routinely ask "how does this game handle relationships mechanically?" rather than treating them as pure roleplay.

### Player Communication Tools

Flags established design pattern of explicit player-to-GM content communication. This influenced broader industry discussions about Session Zero, Lines/Veils, X-Card, and other safety/communication tools. The principle "players should explicitly communicate preferences" became standard design wisdom.

### Party-Focused Play

Bonds challenged traditional RPG assumption that party unity was default state requiring no mechanical support. By mechanically rewarding and tracking relationships, Bonds acknowledged that party cohesion is active achievement requiring work, creating systems supporting it.

### XP for Roleplay

Granting XP for Bond resolution legitimized roleplay-for-advancement, influencing how designers think about progression. Advancement needn't only come from combat/skill use—character development and relationship evolution can be equally mechanically significant.

### Collaborative GMing Vocabulary

"Flags" as term entered RPG theory vocabulary. Designers discuss "what are this character's Flags?" even in games without formal Flag mechanics, demonstrating conceptual adoption beyond literal implementation.

## Strengths and Weaknesses

### Strengths

**Explicit Content Communication**: Players tell GM/table what they want through Flags, eliminating guesswork and ensuring relevant content.

**Mechanical Roleplay Incentive**: XP/bonuses for relationship engagement incentivize character-focused play for mechanically-motivated players.

**Relationship Structure**: Bonds provide framework for party relationships, preventing generic "we're all friends" vagueness.

**Character Development as Advancement**: Bond resolution treats interpersonal growth as mechanically significant progression.

**Collaborative Storytelling**: Flags enable distributed narrative authority where players shape content direction.

**Drama Generation**: Bonds/Flags create obvious story hooks—GM looks at Bonds/Flags and immediately sees potential scenes.

**Safety Integration**: Flags can communicate boundaries alongside interests, creating implicit consent framework.

**Party Cohesion Support**: Mechanics supporting relationship development help forge actual party bonds rather than assuming them.

**Accessible Implementation**: Simple enough for traditional RPG players to adopt without steep learning curves.

**Evolution Through Play**: Bonds change as relationships evolve, creating mechanical representation of character growth.

### Weaknesses

**Player Buy-In Required**: Bonds/Flags only work if players engage sincerely. Mechanical compliance without narrative engagement creates hollow results.

**Writing Difficulty**: Some players struggle articulating specific Bonds/Flags, leading to generic or vague statements that don't generate useful content.

**GM Workload**: Responding to multiple players' Flags creates significant GM prep and improvisation burden.

**Spotlight Distribution**: Players with more assertive/interesting Flags may dominate spotlight, creating imbalance.

**Mechanical Interference**: Some players feel Bonds/Flags make relationships feel transactional—"I'm only helping you for the XP bonus."

**Relationship Forcing**: Predetermined Bond prompts can feel artificial—"Write a Bond with this stranger you just met" creates forced intimacy.

**Flag Exhaustion**: Repeatedly engaging same Flags becomes repetitive. Players need to update/change Flags but sometimes resist.

**Cultural Resistance**: Traditional RPG culture sometimes views relationship mechanics as "unnecessary complexity" or "therapy not gaming."

**Numerical Abstraction**: Hx-style numerical tracking can feel disconnected from actual relationship development—"I have Hx+3 with you but we've barely interacted."

**Maintenance Overhead**: Tracking, updating, and resolving multiple Bonds/Flags across party requires consistent attention.

## Modern Usage

### Best Practices

Contemporary Bond/Flag implementation wisdom:

**Clear Prompts**: Provide specific Bond-writing prompts rather than "write a relationship." "Who saved your life?" more useful than "create a Bond."

**Regular Updates**: Schedule Bond updates (every 2-3 sessions), ensuring they evolve with relationships rather than becoming stale.

**Spotlight Rotation**: Ensure all players' Flags receive attention. Don't let aggressive Flags dominate all content.

**Collaborate on Bonds**: Let players discuss Bonds together rather than writing in isolation. "Can I write that I saved your life?" creates shared buy-in.

**Flag Variety**: Encourage diverse Flags—not all combat-focused or all romance-focused. Variety creates well-rounded content.

**Integrate with Story**: Tie Flags to campaign themes. If running political intrigue, suggest Flags related to power, loyalty, betrayal.

**XP Balance**: Ensure relationship XP doesn't overshadow other advancement methods. Balance incentives so players pursue varied content.

**Soft Flags**: Some players prefer implicit communication. Don't force reluctant players to write formal Flags—observe their play and extrapolate interests.

**Flag Retirement**: Let players retire satisfied Flags and introduce new ones. Character arc completion should enable new directions.

**Party Bonds**: Consider collective party Bonds—shared goals, values, or history creating group cohesion beyond individual relationships.

### When to Use Bonds/Flags

Bonds/Flags work best for:

- Character-driven campaigns prioritizing interpersonal drama
- Story-focused groups valuing narrative over tactics
- Games supporting long-term character development
- Systems where relationships mechanically matter (aid/interfere, emotional support, cooperation)
- Groups comfortable with explicit content negotiation
- Campaigns emphasizing party dynamics and internal conflicts

Bonds/Flags may not suit:

- Tactical combat-focused games with minimal roleplay
- One-shots where relationship development lacks time to matter
- West Marches or rotating cast where party composition constantly changes
- Players uncomfortable with formalized relationship mechanics
- Groups preferring entirely organic relationship development
- Games where party unity is assumed and internal conflict unwanted

### Implementation Variations

**Light Bonds**: Simple "I know/trust/distrust this person" without detailed narrative statements. Lower overhead, easier maintenance.

**Descriptive Flags**: Aspects/traits serving as Flags without mechanical triggers. GM observes and engages them naturally.

**Session Goals**: Simplified Flags—"this session I want to pursue X"—providing short-term direction without long-term commitment.

**Relationship Maps**: Visual diagrams connecting characters with descriptive links replacing text-based Bonds.

**Trust Mechanics**: Numerical trust/suspicion values affected by actions, creating organic relationship tracking.
