# Powered by the Apocalypse (Moves)

**First Appeared In**: [[Apocalypse World (2010)]]
**Year**: 2010
**Complexity**: 2/5
**Popularity**: 5/5

## Description

Powered by the Apocalypse (PbtA), created by D. Vincent Baker for Apocalypse World, represents a revolutionary approach to RPG mechanics. The system centers on "Moves"—discrete triggered actions that resolve using 2d6 + stat, with three-tier outcomes: 10+ (full success), 7-9 (success with cost/complication), and 6- (GM makes a move/bad consequences).

PbtA's innovation lies in triggering mechanics from fictional circumstances rather than player declarations, creating conversation-based play where mechanics structure improvisation. The framework has spawned hundreds of derivative games across every genre, making it one of the most influential modern RPG designs.

## How It Works

### Core Mechanics

**Fiction Triggers Moves**: Players describe what their characters do. If the fiction matches a Move trigger, the Move activates.

**2d6 + Stat Resolution**:
- Roll 2d6
- Add relevant stat (typically -2 to +3)
- 10+: Full success, achieve goal
- 7-9: Partial success, success with cost/complication, or hard choice
- 6-: Failure, GM makes a move (escalate situation, introduce danger)

**Example Move - Seize by Force**:
When you take something by force or defend yourself in combat, roll +Hard. On 10+, choose 3. On 7-9, choose 2:
- You inflict terrible harm
- You suffer little harm
- You take definite hold of it
- You frighten, dismay, or impress your enemy

### Resolution Process

1. Player describes character action in fiction
2. If action matches Move trigger, Move activates
3. Roll 2d6 + appropriate stat
4. Interpret results based on Move text
5. Players and GM narrate outcomes
6. Fiction changes based on results, potentially triggering new Moves

### Edge Cases and Variations

**+1 Forward/Ongoing**: Some Moves grant +1 to next roll (forward) or continuing bonus (ongoing) based on circumstances.

**Hold and Spend**: Some Moves generate "hold" (points) spent later for specific effects.

**Advanced/Playbook Moves**: Each character playbook has unique Moves unavailable to other playbooks, creating distinct play experiences.

**Custom Moves**: GMs create one-off Moves for specific situations, extending the framework dynamically.

**Highlighted Stats**: GM highlights two stats per session; players gain experience when rolling those stats, encouraging varied approaches.

**Player-Facing Rolls**: Only players roll dice. GM never rolls, instead making Moves based on principles when players fail or provide openings.

## Variations and Iterations

**Apocalypse World (2010)**: Original implementation with post-apocalyptic playbooks and explicit GM principles.

**Dungeon World (2012)**: Adapted PbtA to D&D-style fantasy, demonstrating the framework's genre flexibility.

**Monsterhearts (2012)**: Teen supernatural drama showing PbtA's effectiveness for social/emotional play.

**The Sprawl (2016)**: Cyberpunk missions with linked Moves creating procedural structure.

**Masks (2016)**: Superhero teen drama with shifting labels replacing static stats.

**Blades in the Dark (2017)**: Heavily modified PbtA with position/effect negotiation and crew mechanics.

**Fellowship (2016)**: Fantasy fellowship with overlapping playbooks and shared world-building.

**Urban Shadows (2015)**: Political urban fantasy with faction mechanics and debt system.

**Hundred Variations**: Hundreds of PbtA games across every conceivable genre—horror, romance, comedy, historical, sci-fi, etc.

## Design Intent and Purpose

PbtA Moves serve multiple design goals:

**Fiction First**: Mechanics emerge from fictional positioning rather than mechanical declarations. "What does your character do?" rather than "Which Move do you use?"

**Structured Conversation**: Moves structure improvised conversation, providing framework without rigid simulation.

**Fail Forward**: Failure (6-) doesn't stall narrative—it escalates situations, maintaining constant momentum.

**Player Agency**: Players always accomplish something, even on 7-9 partial successes. Total failure doesn't mean nothing happens.

**Three-Tier Drama**: 10+ (triumph), 7-9 (complication), 6- (trouble) creates nuanced outcomes beyond binary success/failure.

**Playbook Identity**: Unique Moves make each playbook play distinctly differently, encouraging diverse party composition.

**GM Guidance**: Explicit principles and Moves teach GMs how to facilitate rather than assuming knowledge.

**Emergent Story**: No predetermined plot. "Play to find out what happens."

## Strengths and Weaknesses

### Strengths

- **Fiction First**: Elegant integration of mechanics and narrative
- **Simple Core**: 2d6 + stat is immediately accessible
- **Nuanced Outcomes**: Three-tier results create interesting complications
- **Fail Forward**: Maintains narrative momentum constantly
- **Accessibility**: Easy to teach new players
- **Hackability**: Creative Commons licensing enables easy adaptation
- **Playbook Diversity**: Each playbook creates distinct play experience
- **GM Support**: Explicit principles demystify facilitation

### Weaknesses

- **Move Parsing**: Determining when fictional descriptions trigger Moves requires judgment
- **Mechanical Similarity**: Many PbtA games feel mechanically similar despite thematic differences
- **Limited Granularity**: 2d6 creates narrow probability range limiting differentiation
- **GM Burden**: "Making Moves" on player failures requires constant GM creativity
- **Optimization Resistant**: System actively resists tactical optimization some players enjoy
- **Fiction Police**: Can create arguments about whether fiction "really" triggered a Move
- **Oversaturation**: Hundreds of PbtA games create fatigue

## Impact on Game Design

PbtA's impact on RPG design rivals D&D's historical influence within the indie sphere:

**Genre Proliferation**: Demonstrated a single framework could support any genre through playbook and Move design.

**Design Accessibility**: Codified indie principles into replicable patterns, enabling new creators.

**Mainstream Influence**: Principles (fail forward, fiction first, advantage/disadvantage) influenced even traditional games.

**Creative Commons Success**: Showed open licensing builds creative communities rather than protecting market share.

**GM Pedagogy**: Explicit GM sections influenced industry-wide teaching approaches.

**Conversation-Based Play**: Legitimized structured conversation as viable alternative to simulation.

**Community Standards**: Safety tools, Session Zero, and consent practices spread through PbtA communities.

## Evolution Over Time

**2010**: Apocalypse World released, introducing the framework.

**2012-2014**: Early PbtA games (Dungeon World, Monsterhearts) demonstrated genre flexibility.

**2015-2017**: PbtA explosion with dozens of games exploring variations.

**2017-Present**: Advanced PbtA games (Blades in the Dark, Root, Ironsworn) heavily modify the framework while maintaining core principles.

**Contemporary**: PbtA is now an established design tradition with hundreds of implementations.

**Future**: The framework continues evolving, with designers finding new ways to adapt core principles to different genres and play styles.

## Related Mechanics

**Fate Core**: Aspect-based mechanics sharing fiction-first philosophy.

**Year Zero Engine**: Similar three-tier outcomes with different dice mechanics.

**Forged in the Dark**: John Harper's evolution of PbtA adding position/effect negotiation.

**d20 Advantage/Disadvantage**: D&D 5e's mechanic echoes PbtA's +1/-1 forward/ongoing.

**Narrative Dice** (FFG Star Wars): Success/failure with advantage/threat creating similar nuanced outcomes.

## Designer Commentary

D. Vincent Baker designed Moves to structure conversation rather than simulate reality. The 2d6 + stat creates bell curve probability (7-9 most likely) encouraging complications over clean success/failure.

Baker emphasized that Moves trigger from fiction, not player intent: "To do it, do it. Don't say you're doing it, don't roll to do it. Just do it. If that triggers a Move, then roll."

The 6- result represents GM opportunity rather than simple failure: "When they miss, you make a move. A real one—something that changes the situation dramatically."

The Creative Commons license was intentional: "I want people to hack this, adapt it, make it their own. That's how design culture advances."
