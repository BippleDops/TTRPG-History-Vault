# Moves

**First Appeared**: [[Apocalypse World]] (2010)
**Designer**: [[D. Vincent Baker]]
**Category**: Resolution Mechanic / Triggered Abilities

## Overview

Moves, created by Vincent Baker for Apocalypse World (2010), revolutionize RPG resolution by replacing generic skill checks with specific triggered abilities written in natural language ("When you [do thing in fiction], roll+stat..."), creating fiction-first gameplay where fictional actions trigger specific mechanical consequences rather than abstract "I roll Persuasion" divorcing mechanics from narrative, becoming defining characteristic of Powered by the Apocalypse games and influencing how narrative RPGs structure resolution through fiction-to-mechanics rather than mechanics-to-fiction flow.

**Structure**: "When you [fictional trigger], [mechanical procedure]"

**Example** (Act Under Fire):
"When you act under fire, roll+cool. On 10+, you do it. On 7-9, you hesitate, flinch, or stall: the MC will offer you a worse outcome, a hard bargain, or an ugly choice. On a miss, be prepared for the worst."

**Revolutionary Approach**: Instead of generic "skill check" covering any use of Persuasion/Stealth/Combat, Moves are specific: "Seduce or Manipulate Someone" is different Move than "Go Aggro" despite both involving social interaction. The specificity creates mechanical distinction for different fictional approaches.

**Fiction-First Flow**: Player describes what character does → if it matches Move trigger → roll that Move. Mechanics follow from fiction rather than declaring mechanic then justifying fictionally.

**Massive Influence**: Moves became PbtA's defining characteristic. Every PbtA game (hundreds) uses Moves. The concept influenced resolution design broadly, even in non-PbtA games, demonstrating how natural-language fictional triggers could replace abstract skill lists.

## Historical Context

Traditional RPG resolution used skills:
- **Generic Skills**: Persuasion, Stealth, Perception, etc.
- **Abstract Application**: Roll skill, apply to situation
- **Mechanics-First**: Declare "I roll Persuasion" then justify how
- **Uniform Outcomes**: Same skill used for vastly different fictional actions

This created disconnect: mechanically identical rolls (Persuasion +5 vs DC 15) could represent seducing lover, interrogating prisoner, negotiating treaty, or conning merchant—vastly different fictional actions with identical mechanical treatment.

Vincent Baker, designing Apocalypse World, wanted:
- **Fiction-First**: Describe action, then mechanics follow
- **Specific Consequences**: Different actions have different mechanical outcomes
- **Natural Language**: Rules readable as English sentences
- **Fictional Triggers**: Mechanics engage when specific fictional events occur

The solution: Moves—abilities triggered by fictional circumstances with specific mechanical outcomes.

**Design Process**: Baker developed Moves iteratively:
- Initial playtests had fewer, broader Moves
- Refinement split broad Moves into specific ones
- Each Move tuned to create specific fictional outcomes
- Final AW (2010) had ~dozen Basic Moves plus playbook-specific ones

**Influence from**:
- Fiction-first gaming discussions on The Forge
- Specific abilities replacing generic skills (narrativist games)
- Natural language programming (clear conditional statements)

The 2010 Apocalypse World release introduced Moves to gaming. Reactions varied:
- **Narrativists**: Immediately recognized brilliance—fiction triggering mechanics perfectly
- **Traditional gamers**: Often confused—"why can't I just roll Persuade?"
- **Designers**: Saw potential for fiction-mechanics integration

As PbtA proliferated (Dungeon World 2012, Monsterhearts 2012, Monster of the Week 2015, Masks 2016, hundreds more), Moves became expected element. Each game created genre-appropriate Moves: Dungeon World had "Hack and Slash" and "Spout Lore"; Monsterhearts had "Turn Someone On" and "Lash Out Physically"; Masks had "Directly Engage a Threat" and "Pierce the Mask."

By mid-2010s, Moves had become fundamental PbtA characteristic alongside playbooks and 2d6+stat resolution. The concept influenced non-PbtA games adopting fiction-triggered abilities, natural language rules, and specific-rather-than-generic resolution.

## Mechanical Framework

### Move Anatomy

**Trigger**: "When you [fictional action]..."
- Describes specific fictional circumstance
- Must occur in fiction to trigger Move
- Can't trigger mechanically without fictional justification

**Examples**:
- "When you go aggro on someone..." (threatening violence)
- "When you act under fire..." (doing something risky)
- "When you seduce or manipulate someone..." (using charm/leverage)

**Resolution**: "...roll+stat"
- Usually 2d6+relevant stat
- Occasionally other mechanics (spending resources, making choices)

**Outcomes**: Result-based consequences
- **10+**: Strong success—achieve goal without cost
- **7-9**: Partial success—achieve goal WITH complication/cost
- **6-**: Failure—don't achieve goal AND face consequences

**Move-Specific Outcomes**: Each Move has specific results, not generic success/failure

**Example** (Apocalypse World's "Seduce or Manipulate"):
- 10+: They do what you want
- 7-9: They do it if you meet condition (promise, evidence, immediate action)
- 6-: They might do it but want concrete assurance first, or won't do it at all

### Basic Moves vs. Playbook Moves

**Basic Moves**: Everyone can use (typically 6-12 per game)
- Available to all characters
- Cover common actions (fighting, perception, social interaction)
- Genre-defining (what everyone in this setting does)

**Playbook Moves**: Specific to character archetype
- Only available to that playbook
- Create mechanical distinction between archetypes
- Embody archetype's fictional role mechanically

**Example** (Monsterhearts):
- **Basic Move**: "Turn Someone On" (everyone can attempt seduction)
- **Playbook Move** (Vampire's "Cold as Ice"): Gain bonuses when emotionally distant—unique to Vampire

### When Moves Don't Trigger

If fictional action doesn't match Move trigger, don't roll that Move:
- Casual conversation doesn't trigger "Seduce or Manipulate" (no leverage/charm being used)
- Walking down street doesn't trigger "Act Under Fire" (no danger)
- Attacking sleeping enemy might not trigger combat Move (no opposition)

This keeps resolution tied to fiction—mechanics engage when fictional circumstances match, not arbitrarily.

### GM Moves

Many PbtA games give GM their own Moves:
- "Separate them"
- "Announce future badness"
- "Make them pay a price"
- "Turn their Move back on them"

These structure GM responses, especially on player failures (6-).

### Custom Moves

Games often include situation-specific Moves:
- Rituals or special circumstances
- Unique challenges
- Setting-specific actions

**Example** (Dungeon World):
"When you enter the cursed forest..." (custom Move for specific location)

## Variations Across PbtA

Each PbtA game creates genre-appropriate Moves:

**Dungeon World**: Fantasy adventuring
- Hack and Slash (melee combat)
- Volley (ranged combat)
- Spout Lore (knowledge)
- Discern Realities (perception)

**Monsterhearts**: Teen supernatural drama
- Turn Someone On (seduction)
- Shut Someone Down (social attack)
- Lash Out Physically (violence)
- Run Away (fleeing)

**Masks**: Teen superheroes
- Directly Engage a Threat (superhero combat)
- Pierce the Mask (understanding others)
- Comfort or Support (emotional support)
- Provoke Someone (getting reaction)

**Monster of the Week**: Supernatural investigation
- Investigate a Mystery (research)
- Kick Some Ass (fighting monsters)
- Protect Someone (defending others)
- Manipulate Someone (social engineering)

Each game's Moves define genre and expected actions.

## Design Philosophy

Moves embody principles:

**Fiction-First**: Fictional action triggers mechanics

**Natural Language**: Rules readable as English

**Specific Over Generic**: Tailored Moves for specific actions

**Mechanical Consequences**: Each Move has unique outcomes

**Player-Facing**: Players roll; GM responds to results

**Fail Forward**: 6- isn't "nothing happens"—it's "something bad happens"

**Partial Success**: 7-9 creates interesting complications

## Cultural Impact

**PbtA Definition**: Moves alongside playbooks define Powered by the Apocalypse

**Fiction-First Gaming**: Demonstrated fiction-to-mechanics flow viability

**Natural Language Rules**: Influenced how designers write mechanics

**Specific Resolution**: Replaced generic skill checks in narrative gaming

**Fail Forward**: 6- outcomes always advance story

**Design Vocabulary**: "Moves" became standard indie term

**Educational**: Taught as example of fiction-mechanics integration

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Elegant fiction-first resolution
- Specific outcomes create genre feel
- Natural language increases accessibility
- Fail forward keeps momentum
- 7-9 partial successes create interesting drama
- Mechanically diverse through different Moves

**Criticism**:
- Learning which Move applies can confuse newcomers
- Some feel constrained by specific Moves
- Traditional gamers want generic skills
- Move proliferation (some games have 50+ Moves)
- "Move hunting" - players searching for applicable Move instead of describing action

## Legacy and Influence

**PbtA Standard**: Mandatory element of Powered by the Apocalypse

**Fiction-First Design**: Influenced how narrative games handle resolution

**Natural Language**: Changed how rules are written

**Triggered Abilities**: Concept spread beyond PbtA

**Design Education**: Studied as resolution design innovation

**Forged in the Dark**: Adapted as Actions with Position/Effect

**Ongoing Evolution**: Designers continue refining Move design
