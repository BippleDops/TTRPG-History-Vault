# Time Tracking

**First Appeared**: Original D&D (1974)
**Category**: Game Structure / Resource Management

## Overview

Time Tracking provides structured methods for measuring in-game time passage—combat rounds (6 seconds), exploration turns (10 minutes), travel time (hours/days), downtime (weeks/months)—creating resource management through time-sensitive challenges (burning torches, wandering monsters, spell durations, poison progression), pacing tools for GMs, and mechanical framework distinguishing quick tactical combat from deliberate exploration from extended travel, with OSR games emphasizing strict time-keeping while modern games often abstracting or eliminating tracking for pacing flexibility.

**Time Scales**:
- **Combat Round**: 6 seconds (one action)
- **Exploration Turn**: 10 minutes (searching room, checking for traps)
- **Travel**: Hours/days (overland movement)
- **Downtime**: Weeks/months (between adventures)

**Resource Management**: Torches burn, spells expire, encounters threaten

**Wandering Monsters**: Rolled per time unit, punishing slow cautious play

**OSR Emphasis**: Strict time-keeping creating pressure

**Modern Trend**: Often abstracted or ignored for pacing

## Time Structures

### Combat Rounds (6 seconds)

**Initiative**: Determining action order

**Actions**: What's possible in 6 seconds

**Spell Durations**: Measured in rounds

**Ongoing Effects**: Tracking timed conditions

**Purpose**: Tactical structure for combat

### Exploration Turns (10 minutes)

**Dungeon Exploration**: Each room/action = 1 turn

**Activities Per Turn**:
- Search room thoroughly
- Check for traps
- Pick lock
- Listen at door

**Wandering Monsters**: Roll per turn (pressure against slow play)

**Torch Duration**: Burns X turns

**Spell Duration**: Some spells measured in turns

**Purpose**: Creating time pressure in dungeons

### Travel Time

**Overland Movement**: Miles per day based on terrain

**Random Encounters**: Rolled per day/hex

**Rations**: Days of food

**Weather**: Days of travel

**Purpose**: Long-distance movement framework

### Downtime

**Between Adventures**: Weeks/months passing

**Activities**: Research, crafting, carousing, training

**Calendar**: Campaign timeline

**Seasonal**: Years passing in long campaigns

## Design Philosophy

**Time as Resource**: Tracking creates pressure

**Wandering Monsters**: Punishment for slow cautious play

**Pacing Tool**: GM controls advancement

**Resource Drain**: Torches, food, spell durations

**Realism**: Simulating time passage

## OSR Strict Time-Keeping

**Every Action**: Tracked in exploration turns

**Wandering Monster Checks**: Regular pressure

**Resource Depletion**: Torches/food management

**No Dithering**: Time pressure forces decisions

**Tension**: Creates urgency

## Modern Abstracting

**Handwave**: "You search the dungeon" without turn-by-turn

**Narrative Pacing**: Time as story needs dictate

**Reduced Pressure**: Less wandering monster threat

**Player Comfort**: Not punishing thorough exploration

**Flexibility**: Adjusting to maintain fun

## Reception

**Strict Tracking Advocates**: Creates tension, resource management, pacing

**Abstract Supporters**: Prefer story flow over bookkeeping

**Middle Ground**: Track when matters, abstract when doesn't

## Legacy

Remains debated. OSR emphasizes strict tracking. Mainstream often abstracts. Tool available when desired, skippable when not. Demonstrates time-pressure varying by creative agenda.

## Notes

- Original D&D structured time units
- OSR strict tracking revival
- Modern games often abstracting
- Design philosophy differences
- Time-as-resource vs. narrative-flow debate
