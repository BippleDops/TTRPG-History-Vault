# Playbooks

**First Appeared**: [[Apocalypse World]] (2010)
**Designer**: [[D. Vincent Baker]]
**Category**: Character Creation / Archetype Framework

## Overview

Playbooks, pioneered by Vincent Baker in Apocalypse World (2010), revolutionize character creation by providing complete character sheets pre-populated with archetype-specific Moves (abilities), stats, advancement tracks, and thematic framing—players choose playbook (The Hardholder, The Battlebabe, The Brainer, etc.), customize details, and begin playing immediately with mechanically distinct character whose abilities embody their fictional archetype, replacing traditional point-buy or class-level systems with integrated packages defining both mechanical capabilities and narrative roles.

**Character Sheet AS Playbook**: Revolutionary approach—each archetype gets unique physical sheet containing everything for that character type. The Gunlugger's playbook has Gunlugger-specific moves, stats, gear; The Angel's playbook has healing moves and medical supplies. This modular approach creates mechanical distinction between archetypes while maintaining system coherence.

**Moves Define Characters**: Unlike classes providing numerical bonuses, playbooks grant unique Moves—specific mechanical abilities triggering in fictional circumstances. The Gunlugger has "Battlehardened" (ignore fear/shock), The Angel has "Healing Touch" (supernatural healing). These moves create distinctive playstyles mechanically, not just narratively.

**Massive Influence**: Playbooks became defining PbtA characteristic, appearing in every Powered by the Apocalypse game and influencing character creation across narrative gaming. The concept of archetype-as-complete-package-with-unique-abilities became standard approach in story games, replacing traditional class-as-numerical-modifier thinking.

## Historical Context

Traditional RPG character creation typically involved:
- **Point Buy**: Distributing points across generic stats (GURPS, Champions)
- **Class Levels**: Choosing class providing abilities at levels (D&D)
- **Lifepaths**: Sequential choices building character (Traveller, Burning Wheel)
- **Templates**: Pre-made concepts customizable (Star Wars d6)

These approaches often created:
- **Lengthy Creation**: Hours building characters
- **Optimization Focus**: Min-maxing rather than concept
- **Mechanical Sameness**: Characters differentiated more by numbers than capabilities
- **Front-Loaded Decisions**: Choices made before understanding game

Vincent Baker, designing Apocalypse World, wanted:
- **Fast Creation**: Begin playing quickly
- **Mechanical Distinction**: Archetypes playing differently mechanically
- **Fiction-Mechanic Integration**: Abilities embodying fictional roles
- **Immediate Understanding**: See all character options on single sheet

The solution: create separate complete character sheets for each archetype—playbooks—containing everything needed. Choose playbook, fill in name/details, select options, start playing. Takes 15 minutes.

The term "playbook" drew from sports—coach's playbook contains team's strategies and plays. Similarly, RPG playbook contains character's "plays" (Moves they can make). This framing helped communicate the concept: your playbook is your character's toolkit.

Apocalypse World (2010) launched with 11 playbooks: The Angel, The Battlebabe, The Brainer, The Chopper, The Driver, The Gunlugger, The Hardholder, The Hocus, The Operator, The Savvyhead, The Skinner. Each mechanically distinct with unique Moves, creating 11 different play experiences.

The innovation's success was immediate. Players loved:
- Choosing archetype and immediately understanding capabilities
- Mechanical distinction between character types
- Integrated character sheets requiring no cross-referencing
- Thematic coherence between fiction and mechanics

Every subsequent PbtA game adopted playbooks as core structure. Dungeon World (2012) adapted them for fantasy classes. Monsterhearts (2012) used them for teen monster archetypes. Monster of the Week (2015) for investigator types. Masks (2016) for teen superhero emotional roles. The framework proved infinitely adaptable.

## Mechanical Framework

### Playbook Structure

Typical playbook contains:

**Archetype Name and Description**: Who this character is

**Look Options**: Choose appearance details (eyes, clothes, body, etc.)

**Stats**: Rating for basic attributes (typically -1 to +3)

**Basic Moves**: Everyone has these (from core rules)

**Playbook Moves**: Unique abilities only this archetype gets
- Start with 1-3
- Gain more through advancement
- Each mechanically distinct

**Special Move**: Unique mechanic for this archetype (often triggered by specific circumstances)

**Gear**: Starting equipment appropriate to archetype

**Hx/Bonds**: Starting relationships to other characters

**Advancement**: How this playbook improves

**Harm/Stress**: Health tracking

**Custom Elements**: Playbook-specific unique mechanics (Holds for Hardholder, Followers for Hocus, Workspace for Savvyhead)

### Creating Character from Playbook

1. **Choose Playbook**: Pick archetype matching desired role
2. **Choose Look**: Select appearance options
3. **Assign Stats**: Distribute ratings using playbook's array
4. **Choose Starting Moves**: Select from playbook's move list
5. **Detail Gear**: Pick equipment from options
6. **Establish Relationships**: Set Hx/Bonds with other PCs
7. **Name Character**: Done—takes 10-20 minutes

### Playbook Moves

Moves are triggered abilities:

**Trigger**: "When you [do thing in fiction]..."
**Resolution**: ...roll+stat or specific mechanics
**Outcomes**: Different results based on roll (usually 10+, 7-9, 6-)

**Example** (Gunlugger's "Battlehardened"):
- "When you act under fire, roll+hard instead of +cool"
- Triggers when doing dangerous things
- Uses different stat (hard instead of cool)
- Makes Gunlugger better at facing danger

**Mechanical Distinction**: Each playbook's moves create unique capabilities, making archetypes play differently mechanically not just narratively.

### Advancement

Playbooks include advancement tracks:
- Mark experience through play
- At thresholds, choose improvements:
  - +1 to stat
  - New playbook move
  - New gear/resources
  - Change to another playbook (rare)

Advancement is playbook-specific—Hardholder advancement includes "improve your Hold," Gunlugger includes "get badass weapon."

### Multi-Classing and Playbook Moves

Some games allow:
- Taking moves from other playbooks (with restrictions)
- Starting with hybrid concepts
- Changing playbooks during play (character evolution)

This creates customization while maintaining playbook structure.

## Playbook Philosophy

Playbooks embody design principles:

**Fiction-First**: Archetype defines fictional role; mechanics follow

**Mechanical Distinction**: Different playbooks genuinely play differently

**Integrated Sheets**: Everything needed on one document

**Quick Creation**: Choose and play, minimal fiddling

**Thematic Coherence**: Mechanics reflect archetype's narrative role

**Advancement Integration**: Improvement tracks built into playbook

**No Cross-Reference**: Self-contained character information

## Cultural Impact

**PbtA Definition**: Playbooks became defining PbtA characteristic alongside Moves

**Character Creation Revolution**: Changed how narrative games approach archetypes

**Modular Design**: Influenced character sheet as modular component thinking

**Quick Play**: Enabled faster start times

**Mechanical Diversity**: Showed archetypes could be mechanically not just narratively distinct

**Design Vocabulary**: "Playbook" became standard term in indie design

**Educational**: Used teaching character design in game design courses

## Comparison to Traditional Classes

### D&D-Style Classes

**Levels**: Abilities gained at level milestones

**Generic Sheets**: Same character sheet for all classes

**Numerical Differences**: Classes differentiated by hit dice, attack bonuses, saves

**Slow Creation**: Building character requires cross-referencing

**Multiclassing Complexity**: Combining classes creates calculation complexity

### Playbooks

**Moves**: Abilities are Moves triggered by fiction

**Unique Sheets**: Each playbook is distinct document

**Mechanical Differences**: Playbooks have unique Moves and mechanics

**Fast Creation**: Choose playbook, fill in, done

**Changing Playbooks**: Rare but simple when happens

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Fast character creation
- Mechanical distinction between archetypes
- Integrated self-contained sheets
- Thematic-mechanical coherence
- Enables immediate play
- Easy to understand at glance

**Criticism**:
- Less customization than point-buy systems
- Archetypes can feel restrictive
- Changing playbooks mid-campaign awkward
- "Locked into" concept some dislike
- Mechanical sameness within playbook
- Can feel like choosing from menu rather than creating unique character

## Variations Across PbtA

**Dungeon World**: Traditional fantasy classes (Fighter, Wizard, Thief)

**Monsterhearts**: Teen monster types (Vampire, Werewolf, Ghost, Fae)

**Masks**: Emotional roles (The Bull, The Delinquent, The Doomed)

**Urban Shadows**: Factional types across four groups

**The Sprawl**: Cyberpunk professions (Killer, Hacker, Driver)

**Night Witches**: Soviet military roles (Pilot, Navigator)

Each game adapts playbook concept to setting/themes.

## Legacy and Influence

Playbooks became essential PbtA element and influenced character creation broadly:

**PbtA Standard**: Every PbtA game uses playbooks

**Narrative Gaming**: Archetype-sheet approach spread beyond PbtA

**Quick Play Culture**: Contributed to faster-starting gaming

**Mechanical Diversity**: Raised expectations for archetypes playing differently

**Design Tool**: Became standard approach for archetype-based games

**Educational**: Studied as elegant character creation solution
