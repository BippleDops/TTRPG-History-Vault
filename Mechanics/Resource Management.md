# Resource Management

## Overview

Resource management mechanics govern how characters access and expend their capabilities across time. These systems transform "what can my character do?" from a static question into a dynamic strategic challenge: "What can I do now, given what I've already done and what I might need to do later?" This temporal dimension of capability creates crucial gameplay loops around conservation, expenditure, and recovery.

At its foundation, resource management introduces scarcity and consequence to character abilities. Without resource limitations, optimal play becomes simple: always use your most powerful abilities. Resources create genuine decisions: spending powerful abilities now solves immediate problems but may leave you vulnerable later; conserving resources provides future security but risks failure in present challenges. These trade-offs generate strategic depth and create narrative rhythm through escalation and exhaustion.

Resource systems take many forms: spell slots that limit spellcasting frequency, hit points that track damage capacity, consumable items with limited uses, daily or encounter-based ability pools, depletable equipment, and abstract metacurrencies. Each type creates different strategic considerations and supports different gameplay styles. Some resources replenish quickly (per-encounter abilities), others slowly (long rest recovery), and some never regenerate within campaigns (consumable items, permanent character resources).

The relationship between resources and rest mechanics establishes the game's adventuring rhythm: how many encounters can characters handle between rests? How dangerous is resource depletion? What activities replenish resources? These questions determine whether games feature attrition-based dungeon crawling where resource management is central challenge, or episodic encounters where characters approach each fight relatively fresh.

Modern game design increasingly recognizes resource management as a tunable parameter that dramatically affects gameplay experience. Generous resource systems support high-action campaigns where characters frequently use impressive abilities. Scarce resources create tension and force meaningful choices. Understanding these implications helps designers calibrate resource systems to desired gameplay and helps players recognize how resource management shapes their strategic options.

## Historical Development

The evolution of resource management in TTRPGs reflects designers' growing understanding of how resource scarcity shapes strategic gameplay, pacing, and player experience.

**Original D&D (1974)** established resource management as central gameplay pillar through the [[Vancian Magic System]]. Spellcasters prepared specific spells in limited slots, expending them with each casting. This created stark strategic choices: use powerful spells now and risk later helplessness, or conserve power and potentially fail current challenges. Hit points functioned as damage resource, healing items (potions) as consumables, and equipment as depletable resource (torches burning out, rations consumed).

The early D&D resource system created distinctive gameplay rhythm: parties ventured into dungeons, expended resources (spells, hit points, healing, equipment) across multiple encounters, then retreated to rest and replenish. The attrition-based design made resource management strategically central—success required judging how much further you could push before mandatory retreat.

**AD&D (1977-1989)** refined these resource systems without fundamentally changing them. Spell slot progression became more structured, magic item charges provided additional expendable resources, and resource management remained core challenge. The expectation was that adventuring days featured multiple encounters with significant resource attrition between rests, rewarding careful resource allocation.

**Warhammer Fantasy Roleplay (1986)** introduced resource management through limited healing supplies, ammunition tracking, and the corruption/mutation system where dark magic use permanently altered characters. These non-renewable resources created long-term consequences beyond immediate encounters.

**D&D 3rd Edition (2000)** systematized resource management extensively. The clear delineation of encounter resources (0/day abilities), daily resources (spell slots, uses per day), and permanent resources (XP costs, item charges) made resource economy transparent. The 15-minute adventuring day problem emerged: spellcasters could expend all daily resources in one encounter, then demand long rest, bypassing intended attrition gameplay. This revealed the challenge of balancing per-day resources with expected encounters per rest.

**D&D 3.5 (2003)** introduced Reserve feats and other mechanics attempting to give spellcasters capabilities that didn't deplete, addressing the problem of resource-depleted characters having nothing interesting to do. The edition also experimented with alternative resource systems through variant classes using power points (Psionics) or invocations (Warlocks with at-will abilities).

**D&D 4th Edition (2008)** revolutionized resource management through its power structure: at-will powers (usable constantly), encounter powers (once per fight), and daily powers (once per long rest). This three-tier system ensured characters always had interesting tactical options (at-wills) while creating meaningful decisions about when to use limited resources (encounter and daily powers). Healing surges limited daily healing capacity, creating resource management around sustainability. The system successfully reduced disparity between fresh and depleted characters while maintaining resource decisions.

Action Points provided per-encounter dramatic moments, while ritual casting offered unlimited but time-consuming magic outside resource economy. The design elegantly solved many resource management problems by ensuring base capability (at-wills) while adding resource-gated exceptional capability (encounter/daily powers).

**Dungeon World (2012)** largely eliminated resource management for spells—spellcasters could attempt any spell thematically appropriate, with fictional positioning and roll results determining success and complications. This removed resource tracking in favor of narrative-first magic, fundamentally different approach from Vancian systems.

**13th Age (2013)** refined 4e's insights while adding flexibility. Spells used flexible frequency system (at-will, per-battle, daily, recharge on rolls) allowing per-spell tuning. Recoveries (limited self-healing resource) created health economy without requiring dedicated healers. The system maintained resource decisions while reducing bookkeeping.

**Blades in the Dark (2017)** introduced elegant abstraction: characters had stress (resource spent to resist consequences, push abilities, or avoid trauma) and load (equipment abstraction where you declare what you brought when needed, limited by load rating). The stress economy created interesting decisions—taking stress now prevents consequences but accumulates toward trauma—while load system handled equipment resource management without tedious pre-mission inventory.

**Torchbearer (2013)** by [[Thor Olavsrud]] and [[Luke Crane]] made resource management absolutely central through its grind mechanics. Every action potentially added conditions (Hungry, Thirsty, Exhausted, Angry, Afraid, Injured), forcing characters to spend resources (food, water, rest) to recover. Light sources, equipment degradation, and inventory limits created constant resource pressure. The game was explicitly about resource management—the dungeon as resource depletion engine.

**The One Ring (2011)** introduced Hope as expendable resource for heroic actions and resistance to corruption, while Shadow accumulated as spiritual damage. The tension between expending Hope for success and preserving it to avoid corruption created thematic resource management aligned with Tolkien's philosophy.

**Forbidden Lands (2018)** featured resource management through willpower (magical resource), consumables (food, water, arrows), and settlement building (permanent resource investment). The survival-hexcrawl design made resource scarcity central challenge.

Contemporary designs increasingly treat resource systems as tunable parameters: designers choose resource abundance/scarcity to create desired gameplay experiences, from resource-rich superheroic games to desperate survival games where every resource matters.

## How It Works

Resource management systems operate through several interconnected mechanisms determining what resources exist, how they're expended, and how they regenerate:

### Resource Types

**Renewable Per-Encounter Resources**: Reset after each combat or significant challenge. Ensure characters can contribute meaningfully in every encounter without long-term depletion.

Example: **D&D 4e Encounter Powers** - Wizard has Lightning Bolt (encounter power). Uses it in first combat, it's gone. After five-minute short rest following combat, Lightning Bolt recharges. Character has access to it again in next combat. System ensures characters never face encounters without interesting options.

Example: **13th Age Flexible Attacks** - Some abilities recharge on specific d6 rolls during short rests. After combat, roll d6; on 4+, ability recharges. Creates some uncertainty but ensures high recharge rate.

**Daily Resources**: Regenerate after long rest (usually 8 hours or overnight). Create strategic arc across adventuring day—how to allocate limited daily resources across expected encounters?

Example: **D&D 5e Spell Slots** - Wizard has four 1st-level slots, three 2nd-level, three 3rd-level. Each spell cast expends slot. All slots return after long rest. Wizard must judge how many encounters remain and allocate slots accordingly—use powerful 3rd-level slot now or save it?

Example: **13th Age Recoveries** - Limited healing resource (usually 8-10) that can be spent during rests or triggered by abilities. Each recovery lets you roll healing dice. Running low on recoveries means reduced sustainability, but increments (small number) means each one is strategically significant.

**Per-Session Resources**: Regenerate between play sessions, creating session-level strategic arc.

Example: **Blades in the Dark Stress** - Stress accumulates during scores, can be cleared through downtime activities between missions. Creates strategic questions about how much stress to accept during current score and whether to spend downtime clearing stress versus other activities.

**Campaign Resources**: Don't regenerate or regenerate very slowly. Create long-term strategic considerations and permanent trade-offs.

Example: **D&D 3.5 XP Costs** - Some powerful spells or magic item creation required spending XP, permanently reducing character advancement. Created genuine high-stakes decisions—is this benefit worth permanent power sacrifice?

Example: **Torchbearer Inventory Slots** - Characters have limited carrying capacity. Bringing more adventuring gear means less loot capacity. Consumables used don't regenerate without purchasing/finding replacements. Equipment decisions have lasting consequences.

**Consumable Resources**: Finite items that deplete with use, requiring resupply or acquisition.

Example: **Arrows, Rations, Torches** - Traditional dungeon-crawling consumables. Each arrow fired is one fewer arrow available. Must track ammunition and resupply in towns. Creates strategic decisions about when to use ranged attacks versus conserving arrows.

Example: **Healing Potions** - Limited-use healing items. Each potion consumed is gone until you acquire more. Spending potion now provides immediate benefit but removes future option.

### Expenditure Mechanics

**Fixed Costs**: Using ability consumes predetermined resource amount.

Example: **D&D 5e Spell Slots** - Fireball costs one 3rd-level spell slot. Cost is fixed and transparent, allowing straightforward resource planning.

**Variable Costs**: Resource expenditure scales with desired effect or chosen options.

Example: **Fate Points** - Invoking aspects costs fate points, with number spent affecting effectiveness. Players decide how much to invest based on situation importance.

Example: **D&D 3.5 Psionics** - Power points could be spent flexibly to augment psionic powers. Psion could spend minimum points for basic effect or invest more for enhanced power, creating dynamic resource allocation.

**Triggered Costs**: Resources expended in response to situations rather than proactively.

Example: **Blades in the Dark Resistance Rolls** - When consequence would occur, spend stress to resist it. Creates reactive resource decisions: accept consequence or pay stress to avoid it?

**Opportunity Costs**: Using one resource precludes using others; expenditure is about choice rather than depletion.

Example: **D&D 5e Bonus Actions** - Only one bonus action per turn. Using Healing Word (bonus action spell) means you can't use other bonus action abilities. Resource is "which bonus action" not "how many."

### Regeneration Mechanics

**Long Rest Recovery**: Extended rest period (usually 8 hours) fully or substantially replenishes resources. Creates multi-encounter strategic arc—how far can you push between long rests?

Example: **D&D 5e Long Rest** - Regain all HP, all spell slots, half your Hit Dice. Takes 8 hours. Characters can typically have one per 24-hour period. Defines daily resource budget.

**Short Rest Recovery**: Brief rest period (minutes to an hour) partially replenishes resources. Provides mid-adventuring-day recovery option without full reset.

Example: **D&D 5e Short Rest** - Takes 1 hour, lets you spend Hit Dice to regain HP, recharges some class features (Warlock slots, Fighter's Action Surge, etc.). Provides breathing room without ending adventuring day.

Example: **D&D 4e Short Rest** - 5 minutes, regains all encounter powers. Very generous recovery encouraging continued adventuring.

**Automatic Recharge**: Resources regenerate automatically at defined intervals without requiring rest actions.

Example: **13th Age Recharge Mechanics** - Some abilities recharge after each battle automatically. Others roll for recharge (succeed on 6, or 16+, etc.), creating uncertainty but generally reliable recovery.

Example: **Modern D&D 5e Short Rest Alternative Rules** - Some tables use "short rest after each encounter" to eliminate rest timing gameplay and focus on per-encounter resource management.

**Activity-Based Regeneration**: Resources replenish through specific in-fiction activities.

Example: **Blades in the Dark Downtime** - Stress cleared through downtime activities (Indulge Vice, Long-Term Project focused on relaxation). Recovery requires taking specific actions with their own costs and complications.

Example: **Torchbearer Camp Actions** - Conditions recover through camp actions (eat to clear Hungry, drink to clear Thirsty, sleep to clear Exhausted). Camp itself requires resources (rations, water, safe location), creating nested resource economy.

**Story-Based Regeneration**: Resources return at dramatically appropriate moments or milestones rather than mechanical timing.

Example: **Fate Refresh** - Fate points refresh at beginning of each session. Simple story-based timing rather than in-fiction rest periods.

### Resource Scarcity and Abundance

The fundamental scarcity of resources determines how much they shape strategic gameplay:

**Abundant Resources**: Generous resource pools, easy recovery, frequent regeneration. Characters can use abilities freely without excessive conservation. Supports high-action gameplay where impressive abilities appear regularly.

Example: **D&D 4e** - At-will powers provide unlimited baseline capability, encounter powers recharge every fight (with quick short rests), healing surges provide generous healing, action points create dramatic moments. Resource management exists but isn't restrictive—characters always have interesting options.

**Moderate Resources**: Sufficient resources for multiple encounters but requires planning and allocation. Typical adventuring day features meaningful resource decisions without excessive scarcity.

Example: **D&D 5e** - Spell slots, class abilities, and healing resources support 6-8 medium encounters per long rest according to design intent. Characters must make real decisions about resource expenditure but aren't constantly at edge of depletion.

**Scarce Resources**: Limited resources create constant pressure. Resource conservation becomes central strategy; depletion is constant threat.

Example: **Torchbearer** - Every action potentially costs condition or equipment use. Rations limited, light sources burn out, conditions accumulate relentlessly. Resource management is primary gameplay challenge, with every decision having resource implications.

Example: **OSR dungeon crawling** - Limited healing, spell slots recovering only at safe havens, encumbrance limits, light and food scarcity. Pushing deeper means increasing vulnerability; retreat means lost progress. Resource pressure drives strategic decision-making.

## Design Philosophy

Resource management design reflects fundamental choices about gameplay focus, strategic depth, power fantasy, and session structure.

**Strategic Depth Through Scarcity**: Resource limitations create meaningful decisions. Abundant resources reduce strategic depth—if you can always use optimal abilities, choices become obvious. Scarcity forces trade-offs: spending powerful abilities now creates immediate advantage but future vulnerability. This temporal strategy—present versus future allocation—adds depth beyond immediate tactical choices.

However, excessive scarcity frustrates players who can't access interesting capabilities or feel constantly depleted. Good design finds the balance: enough scarcity to create decisions without creating frustration.

**The Adventuring Day Problem**: Resource systems must align with expected play patterns. D&D 3.5's "15-minute adventuring day" emerged from mismatch: daily resources balanced for 4-6 encounters, but spellcasters could expend everything in one encounter, pressuring for long rest. If rest is readily available, players optimize by using all resources immediately then resting.

Solutions include: making rest difficult or risky (time pressure, random encounters during rest), using per-encounter resources (4e, 13th Age), generous recovery (5e short rests, healing), or embracing varied encounter pacing. Each approach creates different gameplay rhythm.

**Power Fantasy vs. Strategic Challenge**: Generous resources support power fantasy—characters frequently use impressive abilities and feel capable. D&D 4e's design philosophy ensured players always had cool options; resource management existed but didn't gate basic competence.

Scarce resources emphasize strategic challenge over power fantasy. Torchbearer characters are constantly resource-deprived, creating tense gameplay about survival and hard choices. Neither is inherently better; they serve different game goals.

**Class Balance Through Resource Systems**: Different resource systems can balance classes. D&D 5e Warlocks have fewer spell slots than Wizards (2-4 vs. 20+) but slots recharge on short rests. In campaigns with few short rests, Wizards dominate; with many short rests, Warlocks keep pace. Resource design affects class balance dramatically.

Similarly, 4e's power structure ensured all classes used same at-will/encounter/daily framework, preventing imbalance between resource-dependent classes (spellcasters) and resource-independent classes (fighters).

**Bookkeeping vs. Strategic Depth**: Detailed resource tracking creates authenticity and strategic depth but increases bookkeeping overhead. Tracking individual arrows, rations, and torch durations adds tactical considerations but can slow play. Modern designs often abstract resources (Blades in the Dark load system lets you declare equipment when needed within load rating) to maintain strategic considerations without tedious accounting.

**Resource Management as Genre Emulation**: Resource systems communicate genre and theme. Survival horror games use scarce resources to create tension and vulnerability. Superhero games use generous resources to enable frequent impressive actions. The resource economy tells players what kind of game this is mechanically, not just narratively.

## Variations Across Systems

**D&D 5e Spell Slots and Rests**: Daily spell slots regain on long rest (8 hours), short rests (1 hour) let you spend Hit Dice for healing and recharge some class features. System supports 6-8 medium encounters per long rest theoretically, though actual play varies wildly by table. Generous healing and recovery compared to earlier editions, but still features meaningful resource decisions. The system works well when tables embrace multiple encounters between long rests; struggles when rests are too frequent.

**D&D 4th Edition Powers**: Three-tier system: at-will powers (unlimited use), encounter powers (once per combat, recharge on short rest), daily powers (once per long rest). Healing surges limit daily healing capacity. System ensures characters always have baseline competence (at-wills) while gating exceptional abilities (dailies). Short rests are 5 minutes, encouraging continued adventuring. Successfully balanced resource management with consistent capability.

**13th Age Incremental Advance**: Flexible power frequencies (at-will, per-battle, daily, recharge) with recoveries as healing resource. Quick rest (equivalent to short rest) allows recovery spending and some power recharge; full heal-up (long rest equivalent) restores everything. Incremental Advance system gives small benefits toward next level after each battle (no XP tracking), creating session-by-session advancement that feels like progression even between levels. Resource management balanced between strategic decisions and accessibility.

**Blades in the Dark Stress and Load**: Stress spent to resist consequences, use special abilities, or avoid trauma. Accumulating stress increases trauma risk. Cleared through downtime vice indulgence (with its own risks) or other downtime actions. Load determines available equipment (within reason), declared retroactively when needed. Elegant abstraction maintaining strategic resource decisions without excessive bookkeeping.

**Torchbearer Grind**: Possibly most resource-intensive mainstream TTRPG. Every action potentially increases grind, which triggers condition checks. Conditions (Hungry, Thirsty, Exhausted, Injured, Sick, Angry, Afraid) accumulate relentlessly and require resources to clear (food, water, rest time). Inventory severely limited by carrying capacity. Light sources deplete. Equipment degrades. Resource management IS the game—the dungeon is engine for depleting characters who must balance delving deeper against increasing fragility.

**The One Ring Hope and Shadow**: Hope functions as expendable heroic resource—spend for bonuses, special actions, or resisting corruption. Shadow accumulates from dark deeds, trauma, and corruption. When Hope reduced to 0, character risks falling to Shadow. Creates thematic tension: expending Hope provides power but risks spiritual corruption. Resource management aligned with Tolkien's themes of hope versus despair.

**Fate Fate Points**: Refresh at start of each session. Spend to invoke aspects (gain +2 or reroll), declare story details, power stunts. Earn by accepting compels (complications from aspects). Elegant economy where narrative currency also provides mechanical benefits. Resource management through fate point economy creates interesting decisions without complex tracking.

**Pathfinder 2e Focus Points**: Special resource pool (usually 1-3 points) powering focus spells and abilities. Replenish fully on short rest (10 minutes of refocus). Provides per-encounter magical capabilities for non-casters and supplement for casters. System creates resource decisions at encounter scale without requiring long rest dependency.

**Ironsworn Momentum**: Narrative resource representing drive and progress. Gained through successful rolls, spent to improve chances or resist negative outcomes. Can reset on particularly bad failures. Creates interesting push-your-luck decisions: spend momentum now for advantage or accumulate for bigger opportunities?

**Forbidden Lands Willpower**: Resource spent for magic use, recovering through rest. Combine with resource-scarcity survival game (food, water, equipment). The survival-hexcrawl structure makes resource management central strategic challenge balancing exploration ambition against sustainability.

**Old-School D&D Resource Management**: Traditional dungeon crawling featured tight resource management: limited healing (clerics prepare healing spells, limited slots), light sources with strict duration, encumbrance limits, ammunition tracking, rations and water. Dungeon expeditions featured escalating resource depletion, with parties choosing when to retreat versus risk pressing forward while depleted. Resource attrition was central challenge, creating strategic tension between exploration ambition and prudent retreat.

## Impact on Play

Resource management systems profoundly shape player behavior, strategic planning, gameplay rhythm, and overall game feel.

**Strategic Planning**: Robust resource management creates strategic gameplay layer beyond immediate tactical decisions. Players must consider: How many encounters remain today? Should I spend powerful ability now or save it? Is short rest safe here? Do we need to retreat and rest? These strategic questions add depth and create satisfying gameplay when resource allocation decisions prove consequential.

**Risk vs. Reward Decisions**: Resources create risk/reward dynamics. Using powerful abilities provides immediate safety/success but creates future vulnerability. Being stingy with resources risks present failure while preserving future capability. Finding the balance—spending enough to succeed without excessive expenditure—becomes core skill.

**Gameplay Rhythm**: Resource systems establish session rhythm. Games with generous recovery between encounters create episodic structure where each fight is relatively isolated. Games with daily resource pools create adventuring day arc with escalating tension as resources deplete. Games with scarce long-term resources create campaign-scale strategic considerations.

**Power Curves**: How resources deplete and recover affects character power across time. Characters are strongest after long rest, weakening as resources deplete, then recovering. This creates variable power level within adventuring day. Some designs (4e, 13th Age) minimize this variance; others (Torchbearer, OSR) embrace it as central challenge.

**Decision Quality**: Resource management creates interesting decisions when: resources are meaningfully scarce (abundant resources reduce decisions), future resource needs are uncertain (known future needs make current decisions obvious), and expenditure has clear benefits (weak abilities aren't worth resources). Good design calibrates these factors for desired decision density.

**The Conservation Trap**: Overly scarce resources or harsh depletion can cause players to hoard resources excessively, never using powerful abilities because "I might need them later." This creates degenerate play where players don't engage with cool abilities. Good design encourages expenditure through: reliable regeneration (you'll get resources back), encouraging expenditure (encounter powers that reset frequently), or powerful at-will options (not everything is limited).

**Class Experience Variance**: Resource-dependent classes (spellcasters) experience dramatic power variation based on resource availability, while resource-independent classes (basic fighters) maintain consistency. This can create balance issues and disparate player experiences—some players constantly manage resources while others don't engage with resource systems.

**Table Culture**: Resource management expectations vary wildly between tables. Some groups embrace attrition gameplay with multiple encounters between rests; others long rest after every encounter. Resource systems designed for specific play patterns may fail if table culture doesn't match design assumptions.
