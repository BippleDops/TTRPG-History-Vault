# Clocks and Progress Tracks

**First Appeared**: Apocalypse World (2010, countdown clocks), refined in [[Blades in the Dark]] (2017)
**Designers**: [[D. Vincent Baker]], [[John Harper]]
**Category**: Progress Tracking

## Overview

Clocks and Progress Tracks provide visual circular/linear diagrams for tracking ongoing threats, long-term projects, faction plans, and gradual progress toward goals—segments fill as actions advance toward completion, creating transparent visible representation of off-screen events, complex project progression, and mounting dangers, revolutionizing how RPGs handle non-immediate threats and long-term endeavors while providing GM tools for managing multiple simultaneous storylines.

**Apocalypse World Countdown Clocks** (2010): Vincent Baker introduced countdown clocks (6-segment circles) tracking threats—"The Warlord's Army Arrives" ticks down each session until full, then arrives.

**Blades in the Dark Refinement** (2017): John Harper expanded clocks into comprehensive system:
- **Progress Clocks**: Tracking PC projects (4, 6, or 8 segments based on complexity)
- **Danger Clocks**: Tracking mounting threats
- **Faction Clocks**: Off-screen faction plans advancing
- **Long-term Projects**: Downt

ime activities requiring multiple sessions
- **Obstacles**: Complex challenges requiring sustained effort

**Visual Clarity**: Circles with segments (like pie slices) create immediate visual understanding of progress—half-filled clock shows you're halfway.

**Flexible Sizing**: 4-segment (simple), 6-segment (moderate), 8-segment (complex), 12-segment (epic)—matching task complexity.

**Massive Adoption**: Became standard tool across Forged in the Dark, many PbtA games, and numerous narrative systems for tracking anything requiring sustained effort or mounting danger.

## Historical Context

Before clocks, RPGs tracked long-term events poorly:
- **GM Notes**: Hidden tracking invisible to players
- **Session Counting**: "The army arrives in 3 sessions" felt arbitrary
- **Abstract**: "You're making progress on research" without concrete representation
- **Binary**: Projects were incomplete or complete, no middle visibility

Baker's Countdown Clocks (AW 2010) provided breakthrough: visual diagram showing threat advancement. Each GM move potentially ticked clock segment. When full, threat manifested. This made off-screen threats tangible and created tension as clocks filled.

Harper evolved this into comprehensive system. Recognizing clocks could track anything gradual:
- Player projects (crafting items, researching secrets, building reputations)
- Enemy plans (rival crew's scheme, cult ritual, heist preparation)
- Faction developments (organization growth, political movements, war preparations)
- Obstacles (breaking through defenses, earning trust, completing heists)
- Dangers (spreading fire, gathering reinforcements, demons manifesting)

The Blades in the Dark implementation (2017) made clocks central rather than peripheral—nearly every session involved multiple clocks tracking simultaneous developments. This gave GMs tools for managing complex campaigns with many moving parts while keeping everything visible to players.

Post-Blades, clocks proliferated. Every FitD game includes them. Many PbtA games added them. Non-narrative systems sometimes adapted them. By 2020s, clocks had become standard RPG tool comparable to hit points or experience—a fundamental mechanical element most games either included or consciously chose to exclude.

## Mechanical Framework

### Clock Creation

**Determine Segments**: Based on task complexity/scope
- **4-Segment**: Simple projects (picking complex lock, starting rumor)
- **6-Segment**: Moderate challenges (researching ancient text, earning gang's trust)  
- **8-Segment**: Complex undertakings (crafting magical artifact, infiltrating organization)
- **12-Segment**: Epic projects (building empire, completing grand ritual)

**Label Clock**: Clear description of what completion means

**Track Visually**: Draw circle divided into segments, fill as progress occurs

### Filling Clock Segments

**Player Clocks**: Fill based on successful actions
- Full success: Tick appropriate segments (1-3 based on effect level)
- Partial success: Fewer ticks
- Failure: No progress (possibly clock clear/reset)

**Danger/Faction Clocks**: Fill based on:
- GM moves and scene outcomes
- Time passing
- Logical consequences of events
- Player actions enabling enemy progress

**Multiple Clocks**: Often several active simultaneously
- PC's project clock
- Rival's counter-project clock
- Danger clock (guards mobilizing)
- Faction clock (political ally gaining power)

### Clock Completion

When clock fills completely:
- **Project Clocks**: Project completes, gain benefits
- **Danger Clocks**: Threat manifests, complications arise
- **Faction Clocks**: Off-screen event occurs
- **Obstacle Clocks**: Breakthrough achieved

### Connected Clocks

**Linked Progress**: One clock's completion might:
- Start new clock
- Add segments to another clock
- Clear opposing clock
- Create complications

**Example**: "Research Ritual" clock completion starts "Perform Ritual" clock while also ticking "Cult Awareness of You" danger clock.

### Opposed Clocks

Racing clocks where both sides advance:
- PC clock: "Crack the Safe"  
- Danger clock: "Guards Return"
- Whoever fills first wins

## Games Using Clocks

### Forged in the Dark (All Games)

Clocks are mandatory FitD element:
- **Blades in the Dark**: Original implementation
- **Scum and Villainy**: Ship repair clocks, faction clocks
- **Band of Blades**: Mission clocks, Legion threat clocks, Commander strategic clocks
- **Others**: Every FitD game uses clocks extensively

### PbtA Games

Many adopted clocks:
- **Dungeon World**: Fronts use grim portents (clock-like)
- **Monster of the Week**: Mystery countdown
- **Urban Shadows**: Faction advancement
- **Various Others**: Adapted for specific needs

### Beyond Narrative Games

Even traditional games sometimes adapt:
- **D&D 5E**: Some GMs use clocks for complex skill challenges
- **FATE**: Adapted alongside stress tracks
- **OSR Games**: Borrowed for faction/domain play

## Variations and Adaptations

**Different Shapes**: Some use linear tracks instead of circles (progress bars)

**Different Sizes**: 3-segment to 20-segment depending on scope

**Color Coding**: Different colors for threat types

**Nested Clocks**: Large clock with sub-clocks

**Countdown vs Progress**: Some count down (threat approaching) vs up (project advancing)

**Hybrid Systems**: Combining clocks with other mechanics

## Design Considerations

### When Clocks Excel

**Long-Term Projects**: Anything requiring sustained effort

**Off-Screen Events**: Faction plans, distant threats

**Complex Obstacles**: Multi-stage challenges

**Mounting Dangers**: Gradually escalating threats

**Campaign Management**: Tracking simultaneous developments

**Transparent Stakes**: Showing players what's developing

### When Clocks Don't Fit

**Immediate Actions**: Things resolved in moment don't need clocks

**Binary Outcomes**: Simple yes/no doesn't benefit from tracking

**Surprise Threats**: When you want unpredictability

**Overcomplexity**: Too many clocks can overwhelm

## Cultural Impact

**Universal Adoption**: Became standard tool across narrative gaming

**Visual Gaming**: Legitimized visual diagrams as mechanical elements

**Transparency**: Made GM tracking visible to players

**Campaign Management**: Gave GMs tools for complex multi-thread campaigns

**Faction Play**: Enabled sophisticated off-screen faction development

**Design Vocabulary**: "Clock" became standard term in design discussions

**Educational**: Used teaching game design for progress tracking

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Elegant simple visual tracking
- Creates tension as clocks fill
- Transparent to players
- Flexible for any gradual progress
- Easy to understand and implement
- Scales from simple to complex

**Criticism**:
- Can feel gamey/breaks immersion for some
- Requires tracking multiple simultaneously
- GM must remember to tick appropriately
- Visible clocks might reduce suspense
- Over-use creates clutter

## Legacy and Influence

Clocks became fundamental RPG tool, comparable to experience points or initiative—basic mechanical element most narrative games include. The innovation demonstrated visual diagrams could serve mechanical purposes beautifully.

**Design Standard**: Expected element in FitD, common in PbtA

**GM Tool**: Essential for managing complex campaigns

**Player Transparency**: Shifted toward visible rather than hidden tracking

**Progress Visualization**: Template for any gradual advancement
