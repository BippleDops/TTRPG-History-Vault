# Clocks and Progress Tracks

**First Appeared In**: **[[Blades in the Dark (2017)]]** (formalized), with precedents in Apocalypse World (2010)
**Year**: 2017
**Complexity**: 2/5
**Popularity**: 4/5

## Overview

Clocks (also called Progress Tracks or Progress Clocks) are visual countdown timers represented as segmented circles that fill incrementally as events progress toward completion. Rather than tracking complex conditions, turn counts, or narrative developments through text notes, Clocks provide at-a-glance visual representation of advancing threats, progressing plans, developing situations, and ongoing projects. A 4-segment Clock tracking "Guards Discover Intruders" fills one segment each time players make noise—when full, guards arrive. An 8-segment Clock for "Complete the Ritual" fills as cultists chant incantations—when complete, the demon manifests. This simple visual tool revolutionized narrative pacing by making abstract progress concrete, transparent, and dramatically visible to all players.

The genius of Clocks lies in their elegant simplicity combined with profound flexibility. A circle divided into 4, 6, 8, or 12 segments communicates instantly how far along something is—half-filled means halfway done, one segment filled means just beginning, nearly full means imminent completion. This visual clarity eliminates bookkeeping ("how many turns until the fire spreads?"), creates dramatic tension (watching a danger Clock fill inexorably), and enables transparent GM-player communication about narrative state. Unlike hidden GM notes where players don't know if they're racing against 3 turns or 30, Clocks show progress openly, allowing informed strategic decisions.

Clocks solved fundamental problems in narrative pacing and consequence tracking. Traditional RPGs struggled representing gradual change—fires spread, reinforcements arrive, rituals complete, but tracking these developments required GM notes, memory, or arbitrary timing. "The reinforcements arrive in 5 rounds" feels artificial and videogame-like; "The reinforcements Clock is 6/8 filled" feels organic and responsive. When players create distractions, the Clock might only fill 1 segment instead of 2. When they take too long, the GM might fill 2 segments instead of 1. The Clock responds to fiction while providing structure, creating dramatic pacing without railroading.

## Historical Context

Countdown mechanics existed in earlier games but lacked Blades in the Dark's elegant visual implementation. Apocalypse World (2010) introduced "countdown clocks" as GM tools—textual lists tracking threat progression (6:00 = initial signs, 9:00 = direct threat, 12:00 = disaster). These communicated conceptually but lacked visual immediacy and remained largely GM-facing tools rather than transparent player-facing mechanics.

Traditional RPG approaches to ongoing situations were cumbersome:

**Turn Counting**: "Reinforcements arrive in 10 turns" required tracking countdown, felt arbitrary, didn't respond to player actions beyond speeding/slowing by fixed amounts.

**HP Tracking**: Using HP-like pools for abstract concepts (fire damage to building, ritual completion percentage) repurposed combat mechanics awkwardly.

**GM Notes**: Writing "fire spreading slowly" without concrete tracking led to inconsistent or forgotten progression.

**Triggered Events**: Waiting for specific player actions to trigger consequences removed tension—if players avoid trigger, consequence never occurs.

John Harper developed Clocks for Blades in the Dark to solve these problems. He needed mechanics representing heist complications, faction machinations, and long-term projects that felt responsive to player action while maintaining dramatic pacing. Drawing from board gaming's track mechanics and refining Apocalypse World's countdown concept, Harper created the segmented circle visual that became Blades' signature mechanic.

The visual design proved crucial. A circle divided into segments communicates instantly:
- **4-Clock**: Quick progress (one fight, short chase)
- **6-Clock**: Standard duration (typical obstacle)
- **8-Clock**: Extended progress (complex project, major threat)
- **12-Clock**: Long-term development (faction schemes, epic rituals)

This vocabulary allowed Harper to design scenarios with appropriate dramatic pacing—simple obstacles used small Clocks, epic schemes used large Clocks—without tracking turn counts or arbitrary thresholds.

Blades in the Dark's 2017 release popularized Clocks throughout indie RPG communities. The mechanic's elegance, flexibility, and visual appeal led to widespread adoption. Within years, dozens of Forged in the Dark games used Clocks as foundational mechanics, and non-FitD games borrowed the concept. By 2020, Clocks had become standard indie RPG vocabulary, joining Aspects and Advantage/Disadvantage as recognizable cross-game mechanics.

## Mechanical Function

### Clock Structure

**Visual Format**: Circle divided into 4, 6, 8, or 12 segments (typically)
**Progress**: Fill segments sequentially as clock advances
**Completion**: When all segments filled, event occurs or project completes
**Visibility**: Usually visible to all players, creating shared narrative awareness

**Example**:
```
6-segment Clock: "Fire Spreads to Second Floor"
[■][■][■][□][□][□]
Half-filled = fire halfway up stairs
```

### Clock Sizes and Duration

**4-Clock**: Fast progress
- Minor obstacles: picking a complex lock, brief chase
- Quick threats: nearby guards investigating noise
- Short-term projects: crafting basic item

**6-Clock**: Standard duration
- Typical obstacles: defeating tough opponent, navigating dangerous terrain
- Medium threats: squad searching building, ritual reaching climax
- Standard projects: researching obscure knowledge, establishing contact

**8-Clock**: Extended progress
- Complex obstacles: infiltrating heavily guarded facility, winning political campaign
- Serious threats: mobilizing faction response, unleashing supernatural entity
- Major projects: constructing hideout upgrade, developing new technology

**12-Clock (or 10-Clock)**: Long-term development
- Epic undertakings: overthrowing government, summoning ancient god
- Faction machinations: conspiracy reaching fruition, war preparations
- Legendary projects: creating artifact, mastering ultimate technique

The GM selects Clock size based on narrative scope and desired dramatic pacing—not rigid rules but aesthetic judgment about how much spotlight and progression feels appropriate.

### Filling Clocks

Clocks fill through various triggers:

**Action Consequences**: Player actions fill related Clocks
- Making noise fills "Guards Alerted" Clock
- Spending downtime fills "Project Completion" Clock
- Attacking fills opponent's "Defeat" Clock (representing HP alternative)

**Time Passage**: Some Clocks fill automatically per unit time
- Each scene/hour/day fills faction scheme Clocks
- Environmental hazards fill per round
- Diseases/poisons fill per time period

**Roll Results**: Dice outcomes determine segments filled
- Success on action roll: Fill 1-2 segments
- Partial success: Fill 1 segment
- Failure: No progress (or opponent's Clock fills instead)

**GM Judgment**: GM fills segments responding to fiction
- Loud explosion? Fill danger Clock 2 segments
- Clever distraction? Fill enemy Clock only 1 segment instead of 2
- Ignoring problem? Fill threat Clock 2+ segments

**Contested Clocks**: Both sides have Clocks, racing to fill theirs first
- Players' "Hack the System" (6-Clock) vs. GM's "Security Lockdown" (4-Clock)
- Whoever fills their Clock first wins the contest

### Using Clocks in Play

**Obstacles**: Represent challenges requiring sustained effort
- "Break Down the Door" (4-Clock): Each attempt fills segments
- "Win Over the Noble" (6-Clock): Social actions fill based on quality
- "Traverse the Blizzard" (8-Clock): Travel actions advance progress

**Threats**: Track danger increasing over time
- "Reinforcements Arrive" (6-Clock): Fills based on alarm level
- "Building Collapses" (8-Clock): Fills from structural damage
- "Demon Breaks Free" (6-Clock): Fills as bindings weaken

**Projects**: Long-term undertakings during downtime
- "Research Vampire Weaknesses" (8-Clock): Fill via study actions
- "Build Secret Passage" (12-Clock): Fill with construction work
- "Establish Spy Network" (10-Clock): Fill through recruitment

**Faction Clocks**: Track NPC organization plans
- "The Gray Cloaks Discover Your Identity" (8-Clock): Fills as they investigate
- "The Cult Summons Their God" (12-Clock): Fills via ritual progress
- "War Erupts" (10-Clock): Fills from escalating tensions

**Linked Clocks**: Multiple Clocks interact
- Filling "Stealth Infiltration" Clock prevents filling "Guards Alerted" Clock
- Completing "Disable Wards" Clock makes "Open Vault" Clock smaller
- Faction Clocks fill or empty based on player actions

### Reducing/Emptying Clocks

Clocks don't only fill—they can reduce or reset:

**Active Reduction**: Player actions can erase segments
- "Calm the Angry Mob" empties segments from "Riot" Clock
- "Tend the Wound" reduces "Bleeding Out" Clock
- "Sabotage Ritual" removes segments from "Summon Demon" Clock

**Reset/Clear**: Some events empty Clocks entirely
- Defeating guards clears "Pursuit" Clock
- Completing heist clears all related danger Clocks
- Faction defeated empties their scheme Clocks

**Degradation**: Time might reduce certain Clocks
- "Public Scandal" Clock loses segments as news cycle moves on
- "Heat/Wanted Level" Clock degrades during downtime
- "Trail Goes Cold" represents investigation Clock losing progress

### Multiple Simultaneous Clocks

Complex situations use multiple Clocks tracking different aspects:

**Heist Scene**:
- "Crack the Safe" (6-Clock, player progress)
- "Guards Arrive" (4-Clock, danger)
- "Escape Route Closes" (8-Clock, timer)

**Chase Sequence**:
- "Catch the Fugitive" (player Clock)
- "Fugitive Escapes" (opponent Clock)
- "Bystanders Endangered" (consequence Clock)

**Social Intrigue**:
- "Win Duke's Support" (6-Clock, player goal)
- "Duke's Patience" (4-Clock, danger)
- "Rival Makes Their Move" (6-Clock, opposition)

Players and GM track these Clocks simultaneously, creating layered tension where multiple factors progress toward completion, forcing prioritization and strategic decisions.

## Design Philosophy

### Transparent Dramatic Pacing

Clocks make narrative progression visible rather than hidden. When players see "Guards Discover You" Clock at 5/6 segments, they know they're one slip from disaster. This transparency enables informed decisions—push luck for one more attempt, or escape before the Clock fills?

Traditional hidden tracking ("the GM knows reinforcements arrive soon but players don't") creates tension through ignorance. Clock-based tracking creates tension through knowledge—you see danger approaching and must decide whether to risk it. This informed-tension approach respects player agency while maintaining drama.

### Fiction-First Flexibility

Clocks respond to fiction rather than rigid mechanics. There's no formula for "explosion fills Clocks by X segments"—the GM judges based on narrative circumstances. Massive explosion in quiet library? Fill danger Clock significantly. Small noise during bustling party? Maybe don't fill at all.

This flexibility makes Clocks adaptable to any situation without requiring exhaustive rules. The visual tool provides structure; fictional judgment provides content. GMs don't need tables or calculations—just narrative sense of "does this progress that Clock?"

### Fail Forward Momentum

Clocks enable "fail forward" design where failure doesn't stop progression but complicates it. Failed lockpicking attempt? Don't just "fail, nothing happens"—fill the "Guards Hear Noise" Clock. The scene progresses either way; success advances player Clock, failure advances danger Clock. This maintains momentum and ensures dramatic progression regardless of roll outcomes.

**Traditional**: "You fail to pick the lock. What do you do?"
**Clock-Based**: "You fail to pick the lock. The 'Guards Hear Noise' Clock fills 2 segments (now 4/6). What do you do?"

The second provides concrete escalation and clear stakes, transforming static failure into dynamic complications.

### Visual Communication

Circles with filled segments communicate instantly across language, mathematical ability, and gaming experience. A half-filled Clock clearly means "halfway done" without explanation. This visual clarity reduces cognitive load and makes game state transparent.

The physical act of filling segments creates ritual reinforcement—marking a segment makes progression tangible and memorable. Players remember "when we filled that third segment, everything changed" more than "when the turn counter reached 7."

### Collaborative Pacing

Visible Clocks allow collaborative pacing discussions:

**Player**: "That Clock is nearly full—we need to hurry!"
**GM**: "You can push harder but might trigger complications."
**Group Decision**: Based on visible Clock state

This shared awareness transforms pacing from GM-controlled to collaboratively negotiated. Players see approaching deadlines and strategize accordingly; GM responds to player urgency by adjusting Clock-filling rates.

### Consequence without Railroad

Clocks track natural consequences without predetermined outcomes. "Reinforcements Arrive" Clock fills based on player actions—make noise, it fills faster; create distractions, it fills slower; it completes when it completes, not on schedule turn 15.

This combines structure (something will happen) with flexibility (when depends on circumstances) and player agency (their choices matter). Not railroading ("guards arrive turn 10 regardless") but not formless ("I'll decide when guards arrive based on vibes"). The Clock provides framework that responds to play.

## Evolution Over Time

### Apocalypse World Countdown Clocks (2010)

Vincent Baker's Apocalypse World introduced "countdown clocks" as GM tools—textual threat trackers:

```
The Warlord's Revenge
3:00 - Warlord hears about humiliation
6:00 - Sends scouts to locate players
9:00 - Mobilizes war party
12:00 - Attacks player settlement
```

These conceptually similar Clocks lacked visual circles and remained GM-facing tools. Players might not know Clocks existed, let alone their status.

**Innovations**: Established threat tracking as structured countdown, made consequences inevitable-but-uncertain, encouraged responsive GM play.

**Limitations**: Text-based, GM-secret, less visually immediate than circle segments.

### Blades in the Dark (2017)

John Harper refined countdown Clocks into visual segmented circles and made them transparent player-facing tools:

**Innovations**:
- Visual circle format with 4/6/8/12 segments
- Transparent player-visible Clocks
- Flexible application to obstacles, threats, projects, factions
- Contested Clocks racing to completion
- Integration with action roll consequences

**Impact**: Clocks became signature Blades mechanic, widely imitated and adapted.

### Forged in the Dark Proliferation (2017-Present)

Dozens of games using Forged in the Dark framework adopted Clocks:

**Scum and Villainy** (2018): Space opera using Clocks for ship damage, faction schemes, sector control.

**Band of Blades** (2019): Military fantasy using Clocks for mission objectives, army morale, corruption spread.

**Beam Saber** (2020): Mech combat using Clocks for objectives, relationships, pilot stress.

Each implementation tweaked Clock applications while maintaining core visual format, demonstrating mechanic's flexibility across genres.

### Beyond Forged in the Dark

Non-FitD games adopted Clock-inspired mechanics:

**Progress Tracks** (various): Linear tracks serving similar functions to circular Clocks—visual progress representation.

**Threat Timers** (modern D&D): DMs using segment tracking for lair actions, environmental effects, reinforcement arrivals.

**Countdown Dice** (various): Physical dice showing decreasing numbers representing Clock-like countdowns.

**Burning Wheel Artha**: While predating Clocks, similar visual tracking of earned points.

The mechanic's viral spread demonstrated its utility and accessibility—designers across styles and genres found applications for visual progress tracking.

### Modern Refinements

Contemporary Clock usage includes:

**Clock Interaction**: Completing one Clock affects others (finishing "Hack Security" reduces "Break Into Vault" segments needed).

**Nested Clocks**: Large projects contain sub-Clocks tracking components.

**Degrading Clocks**: Segments empty over time without maintenance.

**Opposition Clocks**: GM and player Clocks directly oppose—filling yours empties theirs.

**Character Clocks**: Tracking recovery, long-term conditions, personal projects separate from scene Clocks.

## Cultural Impact

### Visual Tool Adoption

Clocks demonstrated visual tools' power in traditionally text-based RPGs. The segmented circle became immediately recognizable symbol of Forged in the Dark games and narrative pacing mechanics generally.

Digital tools embraced Clocks—Roll20, Foundry VTT, and custom apps feature Clock tools, making virtual play track progress visually just as physical play does.

### "Fill a Clock" Vocabulary

"Fill a Clock," "Clock's almost full," "start a new Clock" entered indie RPG vocabulary. Designers discuss mechanics in Clock terms even when not directly implementing them, demonstrating conceptual influence beyond literal adoption.

### GM Toolkit Standard

Clocks became standard GM toolkit recommendation even in games not mechanically supporting them. Online GMing advice regularly suggests "use a Clock to track that" for various pacing and consequence challenges, regardless of system played.

### Pacing Revolution

Clocks influenced how designers think about narrative pacing. Rather than arbitrary "event occurs turn X" or purely improvisational "event occurs when GM decides," Clock-based pacing creates structured-yet-responsive development. This middle path influenced narrative design broadly.

### Player Empowerment

Making consequences visible empowered players to make informed decisions. This transparency philosophy influenced design discussions about GM information sharing, hidden rolls, and player agency.

## Strengths and Weaknesses

### Strengths

**Visual Clarity**: Segmented circle communicates progress instantly without explanation or calculation.

**Transparent Pacing**: Players see approaching deadlines and consequences, enabling informed strategic decisions.

**Flexible Application**: Works for obstacles, threats, projects, factions, abstract concepts—nearly anything requiring progress tracking.

**Fiction-First**: Responds to narrative circumstances without rigid mechanical formulas.

**Fail Forward**: Facilitates complications and consequences on failure without halting momentum.

**Collaborative Pacing**: Shared visibility enables group discussions about urgency and prioritization.

**Scalable Complexity**: Simple concept scales from single Clock to multiple interacting Clocks based on scene complexity.

**Accessible**: Requires no special materials (draw circles, mark segments), easy to explain, intuitive to use.

**Dramatic Tension**: Watching Clocks fill creates mounting pressure and deadline-driven urgency.

**GM Tool**: Provides structure for consequence timing without railroading or arbitrary event triggers.

### Weaknesses

**Visual Tracking Requirements**: Physical/digital space needed for tracking multiple Clocks simultaneously. Can clutter table with numerous circles.

**Judgment Dependency**: Requires GM to consistently judge "how many segments does this fill?" without mechanical formulas. Inconsistency creates confusion.

**Complexity Creep**: Complex scenes with 5+ simultaneous Clocks can overwhelm tracking capacity and create cognitive load.

**Arbitrary Sizing**: Choosing 4 vs. 6 vs. 8 segments requires aesthetic judgment that new GMs struggle with. "Is this situation a 6-Clock or 8-Clock?"

**Transparency Trade-offs**: Some tension comes from ignorance. Visible Clocks eliminate surprise timing, reducing certain dramatic techniques.

**Maintenance Overhead**: Multiple Clocks require consistent tracking and updating. Easy to forget to fill Clock after appropriate trigger.

**Fill-Rate Ambiguity**: Without guidelines, determining fill rates ("does loud noise fill 1 or 2 segments?") becomes subjective, potentially contentious.

**Pacing Predictability**: Mathematical players calculate exact Clock-filling rates, potentially reducing tension when outcomes become statistically certain.

**Limited Precision**: Segments are discrete units. Can't represent "25% progress" precisely—must round to nearest segment.

**Cultural Specificity**: While widely adopted in indie circles, remains largely unknown in traditional RPG communities (D&D, Pathfinder, etc.).

## Modern Usage

### Best Practices

Contemporary Clock implementation wisdom:

**Default to 6-Segments**: When uncertain about Clock size, use 6 segments. Works for most situations, easily adjusted if needed.

**Limit Active Clocks**: Track maximum 4-5 Clocks simultaneously per scene. More creates confusion and overwhelm.

**Name Clocks Clearly**: "Guards Discover Intruders" clearer than "Guards Clock." Specific names communicate what completion means.

**Announce Fills**: State when Clocks fill segments: "That explosion fills 'Building Collapses' 2 segments, now at 5/8." Makes progression explicit.

**Collaborative Clock Creation**: When situation arises, discuss Clock size and fill rate with players. "Let's track reinforcements—8-Clock sound reasonable?"

**Use Sparingly**: Not every situation needs Clocks. Reserve for important obstacles, significant threats, or projects requiring spotlight.

**Connect to Fiction**: Ensure Clock-filling connects to fictional events, not arbitrary mechanics. "Clock fills because your explosion was loud" not "Clock fills because you failed your roll."

**Consider Pace**: Adjust Clock size to desired spotlight duration. Short scene? 4-Clock. Extended focus? 8-Clock. Segment count controls dramatic length.

**Reset Between Scenes**: Don't let Clocks persist indefinitely. Clear completed/irrelevant Clocks when situations resolve or scenes end.

**Document During Play**: Track active Clocks on paper or digital tool where all players can see them. Prevents forgetting and maintains transparency.

### When to Use Clocks

Clocks work best for:

- Tracking obstacles requiring sustained effort
- Representing increasing threats over time
- Long-term projects spanning multiple sessions
- Racing deadlines creating urgency
- Faction schemes progressing in background
- Environmental effects (fires, floods, collapses)
- Contested situations where both sides progress toward goals

Clocks may not suit:

- Instantaneous events (explosion happens immediately, no progressive Clock)
- Highly unpredictable situations where progress tracking feels artificial
- Situations benefiting from surprise timing
- Extremely short durations (2-segment Clocks feel pointless—just resolve immediately)
- Traditional simulation-focused games prioritizing mechanical precision over narrative pacing

### Clock Alternatives

When Clocks don't fit, alternatives include:

**Hit Point Tracking**: Use HP-like pools for abstract "damage to situation" tracking.

**Turn Counting**: Simple "event occurs turn X" for precise timing needs.

**Condition Lists**: Narrative description of situation status without numerical tracking.

**Dice Rolls**: Random determination when event occurs (roll each turn to see if reinforcements arrive).

**GM Judgment**: Purely improvisational timing based on narrative flow.

Each approach has trade-offs; Clocks balance structure, flexibility, and transparency effectively for many situations.
