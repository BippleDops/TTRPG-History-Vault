# Conflict Resolution Systems

**First Appeared**: Various narrativist games (2000s), prominently Dogs in the Vineyard (2004)
**CategoryMenuResolution Framework / Design Philosophy

## Overview

Conflict Resolution Systems resolve entire conflicts (not individual tasks) by setting stakes before rolling—"Do I convince the sheriff to help?" not "Do I roll Persuade well?"—determining what's actually at risk rather than just whether single action succeeds, creating resolution focused on dramatic outcomes over task completion, becoming fundamental narrativist principle distinguishing Dogs in the Vineyard's escalation, Burning Wheel's Duel of Wits, and Primetime Adventures' stakes-setting from traditional task resolution (D&D's "I roll to hit" doesn't establish what combat MEANS, just whether sword connects).

**Task Resolution** (Traditional):
- "I attack" → roll → hit/miss
- "I persuade them" → roll → succeed/fail
- Individual actions resolved

**Conflict Resolution** (Narrativist):
- "Do I defeat them in combat achieving my goal?" → roll → yes/no with complications
- "Do I convince them to help?" → roll → they help/refuse/conditional
- Entire conflict stakes resolved

**Revolutionary Shift**: From simulating actions to determining dramatic outcomes

**Impact**: Became defining narrativist/traditional divide. Influenced how designers think about what resolution means. Demonstrated focus on drama over simulation.

## Historical Context

Traditional RPGs (D&D, GURPS) used **task resolution**:
- Roll to perform specific action
- Success/failure determined for that task
- Repeat for each action in conflict
- Victory emerges from accumulated task successes

**Problems**:
- Combat dragged through individual attack rolls
- Social scenes reduced to Persuasion checks not dramatic outcomes
- Didn't address what conflict MEANT
- Simulation over drama

Narrativist designers recognized disconnect: players cared about conflict outcomes (do we defeat enemy? do we convince king?) not individual task success (does my sword hit? does my argument land?).

**Solution**: Resolve conflicts not tasks—set stakes, roll once (or structured exchange), determine outcome.

## Conflict Resolution Examples

### Dogs in the Vineyard (2004)

**Escalating Conflict**:
1. Set stakes: "Do I convince him to confess?"
2. Exchange raises/sees using dice from talking pool
3. Can escalate to physical, then fighting, adding more dice but risking more harm
4. Continue until someone can't/won't see, accepting stakes resolution

**Revolutionary**: Entire conflict resolved through escalating exchange. Stakes clear from start. Can always win if willing to escalate to violence.

### Burning Wheel Duel of Wits (2002)

**Social Combat**:
1. Set stakes for both sides
2. Determine Disposition (social hit points)
3. Choose tactics (Point, Rebut, Obfuscate, etc.)
4. Scripted exchanges
5. First to zero Disposition loses, stakes resolve as set

**RevolutionaryMenuSocial conflicts as mechanically engaging as combat. Clear stakes. Structured resolution.

### Primetime Adventures (2004)

**Stakes-Setting**:
1. Player narrates what they want
2. Producer sets opposition/counter-stakes
3. Draw cards (modified by traits/screen presence)
4. Winner narrates outcome (crucially, can narrate in any way including their loss)

**Revolutionary**: Winner narrates, not necessarily succeeds. Stakes clear. Outcome supports drama.

## Task vs. Conflict Theory

**Task Resolution**:
- **Question**: "Do I successfully perform this action?"
- **SimulationMenuModels individual actions
- **Iteration**: Multiple rolls for extended conflict
- **Tactics**: Positioning, equipment, circumstances affect individual attempts

**Conflict Resolution**:
- **Question**: "Do I achieve my goal in this conflict?"
- **Drama**: Determines narrative outcome
- **Single/Structured**: One roll or structured exchange resolves entire conflict
- **Stakes**: What's actually at risk

**Example** (Convincing Sheriff):

**Task**: "I roll Persuasion" → succeed → "I roll again with different argument" → succeed → "I roll again addressing objection" → ...

**Conflict**: "Do I convince sheriff to help?" → Roll → Result determines entire outcome

**Efficiency**: Conflict resolution faster for same outcome

**Focus**: On what matters (does he help?) not details (which argument landed?)

## Design Philosophy

**Drama Over Simulation**: Caring about outcomes not process

**Stakes Clarity**: Knowing what's at risk before rolling

**Efficiency**: Resolving conflicts faster

**Player Investment**: Clear understanding of consequences

**Narrative Focus**: Supporting story over tactics

**Outcome Determination**: Not action success but dramatic results

**Narrativist Principle**: Defining characteristic of story games

**Task/Conflict Divide**: Major theoretical distinction in design

## Games Using Conflict Resolution

**Dogs in the Vineyard**: Escalating exchanges resolving stakes

**Burning Wheel**: Duel of Wits, Fight!, Range & Cover—all conflict systems

**Primetime Adventures**: Card draw resolving scene stakes

**Many PbtA**: Some Moves resolve conflicts not tasks

**Various Narrativist**: Standard in story game design

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Faster resolution
- Focus on drama
- Clear stakes
- Supports narrative priorities
- Eliminates tedious task repetition

**Criticism**:
- Some miss tactical action detail
- Feels abstract to simulationists
- Can skip over interesting process
- Learning curve for traditional gamers
- Stakes negotiation can slow play

## Task Resolution Advantages

**Detail**: Simulating specific actions

**Tactics**: Positioning and choices matter

**Immersion**: Some prefer action-by-action

**Tradition**: Familiar to most gamers

**Uncertainty**: Individual actions' outcomes create surprise

## Conflict Resolution Advantages

**Speed**: Faster overall resolution

**Drama**: Focus on what matters narratively

**Clarity**: Stakes explicit

**Efficiency**: Skip boring tasks

**Narrative**: Supports story priorities

## Hybrid Approaches

Some games use both:
- Task resolution for unimportant
- Conflict resolution for dramatic scenes
- Player choice which to use
- Context-dependent application

## Design Lessons

**Know What You're Resolving**: Task or conflict? Be explicit.

**Match Priorities**: Use system matching creative agenda

**Stakes Matter**: Clarity about what's at risk

**Scale Appropriately**: Conflict for important, task for detail

## Legacy

Became fundamental narrativist principle. Task/conflict distinction essential design vocabulary. Influenced how designers think about what resolution means. Demonstrated drama-focus viable alternative to simulation.
