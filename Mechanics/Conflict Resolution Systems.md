# Conflict Resolution Systems

## Overview

Conflict resolution systems represent one of the most fundamental and philosophically significant design elements in tabletop roleplaying games. These systems determine not merely how dice are rolled or cards are drawn, but rather what questions players and game masters are actually asking when they invoke the mechanics, what scope of fictional action is resolved by a single mechanical procedure, and what kinds of outcomes are possible from that resolution.

The distinction between different approaches to conflict resolution has profound implications for gameplay experience, pacing, narrative authority, and the relationship between mechanical results and fictional outcomes. A game where every sword swing requires a roll creates a vastly different play experience than one where an entire duel is resolved with a single contested check, which in turn differs dramatically from a game where players negotiate stakes before rolling to determine not whether they succeed but rather what success and failure mean in context.

Understanding conflict resolution systems requires grasping the difference between **task resolution** (resolving specific actions: "Do I hit with my sword?"), **conflict resolution** (resolving entire scenes or conflicts: "Do I win this duel?"), and **stakes-based resolution** (resolving player-defined goals: "Can I defeat him without killing him and while impressing the princess?"). Each approach answers different questions, distributes narrative authority differently, and creates distinct play patterns.

Modern game design has increasingly recognized that the choice of resolution system is not merely technical but deeply philosophical, shaping what the game is "about" at a mechanical level. The granularity at which mechanics engage with fiction, the degree to which outcomes are predetermined versus negotiated, and the relationship between player intent and mechanical procedure all flow from fundamental decisions about how conflicts are resolved.

## Historical Development

The history of conflict resolution systems in TTRPGs is fundamentally a history of games discovering what questions their mechanics were asking and consciously choosing different questions to ask. Early designs inherited assumptions from wargaming without necessarily examining them, while later innovations came from deliberate interrogation of those inherited structures.

**Original D&D (1974)** established task resolution as the default paradigm for roleplaying games, though it did so accidentally rather than through conscious design choice. The game's wargaming heritage meant that combat was resolved through granular procedures—individual attack rolls, damage rolls, morale checks—that modeled specific tactical actions. This task-based approach spread to other activities: picking locks required a percentile roll for the specific task, searching for secret doors involved rolling for that particular action, and so forth. The system asked "Can you accomplish this specific action?" for each discrete thing characters attempted.

This task resolution paradigm dominated early RPG design because it seemed natural and obvious. Games like **RuneQuest (1978)**, **Traveller (1977)**, and **Call of Cthulhu (1981)** all adopted similar approaches, creating skill systems where each roll resolved a specific task. The granularity varied—some games rolled for every sword swing while others might resolve combat rounds more abstractly—but the fundamental question remained the same: "Do you succeed at this particular action?"

**Amber Diceless (1991)** represented an early challenge to mechanical resolution entirely, but its dictatless approach was often misunderstood as simply removing resolution mechanics rather than reconceptualizing what conflicts meant. However, [[Erick Wujcik]]'s design contained seeds of conflict resolution thinking in how entire scenes could be resolved through comparison of character attributes without breaking them down into discrete tasks.

The true watershed moment came with **The Window (1996)** and especially **Sorcerer (1996-2001)** by [[Ron Edwards]]. Edwards explicitly theorized about conflict resolution as distinct from task resolution, arguing that rolling dice should resolve entire conflicts with meaningful stakes rather than individual actions. In Sorcerer, a conflict resolution roll didn't just determine whether you hit someone—it could resolve an entire combat scene with consequences flowing from the mechanical result and negotiated fictional positioning.

**Dogs in the Vineyard (2004)** by [[Vincent Baker]] crystallized and popularized true conflict resolution, creating a system where players explicitly stated what they wanted before rolling, and the mechanics resolved whether they got it. The game's structure of Raises and Sees meant that a single conflict could encompass an entire scene, with mechanical choices (escalating from talking to physical violence) creating emergent narrative rather than modeling specific actions. Baker's design made visible what many games had left implicit: that resolution mechanics are fundamentally about who gets to say what happens in the fiction.

**HeroQuest (2003)** and its predecessor **Hero Wars (2000)** by [[Robin Laws]] developed extended contest mechanics that resolved entire complex conflicts through a single resolution procedure, with degrees of success and failure creating nuanced outcomes. This approach influenced many subsequent designs in recognizing that not every conflict needed blow-by-blow resolution.

**Burning Wheel (2002)** by [[Luke Crane]] introduced "Let It Ride," a rule stating that once you've rolled for something, that result stands until circumstances change significantly. This seemingly simple rule had profound implications: it transformed individual skill checks into conflict resolution by making each roll resolve not just a single attempt but an entire approach to a problem. Combined with Burning Wheel's Duel of Wits and Fight! systems, this created layered resolution where players chose appropriate scope for different conflicts.

**Fate (2003-2013)** developed sophisticated tools for conflict resolution including explicit conflict scenes with measurable stress and consequences, but more importantly, introduced the concept that players could invoke aspects to alter outcomes, effectively negotiating the meaning of mechanical results. The later Fate Core's emphasis on setting stakes before rolling and using outcomes to generate narrative complications represented mature conflict resolution design.

**Apocalypse World (2010)** synthesized many conflict resolution ideas into its move structure, where each move triggered by fiction had predetermined stakes (partial success, full success, failure) but left space for negotiation within those frameworks. PbtA games demonstrated that resolution mechanics could be both structured and flexible, with clear stakes but emergent outcomes.

Contemporary design increasingly treats resolution scope as a deliberate choice rather than an assumed default. Games like **Blades in the Dark (2017)** use flashbacks and flexible positioning to resolve conflicts at various scales, while story games often feature explicit stake-setting phases where players negotiate what's at risk before engaging mechanics.

## How It Works

Conflict resolution systems operate through several key dimensions that determine their functional characteristics and gameplay implications:

### Resolution Scope

**Task Resolution** resolves individual discrete actions. When a player says "I swing my sword at the orc," a task resolution system asks "Does your sword connect?" The answer is binary—yes or no—and tells you only whether that specific action succeeded. If you want to know whether you kill the orc, you need additional procedures (damage rolls, hit points). If you want to know whether you win the fight, you need to resolve many tasks sequentially until some end condition is met.

Example from **D&D 5e**: Player declares attack action. Roll d20+modifiers vs AC. If hit, roll damage dice. Reduce target HP. Repeat until someone reaches 0 HP or other end condition. Each roll resolves only "does this attack hit?" not "do I win this fight?"

**Conflict Resolution** resolves entire meaningful conflicts with single or structured mechanical procedures. When players engage conflict resolution, they're asking "Who gets what they want from this situation?" rather than "Does this specific action work?" The mechanics determine the outcome of the entire scene rather than individual actions within it.

Example from **Dogs in the Vineyard**: Players state what they want from a conflict ("I want the false prophet to publicly recant"). The conflict proceeds through Raises and Sees, with players narrating specific actions as color for their mechanical choices. The conflict ends when someone can't or won't match a Raise, determining who got what they wanted regardless of the specific fictional actions described.

Example from **Burning Wheel Fight!**: Rather than rolling for each sword swing, players script three actions, then resolve them simultaneously using positioning, weapon speed, and tactical choices. The entire exchange is resolved as a unit, with the conflict continuing through multiple exchanges until someone is taken out or yields.

**Stakes-Based Resolution** explicitly negotiates what's at risk before mechanics engage, treating outcomes as bargaining positions rather than predetermined by fictional positioning. Players and GM discuss what success and failure mean, potentially limiting stakes or adding complications, then roll to see whose stakes come to pass.

Example from **Fate Core**: Before rolling to convince the mayor to help, players and GM establish stakes: Success means the mayor provides resources, success with style means she also offers intelligence on the enemy, failure means she refuses, failure badly means she actively opposes the PCs. The mechanical result determines which outcome happens, but the stakes themselves were negotiated.

### Mechanical Structure

**Single Roll Resolution**: One mechanical procedure determines outcome. May include degrees of success (partial/full) or simple binary results. Quick, emphasizes stakes over process, but provides little room for tactical play or dramatic reversal.

Example: **Apocalypse World** moves typically resolve with a single 2d6+stat roll, with 10+ being full success, 7-9 being partial success with complication, and 6- being failure with consequences. One roll determines the entire outcome of the action.

**Extended Contests**: Multiple rolls or mechanical exchanges resolve a single conflict, with accumulation mechanics tracking progress toward resolution. Allows tactical play, dramatic reversals, and variable pacing while maintaining conflict scope.

Example: **HeroQuest Extended Contest**: Participants roll each round, comparing results to generate Resolution Points. First to reduce opponent's RP to zero wins the contest. Multiple rounds allow tactical choice (defensive actions, augments, changes of approach) while resolving the overall conflict rather than individual actions.

**Structured Conflicts**: Dedicated subsystems for specific conflict types (combat, social, chase, etc.) with their own procedures and tactical options. Provides depth and player agency while maintaining conflict resolution scope through structured beginnings, middles, and ends.

Example: **Burning Wheel Duel of Wits**: Social conflict resolved through scripted actions (Point, Feint, Dismiss, etc.) with body of argument tracking damage to positions. The entire social conflict plays out through structured exchanges until one side's position collapses, determining the overall outcome without requiring task-level resolution of individual arguments.

### Outcome Determination

**Binary Success/Failure**: Mechanics answer yes/no questions. Simple, clear, but potentially limiting in narrative flexibility. Traditional task resolution typically uses binary outcomes for each task.

**Degrees of Success**: Mechanics generate scaled outcomes (marginal/solid/critical success; failure/partial/full success). Provides nuance and reduces binary whiplash, allows meaningful consequences without total failure.

**Success with Consequences**: Even successful rolls can impose costs, complications, or trade-offs. Maintains forward momentum while adding texture and tough choices. Common in modern conflict resolution designs.

Example: **Blades in the Dark** uses position (Controlled/Risky/Desperate) and effect (Limited/Standard/Great) to determine what success and failure mean contextually. A 4-5 on a Desperate position means you succeed but face severe consequences—you get what you wanted but at significant cost.

**Negotiated Outcomes**: Players and GM actively discuss what results mean, using mechanical results as input rather than absolute determiners. Maximizes flexibility but requires social contract and good-faith negotiation.

### Stakes and Authority

**Implicit Stakes**: What's at risk is understood from fictional positioning and traditional genre expectations. Works when everyone shares assumptions, but can cause disputes when expectations differ.

**Explicit Stakes**: Games require or encourage stating what's at risk before rolling. Reduces disputes and aligns expectations, but adds procedural step and can feel artificial if not well-integrated.

**Player-Driven Stakes**: Players define what they want and risk in conflicts. Increases player agency and investment but requires careful calibration to avoid trivializing challenges or breaking setting logic.

**GM-Determined Stakes**: GM frames what's possible and what failure means. Maintains setting logic and challenge but can feel arbitrary if not transparent.

**Negotiated Stakes**: Players and GM collaboratively establish what's at risk. Balances agency and setting integrity but requires good-faith engagement and can slow play if overused.

## Design Philosophy

The choice of conflict resolution system reflects fundamental design priorities about what a game is for, what it's about, and how it distributes authority at the table. These choices cascade through every aspect of play experience.

**Pacing and Granularity**: Task resolution creates granular play where small actions matter and tactical positioning emerges from accumulated micro-decisions. This approach excels at creating tense, detailed scenarios where skill and resource management are tested through sustained engagement. Combat in task-based games becomes war-gaming-adjacent, with spatial positioning, action economy, and resource expenditure creating strategic depth. However, this granularity can bog down scenes that don't benefit from blow-by-blow resolution, leading to the common complaint that combat takes "too long" relative to its narrative significance.

Conflict resolution sacrifices tactical granularity for narrative pacing, resolving entire scenes so the game can move to the next meaningful decision point. This approach works beautifully when the outcome of a conflict matters more than the process, when the stakes are clear, and when extended resolution wouldn't add value. However, it can feel unsatisfying when players want to influence outcomes through tactical choice, and it requires different kinds of system mastery—understanding how to set stakes and frame conflicts rather than optimizing action sequences.

**Player Agency and Control**: Task resolution gives players direct control over their characters' specific actions. Players decide exactly what their character does, and mechanics adjudicate whether those specific actions succeed. This creates strong player agency at the action level—you always know what you're trying to do, even if success is uncertain.

Conflict resolution shifts agency from action control to goal articulation and stake negotiation. Players don't necessarily control specific tactics or moment-to-moment decisions, but they have more direct influence over outcomes and narrative direction. The character might lose a fight (task-level failure) but still achieve their goal (conflict-level success), or vice versa. This can feel empowering when focused on outcomes that matter, but disorienting when players want to express their characters through tactical choices.

Stakes-based resolution maximizes player agency in defining what matters, allowing players to literally negotiate what their characters risk and desire. This approach works best in games about meaningful choice and consequence, where what characters want is more important than how they get it. However, it requires players comfortable with meta-level discussion and can break immersion if overused.

**Challenge and Uncertainty**: Task resolution creates uncertainty at the action level—you don't know if your sword will hit—but outcomes can become predictable through probability management. Since each task is independent, skilled players can minimize risk through careful action selection and resource management.

Conflict resolution creates uncertainty at the outcome level—you don't know if you'll achieve your goal—but the path to that outcome is less mechanically determined. This shifts challenge from tactical optimization to strategic framing: setting appropriate stakes, choosing when to engage mechanics, and managing resources across entire conflicts rather than individual actions.

**Narrative Authority**: Task resolution traditionally concentrates narrative authority with the GM, who determines what tasks are required, sets difficulties, and interprets results in terms of fictional outcomes. Players control their characters' attempted actions; the GM controls the world's response and the meaning of mechanical results.

Conflict resolution distributes narrative authority more broadly. When a player states "I want to convince the duke to support us" and the mechanics resolve that conflict, the mechanical outcome directly determines fictional reality without requiring GM interpretation. The player's statement of intent becomes mechanically binding rather than merely suggestive.

Stakes-based resolution can go further, allowing players to propose specific outcomes (both success and failure) that become reality when mechanics support them. This makes players co-authors of the story rather than solely protagonists within it, which aligns with story game aesthetics but may feel wrong in games emphasizing GM authority and world simulation.

**Verisimilitude and Simulation**: Task resolution supports simulationist play by modeling specific actions and their probable outcomes. If your game values tactical realism, detailed causality, and emergent complexity from simple rules interactions, task resolution provides the granularity needed for those qualities to manifest.

Conflict resolution prioritizes narrative logic over causal simulation. The question isn't "would this action realistically succeed?" but rather "what outcome serves the story?" This approach excels in games emphasizing drama, theme, and narrative satisfaction over tactical realism.

**System Mastery and Learning Curve**: Task resolution rewards system mastery through optimization and tactical play. Players learn which actions are efficient, how to combine abilities, and how to manipulate probabilities. This creates satisfying depth for players who enjoy mechanical engagement.

Conflict resolution requires different expertise: understanding how to frame conflicts, set appropriate stakes, and leverage narrative positioning. The learning curve is less about mechanical optimization and more about understanding fictional leverage and negotiation skills.

## Variations Across Systems

Different games implement conflict resolution in ways that reflect their broader design goals and genre conventions:

**Traditional D&D Task Resolution**: Each significant action requires a discrete roll. Attack rolls, saving throws, ability checks, and skill checks all resolve specific tasks. Outcomes are primarily binary (success/failure) though critical hits/misses add extreme results. The system's strength lies in its tactical granularity—combat becomes a chess-like optimization puzzle where positioning, action economy, and resource management create emergent complexity. Its weakness is pacing: scenes that don't benefit from blow-by-blow resolution still receive it, and the system provides few tools for resolving complex social or investigative conflicts without reducing them to single skill checks.

**Burning Wheel's Layered Resolution**: Luke Crane's design recognizes that different conflicts warrant different mechanical engagement. Simple tasks use obstacle-based resolution—roll your skill vs. obstacle number, succeed or fail. "Let It Ride" transforms these task rolls into conflict resolution by making results persistent: once you've rolled Persuasion to convince someone, you can't just keep trying; that result stands until circumstances change. For complex conflicts, dedicated subsystems (Fight!, Duel of Wits, Range and Cover) provide tactical depth while maintaining conflict-level stakes. You're not rolling to see if one argument lands; you're engaging in a structured conflict that resolves the entire debate.

**Dogs in the Vineyard's Raise and See**: Vincent Baker's design makes conflict resolution explicitly adversarial and escalating. Players state what they want, then take turns Raising (describing action and rolling dice to threaten opponent) and Seeing (rolling dice to resist). The mechanical choices—using bigger dice, bringing in new traits, escalating from talking to physical violence—drive fictional escalation organically. You're never rolling to see if your punch lands; you're rolling to see if your opponent can withstand your escalation or must give you what you want. The system elegantly models how conflicts intensify and how costs accumulate, with players choosing how far they'll go to get what they want.

**Fate's Four Outcomes**: Fate Core structures all conflicts around four possible outcomes: Failure (result below target), Tie (result equals target), Success (result exceeds target by 1-2), and Success with Style (result exceeds target by 3+). This simple framework applies across all conflict types, with context determining what each outcome means. Conflicts track stress and consequences, but the core resolution asks "How well did you achieve your stated goal?" rather than "Did your specific action work?" The invocation mechanic adds negotiation layer—players spend fate points to make aspects relevant, effectively arguing why their character should succeed for narrative reasons supported mechanically.

**HeroQuest's Abstract Approach**: Robin Laws designed HeroQuest to resolve conflicts at whatever scope suits the moment. Simple contests resolve straightforward conflicts with a single roll, comparing results to determine outcome degree. Extended contests break complex conflicts into multiple rounds with accumulating Resolution Points, allowing tactical choices (cautious vs. aggressive approaches, augments from supporting abilities) while maintaining focus on overall conflict outcome. Group extended contests resolve team conflicts without requiring individual task resolution for each participant. The system's flexibility allows the same mechanics to resolve a quick negotiation, an extended chase scene, or a climactic battle, always asking "Who gets what they want?" rather than "What happens next?"

**Powered by the Apocalypse Moves**: PbtA games structure resolution around moves—discrete mechanical procedures triggered by fictional actions. Each move has predetermined possible outcomes (usually 10+ full success, 7-9 partial success/success with complication, 6- failure with consequences). The move structure ensures mechanical engagement resolves meaningful situations rather than trivial actions: there's no "attack" move, but rather "Seize by Force" which resolves what you take and what it costs, or "Go Aggro" which resolves whether you intimidate someone into compliance. Moves transform task-like triggers ("when you attack") into conflict-like resolutions ("you deal harm and suffer harm" or "you force them to choose: cave to your demands or suffer consequences"). The MC's agenda and principles guide how outcomes manifest fictionally, but the moves themselves determine stakes and scope.

**Blades in the Dark Position and Effect**: John Harper's design separates action type (what you're doing) from position (how dangerous it is) and effect (how much you accomplish). A single action roll might resolve an entire heist scene if you roll well with great effect, or require multiple rolls if you're working with limited effect. The system explicitly negotiates stakes through position and effect discussion—players and GM establish what success and failure mean before rolling. The distinction between Desperate, Risky, and Controlled positions makes danger transparent, while Limited, Standard, and Great effect determines narrative scope. Teamwork mechanics (Setup, Assist, Protect) resolve how allies contribute without requiring individual rolls for each participant.

**Swords of the Serpentine Clue-Gathering**: Kevin Kulp and Emily Dresner's investigative game treats clue-gathering as special case: core clues appear automatically when you look in the right place with the right ability, eliminating the possibility that failed rolls derail investigations. This isn't conflict resolution per se, but it reflects conflict resolution thinking: the interesting question isn't "Do you find the clue?" but rather "What do you do with the information?" The system's challenge rolls (actual conflicts) use a spend-pool system where players decide how much to invest, effectively setting their own difficulty and stakes.

## Impact on Play

The resolution system fundamentally shapes play experience in ways that extend far beyond mechanical procedures. Players internalize their game's resolution paradigm, developing strategies and expectations based on how mechanics engage with fiction.

In task resolution games, players learn to think tactically at the action level. D&D players develop elaborate strategies around action economy, movement, opportunity attacks, and ability combos because the granular resolution makes those micro-decisions meaningful. The play culture emphasizes optimization, system mastery, and tactical execution. Players experience satisfaction from clever action sequences and efficient resource deployment.

In conflict resolution games, players learn to think strategically at the goal level. Dogs in the Vineyard players develop sophisticated understanding of escalation dynamics and cost-benefit analysis—when to give ground, when to escalate to violence, how to frame Raises to make Seeing costly. The play culture emphasizes stake framing, narrative positioning, and meaningful choice. Players experience satisfaction from achieving goals cleverly or accepting interesting failures.

The resolution system also shapes preparation and play flow. Task resolution games often require detailed tactical maps, precise positioning, and careful tracking of action economy and resources. The GM prepares by designing tactical encounters with interesting terrain and enemy combinations. Play proceeds through careful turn-by-turn resolution, with combat scenes potentially lasting hours.

Conflict resolution games need different preparation: clear stakes, interesting complications, and meaningful opposition. GMs prepare by thinking about what NPCs want and what they'll do to get it, rather than optimizing enemy tactics. Play proceeds through rapid conflict resolution with more time spent on establishing stakes and interpreting outcomes than on mechanical procedures.

The social contract around resolution systems varies dramatically. Task resolution games often feature rules-based negotiation: "Can I get advantage? Does that enemy have cover? Do I get sneak attack?" Players advocate for mechanical benefits based on fictional positioning and rules interpretations. This creates "rules lawyer" culture where system knowledge is social capital.

Conflict resolution games feature more fiction-based negotiation: "If I invoke this aspect, could it help? What are the stakes if I fail? Can we frame this as one conflict or multiple?" Players advocate for narrative legitimacy based on fiction and theme. This creates "authorial" culture where narrative sensibility is social capital.
