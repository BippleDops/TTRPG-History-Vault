# Advancement Systems

**First Appeared In**: [[Dungeons & Dragons (1974)]]
**Year**: 1974
**Complexity**: 3/5
**Popularity**: 5/5
**Innovation Score**: 4/4

## Definition

Advancement systems are mechanical frameworks governing how characters improve, develop, and grow in power throughout campaigns. These systems determine what improvements characters gain, when they gain them, and what form those improvements take—whether sudden level-ups granting new abilities, gradual skill increases through practice, point-buy capability purchases, or narrative milestone unlocks. Advancement systems shape long-term campaign structure by defining power progression rates, capability expansion scope, and character development trajectories. They answer fundamental questions: How quickly do characters become more powerful? Do they improve across all capabilities or specialize increasingly? Does advancement come from mechanical performance, narrative progression, or time investment?

Beyond mechanical function, advancement systems embody design philosophies about player motivation and campaign structure. Level-based systems supporting vertical progression (becoming more powerful) create power fantasy where characters progress from weak beginners to godlike masters, supporting epic campaigns tracking heroes' rise from obscurity to legendary status. Skill-based systems supporting horizontal progression (becoming more versatile) create competency fantasy where characters accumulate specialized expertise, supporting campaigns about masterful professionals solving complex problems. Milestone systems treating advancement as narrative pacing tool support story-driven campaigns where mechanical power serves dramatic arc. The advancement system chosen fundamentally shapes what campaigns feel like and what players work toward.

## Historical Development

### Level-Based Advancement: Original D&D (1974)

Original D&D established level-based advancement as RPG baseline: characters accumulate experience points (XP) until reaching thresholds triggering sudden level increases granting new capabilities. Each class followed specific level progression with defined improvements at each level:

**Level Progression Example (Fighter)**:
- Level 1: 1d6 HP, +0 attack, limited equipment
- Level 4: 4d6 HP, improved attack, better equipment access
- Level 9: 9d6 HP (+3 HP per level beyond), strong attack, command armies
- Level 12: Extended HP, powerful attacks, legendary capabilities

**Key Features**:

**Discrete Levels**: Characters progressed through numbered levels (typically 1-20), with each level representing significant power tier

**Exponential XP Curves**: Each level required more XP than previous—1st to 2nd might need 2,000 XP while 10th to 11th needed 100,000 XP. This created natural campaign pacing with early rapid advancement and later slow progression

**Class-Specific Progression**: Fighters, wizards, clerics, and thieves had completely different level progressions, reinforcing class identity and mechanical distinctiveness

**Sudden Capability Jumps**: Leveling up granted immediate improvements—more HP, better attack bonuses, new spell slots, new class abilities. Characters became noticeably more powerful overnight (narratively after resting and training)

**Hit Point Scaling**: HP increased each level, creating vertical power progression where high-level characters could survive damage that instantly killed low-level characters

This system created compelling advancement fantasy: visible progress through numbered levels, anticipation of reaching next threshold, and dramatic power increases making characters feel genuinely stronger. The level system became so foundational that "level up" entered general vocabulary beyond gaming contexts.

However, level-based advancement had limitations. The discrete jumps meant characters were stagnant between levels (accumulating XP without mechanical change), then suddenly jumped in power. Classes advanced identically within their progression—all 5th-level fighters were mechanically similar regardless of play style. The system prioritized combat capabilities (HP, attack bonuses, spells) over non-combat development.

### Skill-Based Advancement: RuneQuest (1978)

*RuneQuest* introduced alternative advancement through skill-based progression where individual skills improved through use rather than wholesale level increases:

**Skill-Based Mechanics**:
- Characters had numerous skills rated as percentages
- Successfully using skill in challenging situation marked it
- At session end, marked skills rolled for improvement
- Roll over current skill rating = skill increased (typically by 1d6%)
- Characters advanced organically based on what they practiced

**Example**: A character with Climb 45% who successfully climbed difficult cliff marks Climb. At session end, they roll d100—if result is 46+, their Climb skill increases by 1d6%.

This created fundamentally different advancement experience:

**Granular Progression**: Small, frequent improvements rather than sudden level jumps created continuous development feeling

**Use-Based Growth**: Characters improved at what they did—fighters improved fighting skills, researchers improved research, pilots improved piloting. Natural specialization emerged from play style

**No Classes**: Without levels, no class-based restrictions. Characters defined entirely by skill selection and ratings, creating maximum customization

**Horizontal Progression**: Rather than becoming generally more powerful, characters became more competent at specific tasks, supporting specialist rather than superhero fantasies

The skill-based approach influenced *Call of Cthulhu*, *Basic Roleplaying*, *Elder Scrolls* video games, and numerous indie RPGs. It demonstrated advancement could focus on competency development rather than power escalation.

However, skill-based systems had problems. The use-based improvement created perverse incentives—players repeatedly used skills for advancement rather than story reasons. Skills useful in play improved while neglected skills stagnated, creating unintended specialization. The granular tracking required more bookkeeping than level systems.

### Point-Buy Advancement: GURPS (1986)

GURPS introduced point-buy advancement where characters earned character points (CP) spent purchasing improvements:

**Point-Buy Advancement**:
- Complete adventures or achieve goals = earn CP (typically 1-5 per session)
- Spend CP on any improvement: attributes, advantages, skills, removing disadvantages
- Costs scale with current capability (improving high-level skill costs more than low-level skill)
- Complete customization—spend points on anything desired

**Example**: After adventure, character earns 3 CP. Player spends 2 CP improving Sword skill from 14 to 15, and 1 CP raising Intelligence from 11 to 12.

This system provided:

**Total Flexibility**: No class restrictions or predetermined progressions—customize every aspect
**Balanced Power**: CP budgets ensured characters remained roughly balanced
**Incremental Growth**: Small regular improvements rather than sudden jumps
**Player Agency**: Players control exactly how characters develop

Point-buy advancement became standard in many point-buy systems (*Champions*, *Mutants & Masterminds*) and influenced games using "talent point" advancement within class frameworks.

However, point-buy advancement required significant system mastery. Experienced players knowing efficient CP expenditure created mechanically superior characters to novices. The flexibility sometimes caused analysis paralysis as players agonized over optimal spending. Some players found granular incremental growth less satisfying than dramatic level-up moments.

### Milestone Advancement: Modern Story Games (2000s)

Modern games increasingly used milestone advancement—characters improve at narratively appropriate moments regardless of XP accumulation:

**Milestone Approaches**:
- Advance after completing story arcs
- Advance after fixed session count (every 3-4 sessions)
- Advance when achieving character goals
- Advance at dramatically appropriate moments

**Benefits**:
- **Narrative Pacing**: Advancement serves story rather than dictating it
- **No Grinding**: Can't "farm" XP through repetitive actions
- **Simplified Tracking**: No XP bookkeeping
- **GM Control**: GM calibrates advancement speed to desired campaign length
- **Party Unity**: Entire party advances together, preventing level disparity

D&D 5th Edition adopted milestone advancement as recommended primary method, while maintaining traditional XP as option. Many modern games use exclusively milestone progression, treating advancement as narrative tool rather than mechanical reward for challenges overcome.

Critics argue milestone reduces player agency—advancement happens when GM decides rather than through player-controlled XP accumulation. Proponents counter that GM controlled XP rate anyway through encounter design, making milestone more honest about GM control while removing tracking burden.

### Horizontal vs Vertical Progression

Modern design discourse increasingly distinguishes advancement types:

**Vertical Progression** (D&D standard):
- Characters become more powerful
- Gain more HP, better bonuses, stronger abilities
- Face increasingly powerful threats
- Support power fantasy and epic campaigns

**Horizontal Progression** (Many indie games):
- Characters become more versatile, not more powerful
- Gain new capabilities without increasing power ceiling
- Face varied challenges rather than escalating threats
- Support competency fantasy and grounded campaigns

**Example Comparison**:
- Vertical: Level 1 wizard has 6 HP and 2 spells; level 20 wizard has 120 HP and reshape reality
- Horizontal: Starting wizard knows evocation magic; advanced wizard knows evocation, illusion, and divination at similar power levels

Some games blend approaches—early levels provide vertical progression establishing competency, later levels provide horizontal progression expanding versatility.

### Advancement Triggers Evolution

How players trigger advancement evolved significantly:

**Performance-Based** (Traditional): Overcome challenges, accumulate XP, earn advancement through gameplay success

**Time-Based** (Milestone): Play certain number of sessions, advancement happens automatically

**Goal-Based** (*Burning Wheel* Beliefs): Complete character goals and beliefs, advancement tied to character-driven play

**Move-Based** (PbtA): Trigger specific advancement moves through play, accumulate marks leading to improvement

**Failure-Based** (Some indie games): Advance by failing rolls, learning through adversity

Each approach incentivizes different play styles and reflects different philosophies about what should drive character development.

## Mechanical Implementation

### Advancement Triggers

**XP Accumulation** (Traditional D&D):
- Earn XP from encounters, treasure, quests
- Accumulate to threshold
- Level up when reaching threshold

**Session-Based Milestone**:
- Advance every X sessions
- Predictable, calendar-based
- Simple tracking

**Story Milestone**:
- Advance at story arc completion
- Variable timing
- Tied to narrative

**Goal Completion**:
- Define character goals
- Advance when achieving them
- Player-driven triggers

**Ability Use**:
- Mark abilities when used
- Roll for improvement
- Organic skill development

### Improvement Types

**Wholesale Level-Up** (D&D):
- Gain package of improvements
- HP increase, attack bonus, class features, spell slots
- Sudden power jump
- Follows class progression table

**Point-Buy Customization** (GURPS):
- Earn character points
- Spend on any improvement
- Granular customization
- Incremental growth

**Skill Rank Increases** (D&D 3E):
- Gain skill points per level
- Allocate to skills
- Balanced structure and customization

**Binary Feature Unlocks** (5E):
- Gain class features at specific levels
- Predefined progression
- Some customization (feat choices, subclasses)

**Talent/Feat Selection**:
- Choose special abilities from list
- Customize within framework
- Periodic selection points

### Advancement Rates

**Fast**: Level every 1-2 sessions
- Get to high-level play quickly
- Short campaigns (10-15 sessions to max level)
- Rapid power fantasy

**Medium**: Level every 3-4 sessions
- Balanced progression
- Medium campaigns (30-40 sessions to max level)
- D&D 5E default

**Slow**: Level every 5+ sessions
- Extended progression
- Long campaigns (50+ sessions to high levels)
- Savor each power tier

**Milestone**: Variable by story
- Pacing serves narrative
- Adjustable to campaign needs

### Progression Caps

**High Cap** (D&D 20 levels):
- Long progression runway
- Epic power scaling
- "Zero to hero" campaigns

**Medium Cap** (10-15 levels):
- Focused power range
- Manageable scope

**Low Cap** (5-6 levels):
- Tight power band
- Emphasize skill over power

**No Cap** (GURPS):
- Unlimited progression
- Long-term campaigns
- Potential imbalance

### Advancement Costs

**Fixed Costs** (D&D):
- Each level costs fixed XP
- Predictable progression
- No optimization decisions

**Scaling Costs** (GURPS):
- Higher skills cost more to improve
- Diminishing returns
- Encourages breadth

**Prerequisite Chains** (D&D 3E Feats):
- Advanced abilities require earlier abilities
- Creates feat trees
- Long-term planning

## Design Philosophy

### Player vs Character Advancement

**Character Advancement**: Mechanical capabilities improve—HP, skills, abilities
**Player Advancement**: Player skill improves—tactics, system mastery, problem-solving

Old-school games emphasized player advancement—clever players survived regardless of character level. Modern games emphasize character advancement—higher-level characters overcome challenges lower-level characters can't.

### Advancement as Motivation

**Primary Motivation**: Some players play primarily to advance characters, with advancement as core engagement hook

**Secondary Motivation**: Some players prioritize story, social play, or problem-solving, viewing advancement as nice but not central

Design must consider target audience's motivational priorities. Combat-focused games should emphasize advancement since power matters for combat effectiveness. Narrative games can de-emphasize advancement since story doesn't require mechanical growth.

### Power Curve Philosophy

**Steep Curve**: Dramatic power increase creates satisfying progression but risks low-level fragility and high-level imbalance

**Shallow Curve**: Maintains challenge across levels but can feel like inadequate advancement reward

**Bounded Accuracy**: 5E's approach limiting numerical growth while expanding capabilities—keeps math manageable while maintaining progression feeling

### Advancement Speed and Campaign Length

Faster advancement shortens campaigns—reaching max level in 20 sessions supports tight story arcs. Slower advancement extends campaigns—40+ sessions provides epic sagas. Milestone allows flexible calibration matching intended campaign length.

## Evolution Across Games

### D&D Advancement Evolution

**OD&D (1974)**: Level-based, exponential XP curves, class-specific
**AD&D (1977-1989)**: Maintained levels, refined XP tables
**D&D 3E (2000)**: Levels plus skill points, feat choices, customization
**D&D 4E (2008)**: Streamlined level progression, power tiers
**D&D 5E (2014)**: Simplified levels, milestone recommended, bounded accuracy

Progression from pure level system to hybrid incorporating customization while maintaining level framework.

### Alternative System Progressions

**BRP/RuneQuest**: Skill-based use-driven advancement
**GURPS**: Pure point-buy customizable advancement
**PbtA**: Playbook-specific advancement through experience marks
**Fate**: Milestone-based with aspect and skill adjustments
**OSR Games**: Slow level progression emphasizing player skill
**13th Age**: Level-based with flexible advancement triggers

## Cultural Impact

### "Leveling Up" as Cultural Phrase

"Level up" transcended gaming to describe any significant improvement or achievement in life—getting promoted at work is "leveling up," improving skills is "leveling up." This demonstrates RPG advancement's cultural penetration.

### Optimization and Min-Maxing Culture

Detailed advancement systems spawned optimization communities analyzing "optimal" advancement paths, multiclass combinations, and feat chains. This created sophisticated theorycrafting culture but sometimes reduced characters to mathematical optimization exercises.

### "Treadmill" Criticism

Video game critics adopted RPG community's "treadmill" criticism—advancement that increases player power while proportionally increasing challenge creates illusion of progress without substantive change. This sparked discussions about meaningful versus cosmetic advancement.

### Power Gamers vs Roleplayers

Advancement systems became flashpoint in "power gamer vs roleplayer" cultural division. Power gamers optimized advancement for mechanical effectiveness; roleplayers chose advancement based on character concept. This false dichotomy created unnecessary tension—both approaches are legitimate.

## Modern Usage

### Best Practices

**Communicate Advancement Rate**: Set clear expectations about how quickly characters advance

**Balance Advancement Paths**: Ensure all advancement options provide comparable value, preventing "trap" choices

**Allow Retraining**: Permit character rebuild if advancement choices don't work as expected

**Milestone for Story Campaigns**: Use milestone advancement when narrative pacing is priority

**XP for Sandbox**: Use XP when player-driven exploration and challenge-seeking is focus

**Mix Approaches**: Combine milestone (level timing) with choice (feat/ability selection)

### Contemporary Variants

**Accelerated Advancement**: Grant levels faster for short campaigns or late starts

**Slow Advancement**: Extend progression for long campaigns

**Milestone Objectives**: Define specific achievements earning advancement

**Flexible Retraining**: Allow rebuilding characters between arcs

**Horizontal Expansion**: Grant new abilities without increasing power at high levels

### House Rules

**Level Caps**: Limit maximum level for particular power level
**Free Feats**: Grant bonus feats for flavor without sacrificing advancement choices
**Skill Point Bonus**: Award extra skill points for good roleplay
**Advancement Variety**: Mix XP and milestone triggers
**Downtime Training**: Require training time for advancement
**Goal-Based XP**: Grant XP for achieving character goals beyond standard activities
