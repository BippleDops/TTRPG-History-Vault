# Dungeons and Daddies

**Format**: Audio podcast actual play
**Subtitle**: Not a BDSM Podcast
**System**: D&D 5th Edition
**First Episode**: January 16, 2019
**Cast**: Anthony Burch (DM), Freddie Wong, Matt Arnold, Will Campos, Beth May
**Status**: Ongoing (Season Two)

## Overview

Dungeons and Daddies (with the tagline "Not a BDSM Podcast") is a comedy actual play podcast that explores themes of fatherhood, family, and personal growth through the lens of Dungeons & Dragons. Launching in January 2019, the show follows four dads from our world who find themselves transported to the Forgotten Realms on a quest to rescue their sons from the clutches of the mysterious Daddy Master. What begins as a comedic premise evolves into a surprisingly emotional exploration of masculinity, parenthood, and the legacies we leave for our children.

The show is hosted by Dungeon Master Anthony Burch, an acclaimed video game writer known for his work on *Borderlands 2* and the web series *Hey Ash, Whatcha Playin'?*, alongside players Freddie Wong (filmmaker and co-founder of RocketJump), Matt Arnold (comedian and musician), Will Campos (performer and producer), and Beth May (comedian and writer). This combination of creative professionals from various entertainment backgrounds creates a unique chemistry that balances improvised comedy, genuine emotion, and thoughtful character development.

Dungeons and Daddies distinguishes itself from other actual play shows through its particular focus on fatherhood and parenting, themes rarely explored in depth within the TTRPG space. Each of the four original dads—Henry Oak (Matt Arnold), Darryl Wilson (Will Campos), Ron Stampler (Beth May), and Glenn Close (Freddie Wong)—represents different approaches to fatherhood and masculinity, and their journeys throughout the campaign force them to confront their failures, insecurities, and opportunities for growth as parents and people.

The show has achieved remarkable popularity, regularly appearing in the top charts for comedy and fiction podcasts. Its success demonstrates that actual play content can appeal to broad audiences beyond traditional TTRPG fans by focusing on universal themes and emotional storytelling while maintaining mechanical gameplay and fantasy adventure elements. The podcast's devoted fanbase, known for their extensive fan art and community engagement, has helped establish Dungeons and Daddies as one of the most successful independent actual play podcasts.

## Production History

### Origins and Development (2018-2019)

The genesis of Dungeons and Daddies came from conversations between Anthony Burch and Freddie Wong about creating an actual play podcast that would differentiate itself in an increasingly crowded market. By late 2018, the actual play boom was well underway, with shows like [[Critical Role]], [[The Adventure Zone]], and [[Not Another DnD Podcast]] demonstrating the format's potential. However, most shows focused on traditional fantasy adventure or general comedy, leaving room for more thematically focused content.

Burch and Wong conceived of a show centered around the specific theme of fatherhood, partly inspired by their own experiences and questions about parenthood. The premise—four dads from our world transported to a fantasy realm on a quest to save their kidnapped sons—provided a clear narrative framework while allowing for character exploration and thematic depth.

The duo recruited Matt Arnold, Will Campos, and Beth May to round out the cast. This group brought diverse comedic sensibilities and performance backgrounds:
- **Freddie Wong**: Known for action-comedy short films and YouTube content
- **Matt Arnold**: Experienced improviser and musician
- **Will Campos**: Theater performer with strong character work
- **Beth May**: Comedian and writer with sharp observational humor

The casting of Beth May as Ron Stampler, a soft-spoken stepfather, proved particularly inspired, as May's performance created one of the show's most beloved characters while also adding LGBTQ+ representation to the cast.

### Season One: The Quest for the Lost Dads (2019-2021)

Dungeons and Daddies premiered on January 16, 2019, with the episode "Welcome to the Forgotten Realms." The premise was immediately clear: four dads—Henry Oak (hippie nature-loving father), Darryl Wilson (emotionally repressed football-loving dad), Ron Stampler (awkward stepfather), and Glenn Close (rock-and-roll absent father)—are transported from our world to the Forgotten Realms during a trip to Disneyland when a mysterious portal appears.

In the Forgotten Realms, they discover their sons have been kidnapped by "The Forgotten," entities seeking to steal youth and innocence. To find their sons, the dads must confront various challenges, navigate a strange fantasy world, and ultimately face their own inadequacies as fathers.

The first season's structure brilliantly balanced episodic adventures with ongoing character development. Each arc typically involved the dads pursuing a lead on their sons' location while confronting situations that forced them to examine their parenting approaches:

- **Henry Oak** had to balance his peace-loving nature with the violence necessary to protect his family
- **Darryl Wilson** confronted his emotional repression and inability to communicate feelings
- **Ron Stampler** struggled with being a stepfather and earning his stepson's love and respect
- **Glenn Close** faced the consequences of his absence from his son's life and his prioritization of his music career

Throughout Season One, the show established several key elements that defined its identity:

**Thematic Depth**: Unlike many comedy actual plays, Dungeons and Daddies consistently explored serious themes about masculinity, parenting, and family. The comedy never undercut the emotional stakes; instead, humor and pathos worked together to create fully realized characters.

**Mechanical Flexibility**: While using D&D 5E as a framework, Anthony Burch freely modified or ignored rules when they interfered with narrative flow. This approach prioritized storytelling over mechanical rigor, distinguishing the show from more tactically-focused actual plays.

**Musical Elements**: The show incorporated music extensively, with original songs, character themes, and carefully selected licensed music enhancing emotional moments. Glenn's character, being a guitarist, provided natural opportunities for musical interludes.

**LGBTQ+ Representation**: The show featured LGBTQ+ characters prominently and thoughtfully, including Ron's eventual relationship with Samantha Stampler and various queer NPCs. This representation felt organic rather than performative, integrated naturally into the story.

Season One concluded in June 2021 after 69 episodes (a number the cast found endlessly amusing), providing a narratively satisfying conclusion while setting up future stories. The finale brought together threads from throughout the campaign, delivering emotional payoffs for each dad's journey while resolving the central quest.

### Between Seasons: Talking Dads and Community Building (2021-2022)

Following Season One's conclusion, the show released "Talking Dads" episodes—after-show discussions featuring the cast reflecting on the campaign, answering listener questions, and discussing their creative process. These episodes provided insight into the show's development and strengthened the connection between creators and audience.

During this period, the Dungeons and Daddies community flourished. The show's Patreon, offering bonus content including extended episodes and exclusive mini-campaigns, grew substantially. Fan artists created extensive illustrations of characters and scenes, with some artists becoming well-known within the community. The show's Discord server became a hub for fan interaction, discussion, and community organization.

The success of Season One also led to live shows across North America, with the cast performing special episodes in front of sold-out audiences. These live performances demonstrated the show's popularity and created shared experiences that strengthened community bonds.

### Season Two: The Peachyville Horror (2022-Present)

Season Two, subtitled "The Peachyville Horror," launched in August 2022 with a bold creative decision: an almost entirely new cast of characters. Set years after Season One's events, the new campaign follows the teenage sons from Season One (now young adults) plus a new character, as they navigate the strange town of Peachyville and uncover dark secrets about their fathers' legacy.

The new player characters are:
- **Normal Oak** (Matt Arnold): Henry's son, trying to live up to his father's ideals
- **Scary Marlowe** (Beth May): A goth teen dealing with demonic forces
- **Lincoln Li-Wilson** (Will Campos): Darryl's son, grappling with his father's emotional legacy
- **Taylor Swift** (Freddie Wong): Glenn's son, a confident youth hiding insecurities

This generational shift allowed the show to explore new thematic territory while building on Season One's foundation. Instead of examining fatherhood from the parents' perspective, Season Two explores how children process their parents' failures and successes, how trauma passes between generations, and how young people forge their own identities while carrying family legacies.

Season Two has also demonstrated creative evolution in several areas:

**Horror Elements**: The new season incorporates more horror themes and imagery, with the mysterious town of Peachyville hiding disturbing secrets. This tonal shift shows the show's range beyond pure comedy.

**Coming-of-Age Themes**: Replacing parenting themes with adolescent identity formation, the new season explores teenage experiences, peer relationships, and the transition to adulthood.

**Mechanical Evolution**: Anthony Burch has continued refining his approach to game mechanics, creating custom systems and modifications that serve the story while maintaining D&D's basic framework.

**Deeper Lore**: Season Two has revealed extensive backstory about Season One's events, recontextualizing earlier moments and adding layers to the show's mythology.

## Cultural Impact

### Redefining Actual Play Themes

Dungeons and Daddies' most significant cultural contribution is demonstrating that actual play podcasts can center on specific, universal themes while still delivering entertaining fantasy adventure. Before Dungeons and Daddies, most actual play shows focused on traditional fantasy themes—defeating evil, saving the world, treasure and glory—or general comedy without particular thematic focus.

By explicitly centering fatherhood and parenting, Dungeons and Daddies opened space for thematically-focused actual play content. The show proved that audiences would embrace actual play stories that used fantasy settings to explore real-world concerns and emotions. This influence can be seen in subsequent shows that have adopted thematic focuses—relationship dynamics, mental health, professional careers, etc.—rather than simply following traditional adventure narratives.

The show's exploration of masculinity and fatherhood also filled a gap in media representation. While there is extensive media about motherhood and female parenting experiences, thoughtful examination of fatherhood—particularly non-toxic masculinity and emotional vulnerability in men—is less common. Dungeons and Daddies presented complex, flawed fathers trying to do better, acknowledging failures while demonstrating growth, in ways that resonated with listeners regardless of their parenting status.

### LGBTQ+ Representation in Actual Play

Dungeons and Daddies has been notable for its natural, integrated LGBTQ+ representation. Ron Stampler's character arc, including his relationship with Samantha and his journey as a stepfather, provided positive representation of LGBTQ+ parenting. Beth May's performance as Ron avoided stereotypes while creating a deeply empathetic, fully-realized character who happened to be queer.

Season Two expanded this representation with Scary Marlowe's sexuality and various LGBTQ+ NPCs integrated throughout the story. The show's approach—treating queer characters as normal parts of the world without making their queerness their defining characteristic or source of conflict—influenced how other actual play shows approached representation.

### Podcast Success and Independence

As an independent podcast without network backing, Dungeons and Daddies' success demonstrated the viability of self-produced actual play content. The show's popularity—regularly appearing in top podcast charts and maintaining a substantial Patreon subscriber base—proved creators could build sustainable careers through actual play content without corporate support or existing media company infrastructure.

This independence allowed creative freedom while building direct relationships with audiences through Patreon, Discord, and social media. The model has inspired other creators to pursue independent actual play production, contributing to the diversification of actual play content beyond network-backed shows.

### Comedy and Emotional Storytelling Balance

Building on the foundation established by shows like [[Not Another DnD Podcast]], Dungeons and Daddies further refined the balance between comedy and emotional depth. The show demonstrated that crude humor, running gags, and absurdist comedy could coexist with genuine pathos and serious thematic exploration without either element undermining the other.

This balance has become a hallmark of successful comedy actual play, with audiences expecting both laughter and tears. The show's ability to transition from jokes about Glenn's sexual exploits to Henry's tender moments with his son, often within the same scene, created an emotional range that kept listeners engaged and invested.

### Musical Integration in Actual Play

Dungeons and Daddies' extensive use of music—both original compositions and carefully selected licensed tracks—demonstrated how audio actual play could create cinematic experiences through sound design alone. The show's music became integral to its identity, with character themes, emotional cues, and musical performances enhancing storytelling.

Glenn Close's character, being a rock musician, provided natural opportunities for musical content, including original songs performed by Freddie Wong. These musical moments became highlights for fans, with songs like "Magic Tavern" and various character themes becoming beloved elements of the show.

The show's approach to music licensing and original composition influenced other podcasts to invest in musical elements, raising production value standards across the actual play podcast space.

### Fan Community and Creative Engagement

The Dungeons and Daddies community has become one of the most creative and engaged in actual play podcasting. The volume and quality of fan art produced by the community is remarkable, with dozens of talented artists creating illustrations, comics, and animations based on the show.

The show's approach to fan engagement—regularly showcasing fan art, engaging with fans on social media, maintaining active Discord community, and hosting live shows—created a feedback loop that encouraged creative participation. Some fan artists have gained substantial followings within the community, with their character interpretations becoming iconic representations.

The community's organization includes:
- **Discord server**: Active community discussion and interaction
- **Fan art tags**: Organized social media presence for community art
- **Live show culture**: Communal experiences at live performances
- **Charity initiatives**: Community-organized charitable efforts
- **Fan content**: Podcasts, blogs, and other media discussing the show

## Notable Moments

### Season One Highlights

**"Welcome to the Forgotten Realms" (Episode 1)**: The premiere established the show's tone perfectly, introducing the four dads and their sons before immediately separating them through the portal. The character introductions efficiently communicated each dad's personality and parenting style while setting up the central quest.

**The Library Arc (Episodes 11-15)**: This extended sequence in a magical library forced the dads to confront personal truths about themselves and their parenting. The arc balanced comedy with genuine character development, particularly Henry's pacifism being tested and Ron's stepfather insecurities being explored.

**Glenn's Past (Episodes 20-25)**: Freddie Wong's portrayal of Glenn confronting his failures as a father created some of the season's most emotionally powerful moments. The revelation of Glenn's relationship with his son and his prioritization of his music career provided depth to what could have been a one-note "cool dad" character.

**Darryl's Emotional Journey (Episodes 30-40)**: Will Campos' performance as Darryl slowly opening up emotionally across multiple episodes demonstrated subtle character development. Darryl's struggle to express feelings and his eventual growth became one of the season's most satisfying arcs.

**Henry and Mercedes (Episodes 45-55)**: Matt Arnold's portrayal of Henry's relationship with the woman who killed his son (later revealed to be more complicated) explored themes of forgiveness, violence, and moral complexity. Henry's pacifism being tested repeatedly created ongoing tension.

**Ron and Samantha (Episodes 50-60)**: Beth May's development of Ron's relationship with Samantha (and the revelation of Samantha's gender identity) provided touching moments while advancing Ron's character growth. The relationship felt earned and genuine.

**The Throne of Chaos (Episodes 65-69)**: The finale brought together threads from throughout the season, with each dad facing ultimate tests of their growth. The emotional resolution of the parent-child relationships provided satisfying conclusions to year-long character arcs.

### Season Two Highlights

**"Welcome to Peachyville" (Episode 1)**: The Season Two premiere successfully introduced new characters while honoring Season One's legacy. The shift to teenage protagonists immediately established different dynamics and thematic concerns.

**The Doodler Revelation (Episodes 10-15)**: The gradual revelation of the Doodler's nature and connection to Season One events created compelling mystery while exploring how children process their parents' choices.

**Normal's Identity Crisis (Episodes 20-25)**: Matt Arnold's portrayal of Normal struggling with his father's legacy and forging his own identity explored coming-of-age themes with nuance and humor.

**Scary's Demonic Struggles (Episodes 15-30)**: Beth May's performance as Scary dealing with demonic forces while navigating teenage social dynamics balanced horror elements with character development.

**Lincoln and Grant (Episodes 25-35)**: The complex relationship between Lincoln and his stepfather Grant explored blended family dynamics and the legacy of Darryl's emotional repression.

**Generational Trauma (Ongoing)**: Season Two's exploration of how trauma and behavioral patterns pass between generations has created thematic depth that builds on Season One's foundation.

### Running Gags and Recurring Elements

**"Not a BDSM Podcast"**: The show's subtitle arose from the need to clarify the show's content given its title, becoming a running joke and part of the show's identity.

**Beth May's NPCs**: Beth May's tendency to create elaborate backstories for random NPCs became a beloved running bit, with May fully inhabiting throwaway characters.

**Freddie's Rolls**: Freddie Wong's notoriously bad dice luck became a running joke, with catastrophic failures at crucial moments creating both comedy and genuine consequences.

**The Stairs**: A running gag about the dads' various encounters with stairs in dungeons became unexpectedly prominent.

**Music References**: The show's extensive music references and Glenn's musician background created ongoing opportunities for musical comedy and references.

## Legacy and Influence

### Thematic Actual Play

Dungeons and Daddies' success with thematic focus inspired other creators to develop actual play shows centered on specific themes rather than general adventure. Shows exploring particular relationships, psychological concepts, or social issues followed Dungeons and Daddies' model of using TTRPG frameworks to examine real-world themes.

The show demonstrated that audiences wanted substantive thematic content alongside entertainment, that actual play could provide meaningful exploration of complex topics while remaining fun and engaging. This elevated actual play as a medium beyond simple entertainment into a form capable of genuine artistic and thematic expression.

### Independent Podcast Success

As one of the most successful independent actual play podcasts, Dungeons and Daddies proved that creators didn't need network backing, existing media company infrastructure, or corporate support to reach massive audiences and build sustainable careers. The show's success encouraged other creators to pursue independent production, contributing to the democratization of actual play content creation.

The show's business model—combining free podcast episodes with Patreon exclusive content, live shows, and merchandise—provided a template for other independent creators seeking to monetize actual play content while maintaining creative control.

### Comedy Actual Play Evolution

Dungeons and Daddies represents a maturation of comedy actual play, demonstrating that humor doesn't preclude emotional depth or thematic sophistication. The show's influence on comedy actual play has been substantial, with subsequent shows adopting similar balances of irreverent humor and genuine emotion.

The show proved that comedy actual play could appeal to audiences beyond comedy fans, attracting listeners interested in good storytelling regardless of genre. This broadened the potential audience for comedy actual play and encouraged more ambitious creative approaches.

### Representation and Inclusivity

The show's natural integration of LGBTQ+ characters and themes influenced how other actual play shows approached representation. Rather than treating queer characters as special cases or making queerness a source of conflict, Dungeons and Daddies normalized LGBTQ+ presence in fantasy storytelling.

This approach—representation through inclusion rather than spotlight—became increasingly common in actual play content, with shows recognizing that diverse representation could be integrated organically into stories without requiring special justification or explanation.

### Multi-Season Campaign Structure

Season Two's decision to follow the next generation of characters while maintaining connections to Season One created a model for how long-running actual play shows could refresh their content while honoring their history. This approach allowed the show to explore new themes and character dynamics without abandoning the world and relationships that made Season One successful.

The generational approach has inspired other shows to consider similar structures, using time skips and new characters to maintain freshness across multiple campaigns while building connected fictional universes.
