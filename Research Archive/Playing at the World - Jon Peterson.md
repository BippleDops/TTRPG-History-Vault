## Resource Overview

Jon Peterson's "Playing at the World" is the definitive academic history of roleplaying games from their wargaming origins through the early 1980s. Published in 2012 after seven years of research, this 720-page tome draws from primary sources, interviews, and archival materials to trace how RPGs emerged from miniature wargaming. Peterson's companion blog continues to publish deep-dive research into specific aspects of early RPG history.

The work is notable for its rigorous academic methodology, extensive footnoting (over 1,800 citations), and use of primary sources many of which had never been publicly examined before. Peterson uncovered original documents, letters, zines, and early game publications that illuminate the actual development of D&D versus the mythology that had grown around it.

## Key Content Summary

### Part I: Simulating Wars (Chapters 1-5)
- Traces wargaming from 19th-century Kriegsspiel through H.G. Wells' "Little Wars"
- Documents the rise of American miniature wargaming in the 1960s
- Details the formation of the International Federation of Wargaming and key clubs
- Explains how Dave Wesely's Braunstein games introduced referee-controlled scenarios
- Shows how Dave Arneson adapted Braunstein concepts into Blackmoor

### Part II: Simulating People (Chapters 6-9)
- Analyzes character-based gameplay emergence in the late 1960s
- Documents Arneson's Blackmoor campaign (1971-1973) in unprecedented detail
- Reveals the actual collaboration between Arneson and Gygax on D&D
- Explains Chainmail's role as the combat system foundation
- Clarifies the timeline and contributions of each designer

### Part III: Simulating Fantastic Adventures (Chapters 10-15)
- Chronicles the writing and publication of original D&D (1974)
- Documents the early growth of TSR and the D&D phenomenon
- Analyzes the spread of RPGs beyond D&D: Tunnels & Trolls, Chivalry & Sorcery, etc.
- Examines the early gaming convention scene and fanzine culture
- Details the development of Advanced D&D and the market explosion

### Blog Content
Peterson's blog extends the book with ongoing research:
- Discovery and analysis of newly-found documents
- Deep dives into specific mechanical innovations
- Biographical research on obscure early designers
- Analysis of gaming culture and community formation
- Documentation of international RPG development

## Key Quotes/Facts

**On Arneson's Contribution:**
"Arneson's Blackmoor campaign, which began in 1971, introduced nearly all the core concepts we associate with roleplaying games: persistent characters, experience points, character classes, dungeon exploration, and treasure as reward."

**On D&D's Development:**
"The original D&D was not a polished design document but rather an attempt to package the Blackmoor campaign experience into playable rules for others. This explains its notorious incompleteness - it was documenting an ongoing practice, not creating a finished system."

**On Sources:**
"Gygax's later accounts of D&D's creation, while colorful, often contradict contemporary documents from 1972-1974. Where they differ, the contemporary sources must be privileged."

**Critical Timeline Points:**
- **1967**: Dave Wesely runs first Braunstein game
- **April 1971**: Dave Arneson begins Blackmoor campaign
- **November 1972**: Arneson demonstrates Blackmoor to Gygax
- **January 1974**: D&D published by TSR

**Mechanical Innovations Traced:**
- **Hit points**: Adapted from naval wargaming ship damage points
- **Character levels**: Arneson's innovation for Blackmoor
- **Experience points**: Created to measure character growth over multiple sessions
- **Dungeon exploration**: Emerged from Arneson using Braunstein referee techniques

## Research Value

**Academic Credibility**: Peterson's work is the first truly academic treatment of RPG history, using historiographic methodology. Citations allow verification of claims, making it the gold standard for sourcing vault entries.

**Myth Correction**: Essential for correcting common misconceptions:
- "Gygax invented D&D alone" → Collaborative creation with Arneson's concepts
- "D&D appeared from nowhere" → Emerged from years of wargaming evolution
- "Early RPGs were complete games" → They were documentation of ongoing play practices
- Various timeline errors repeated in popular sources

**Connection Making**: Shows how disparate elements connected:
- Naval wargaming → Hit points
- Prussian military training → Referee/GM role
- Miniature combat → Tactical RPG play
- Science fiction fandom → Early RPG community

**Future Research**: The blog continues to publish new findings, making it a living resource that:
- Adds newly discovered documents
- Corrects previous historical accounts
- Explores international RPG development
- Documents obscure early games and designers

**Vault Integration**: Every early D&D entry should reference Peterson's findings. When creating timeline events, designer bios, or game histories for 1970s-early 1980s material, this is the primary scholarly source.

**Critical for Understanding:**
- Why D&D's original rules were so incomplete (documenting practice, not designing from scratch)
- The actual social/creative process behind early RPGs (community-driven, collaborative)
- How game mechanics were borrowed and adapted from wargaming
- The business and publishing context of early TSR

This resource is absolutely essential - it transforms TTRPG history from mythology and reminiscence into documented, sourced historical fact.
