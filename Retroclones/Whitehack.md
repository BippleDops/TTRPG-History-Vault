# Whitehack

**Publisher**: Self-published (Christian Mehrstam)
**Designer**: [[Christian Mehrstam]]
**Year**: 2013
**System**: OSR (OD&D Reimagined)
**Genre**: Universal (Fantasy default)
**License**: Proprietary
**Status**: In Print (3rd Edition 2021)

## Overview

Whitehack represents third-wave OSR's most sophisticated synthesis of old-school mechanical frameworks with story game innovations, creating system that simultaneously feels like D&D and operates on entirely different principles. Designed by Swedish game designer Christian Mehrstam, Whitehack takes OD&D's skeletal framework—six abilities, d20 rolls, hit points, levels—and rebuilds it using radical reconceptualizations that make familiar elements serve novel functions. The result is game recognizable to D&D players yet mechanically unrecognizable, achieving seemingly impossible feat of being both traditional and revolutionary.

The game's philosophical foundation rests on "double-duty mechanics"—design principle where every rule serves multiple functions simultaneously. Ability scores aren't just statistics; they're resource pools for special abilities. Classes aren't mechanical straitjackets; they're conceptual frameworks for defining capabilities. Hit points don't just measure injury; they're currency for narrative control. This multifunctionality creates remarkable mechanical density where slim rulebook (64 pages in 3rd edition) provides framework matching or exceeding much longer games' capability through elegant reuse of elements.

The three-class framework—Deft, Strong, Wise—exemplifies Whitehack's reconceptualization approach. These aren't classes in traditional sense with fixed abilities and prescribed progressions. They're archetypes defining how characters interact with mechanics. Deft characters define "groups" representing affiliations, species, or vocations, gaining mechanical benefits when acting appropriately to group identity. Strong characters attach descriptive words to attacks and defenses, creating custom combat styles. Wise characters define "miracles" they can perform, creating bespoke magic systems. This framework enables any character concept—from traditional fantasy archetypes to contemporary settings to science fiction—through same three-class structure.

The slot system transforms resource management into unified framework serving multiple purposes. Characters have slots based on level, usable for various functions: carrying items (encumbrance), purchasing advantages in conflicts (auctions), fueling special abilities (character powers), or negotiating outcomes (damage mitigation). This consolidation means players manage single resource pool rather than tracking separate systems for equipment, spell slots, special abilities, and other traditional categories. The elegance recalls video game design where single "energy" mechanic powers diverse capabilities.

The auction system for conflicts introduces controlled narrative bidding unprecedented in OSR games. When outcomes matter significantly, players can spend HP to improve chances or narrate advantages. This expenditure represents not injury but effort, determination, and risk-taking. Mechanically, it creates dramatic tension as players weigh certain success now (spending resources) versus conserving for later. Philosophically, it grants players narrative control through resource spending, importing story game principles into traditional OSR framework.

The damage system inverts conventional assumptions about hit points. In most RPGs, HP measures how much punishment character withstands before falling. In Whitehack, HP represents capacity for action—depleting through exertion, risk-taking, and yes, injury, but conceptualized as overall resources rather than wound tracking. This reconceptualization makes HP useful beyond combat: players might spend HP for non-combat advantages, knowing they're depleting same pool that protects from damage. The unification creates meaningful choices about resource allocation across all play activities.

## Historical Context

Whitehack emerged in 2013 during crucial period when OSR grappled with relationship to story games movement and contemporary indie design. First-wave retroclones had established OSR's core project: preserving classic D&D mechanics and play styles. However, by 2013, designers increasingly asked whether OSR principles could incorporate innovations from story games, indie RPGs, and European design traditions without compromising old-school identity. Whitehack answered affirmatively, demonstrating that narrative mechanics, player empowerment, and GM-less techniques could coexist with OSR frameworks.

Christian Mehrstam brought unique perspective combining Swedish gaming culture (which embraced narrative and experimental play earlier than Anglo-American scenes), programming background (thinking about elegant code), and deep familiarity with both traditional RPGs and story games. His design philosophy emphasized parsimony and multifunctionality—goals familiar in software engineering applied to game design. Whitehack emerged from asking: how few mechanics could generate how much gameplay variety? What's most compressed framework enabling maximum expression?

The decision to pursue radical reconceptualization rather than incremental improvement distinguished Whitehack from contemporary retroclones. Where most OSR games simplified D&D or adapted it toward specific settings, Mehrstam asked deeper questions: why does D&D use these specific mechanics? Could different implementations serve same purposes better? What if we kept D&D's surface (d20, abilities, classes, levels) while completely rebuilding underlying logic? This approach risked alienating both traditionalists (who wanted D&D preserved) and revolutionaries (who rejected D&D entirely), but attracted designers interested in "middle path."

The 2013 timing positioned Whitehack amid broader indie RPG conversations about "innovation through constraint." Designers increasingly recognized that tight constraints—few pages, minimal dice, simple mechanics—could drive creativity rather than limiting it. Whitehack exemplified this through its compressed page count and mechanically minimal but conceptually rich framework. The approach aligned with contemporary design trends across tabletop gaming, from minimalist card games to tight board game designs.

The initial release as premium small-format hardcover distinguished Whitehack from typical OSR production. Rather than digest-sized staple-bound books or PDF-first releases, Mehrstam produced pocket-sized hardcover with sewn binding and high-quality paper. This presentation positioned Whitehack as design object and conversation piece, something to be treasured rather than merely used. The physical premium reflected confidence in design quality and targeted audience valuing elegance and craftsmanship.

The Swedish gaming culture influence manifested in Whitehack's holistic approach. Nordic game design traditions emphasized player agency, collaborative storytelling, and GM techniques supporting rather than dictating narratives. While Whitehack maintained OSR's referee authority and deadly consequences, it incorporated Nordic sensibilities about negotiated outcomes, character investment, and meaningful player input. This cultural hybridity created distinctive flavor—recognizably OSR yet different in feel from Anglo-American designs.

The modest initial release without marketing blitz relied on design quality and community discovery. Mehrstam didn't run Kickstarter or advertise extensively—he made game available through traditional distribution and let quality speak for itself. Early adopters recognized sophistication, wrote enthusiastic reviews, and evangelized through blogs and forums. This organic growth validated "build it well and they'll find it" approach while creating devoted community invested in Whitehack's success through personal discovery rather than marketing persuasion.

## What It Clones

Whitehack clones Original D&D's conceptual architecture while reimagining nearly every specific implementation. The surface resembles OD&D: six ability scores, three classes, d20 resolution, armor class, hit points, levels, saving throws, spell-like abilities. However, each element functions differently, creating game that feels like D&D while operating on distinct principles.

The six ability scores (Strength, Dexterity, Constitution, Intelligence, Wisdom, Charisma) retain D&D's familiar framework and 3-18 range (rolled 3d6). However, they serve triple duty: determining bonuses for relevant actions (traditional), providing target numbers for saving throws (inverted from D&D), and functioning as resource pools for special abilities (novel). This triplication means abilities matter constantly rather than being occasionally-relevant statistics.

The three-class structure recalls OD&D's Fighting Man, Magic-User, Cleric but reconceptualizes what "class" means. Rather than prescribing specific abilities and restrictions, Whitehack's classes (Deft, Strong, Wise) provide frameworks for players to define capabilities. A Strong character might be traditional fighter, superhero, or vehicle—determined by player description rather than prescribed abilities. This freeform approach recalls Risus or Over the Edge while maintaining D&D's familiar class structure.

Combat uses d20 attack rolls against Armor Class superficially matching D&D. However, AC calculation includes class features, descriptive tags, and situation modifiers rare in traditional D&D. More significantly, the auction system enables players to increase attack effectiveness or defensive strength by spending HP, introducing bidding mechanic foreign to traditional D&D but aligned with old-school emphasis on player skill and resource management.

Magic in Wise class recalls D&D's spellcasting but operates through freeform "miracles" rather than spell lists. Wise characters define areas of supernatural influence ("fire," "healing," "illusion," "death") and can perform relevant effects by succeeding at ability checks and spending HP. This approach captures D&D magic's versatility while avoiding Vancian memorization system, lengthy spell descriptions, and mechanical complexity. The result feels magical while requiring minimal rules text.

Character advancement through levels follows D&D's structure: gain XP through treasure and adventure, accumulate XP to gain levels, receive benefits at each level. However, level benefits focus on additional slots and improved ability scores rather than new capabilities, maintaining relatively flat power curve. This bounded progression preserves OD&D's relative lethality across level range while avoiding high-level play's superheroic power escalation.

The saving throw framework inverts D&D's implementation. Rather than rolling d20 and adding modifiers to beat target numbers, characters roll under abilities modified by situation. Success means avoiding negative outcome. This "roll under" approach became common in OSR but Whitehack implements it particularly cleanly, with abilities serving as saving throws directly rather than requiring conversion tables.

The equipment and encumbrance system uses slots like many contemporary OSR games but integrates with other slot uses. Rather than separate encumbrance and ability resource tracking, Whitehack unifies through single slot pool. This compression maintains resource management challenge central to old-school play while reducing bookkeeping overhead and creating interesting choices about slot allocation.

## Innovations and Differences

The freeform class system represents Whitehack's most radical innovation. Rather than selecting from prescribed classes with fixed abilities, players choose archetype (Deft, Strong, Wise) then define what that means for their character. A Deft character becomes member of "groups"—species, professions, affiliations, or any categorization—gaining mechanical benefits when acting according to group identity. A player might define groups as "urban thieves' guild," "elven heritage," and "former soldier," gaining bonus when relevant. This system enables any character concept through same mechanical framework.

The Strong class's attack/defense words transform combat optimization into creative wordplay. Strong characters attach descriptive words ("precise," "brutal," "defensive," "quick") to attacks or defenses, gaining mechanical benefits when utilizing those approaches. This encourages tactical creativity—players describe how they fight rather than selecting from feat lists—while maintaining mechanical teeth through actual bonuses. The system bridges OSR's "describe actions, roll dice" and tactical gaming's "choose abilities for effect."

The Wise miracles system provides freeform magic without requiring spell lists or comprehensive effect descriptions. Wise characters define spheres of influence (concepts they can manipulate supernaturally) and vocations (magical traditions they belong to). When attempting miracles, they describe desired effects, referee assesses difficulty, player rolls and potentially spends HP for success. This open-ended approach enables any magical effect referee deems appropriate while maintaining resource costs and failure chances preventing magic from trivializing challenges.

The slot economy unifying encumbrance, special abilities, auctions, and character powers exemplifies double-duty mechanics at finest. Single resource pool serves multiple functions: carrying equipment, fueling extraordinary abilities, bidding in conflicts, representing capacity. This consolidation means players make meaningful trade-offs across previously separate systems—carry more equipment or save slots for powers? Use slot for combat advantage or preserve for later?

The auction system for contested actions introduces controlled narrative bidding into OSR framework. When outcomes truly matter, players can spend HP to improve chances—representing determination, skill application, or dramatic focus. This spending isn't injury (though it uses same HP pool that absorbs damage) but effort allocation. The mechanic grants players narrative control through resource management, importing story game principles while maintaining OSR's deadly stakes and resource depletion.

The HP-as-resources reconceptualization transforms combat's purpose and pacing. Rather than HP measuring only injury capacity, they represent overall capability to act decisively, take risks, and push beyond normal limits. Combat depletes HP through injury, but non-combat actions might also spend HP for advantages. This unification makes HP relevant constantly rather than only during combat while maintaining their traditional function as buffer before death.

The fast character creation enables session-zero play rivaling even [[Into the Odd]]'s rapidity. Roll abilities, choose class archetype, define groups/words/vocations as appropriate, note starting equipment, begin playing. The freeform nature means players can create any character concept without flipping through class descriptions or feat lists—just define what character does, map to appropriate archetype, play.

The explicit referee guidance about negotiating outcomes with players imports story game GMing techniques into OSR framework. Whitehack encourages referees to discuss challenges with players, negotiate reasonable approaches, and collaborate on exciting outcomes rather than unilaterally determining everything. This guidance, unusual in OSR games emphasizing referee authority, creates space for collaborative storytelling while maintaining referee's final authority.

The integration of concepts into mechanics transforms abstract ideas into playable elements. Deft characters' groups might be abstract concepts ("night," "betrayal," "music") rather than literal organizations, gaining mechanical benefits when acting thematically appropriate. This enables playing themes mechanically—character with "revenge" group gains bonuses when pursuing vengeance—bridging narrative focus with mechanical rewards.

## Community and Culture

The Whitehack community, smaller than major OSR games but intensely devoted, coalesced around sophisticated design appreciation and experimental play. Community members tend toward experienced players who've explored multiple systems, recognize Whitehack's innovations, and value elegant design. Discussions emphasize creative applications of freeform systems, sharing interesting group/word/vocation concepts, and exploring boundary cases where Whitehack's loose framework requires collaborative interpretation.

The community culture emphasizes collaborative referee-player relationships unusual in OSR spaces. Where many OSR communities stress antagonistic or neutral referee stances ("the dungeon doesn't care about you"), Whitehack discussions emphasize negotiation, communication, and collaborative outcome determination. This reflects Scandinavian gaming culture influence and attracts players interested in story game sensibilities within OSR framework.

The international community, particularly strong in Europe, brings multilingual and culturally diverse perspectives. Whitehack's Swedish origins, European distribution, and cultural hybridity attract players beyond Anglo-American OSR mainstream. Discussions occur across languages and cultural contexts, creating cosmopolitan atmosphere distinct from US-centric OSR spaces. This diversity enriches community by introducing design perspectives, play preferences, and cultural assumptions from varied gaming traditions.

The design-focused community attracts game designers and hobbyist creators studying Whitehack as design exemplar. Many community members are themselves designers analyzing how Mehrstam achieved such mechanical density through double-duty mechanics and elegant consolidation. This analytical community produces sophisticated discussions about design philosophy, mechanical implementation, and theoretical principles beyond typical play reports or house rules.

The third-party content creation remains modest compared to OGL-licensed games but includes quality supplements and adaptations. Without open licensing, commercial third-party publishing is limited, but fan content addressing various settings, optional rules, and play advice circulates through community channels. The proprietary licensing creates tighter community focused on core game rather than fragmenting across numerous derivatives.

The actual play scene, while smaller than games with larger mainstream appeal, showcases Whitehack's flexibility. Streams and podcasts demonstrate system supporting diverse genres and play styles—from traditional fantasy dungeon-crawling to contemporary horror to science fiction. These demonstrations prove Whitehack's universal claims while showing how freeform systems translate to actual play.

## Legacy and Influence

Whitehack's legacy centers on demonstrating OSR mechanics could incorporate story game innovations without compromising old-school identity. The game proved that narrative control mechanics, freeform character definition, and collaborative storytelling techniques could coexist with high lethality, resource management, and referee authority. This validation encouraged subsequent designers to explore hybrid approaches rather than treating OSR and story games as incompatible paradigms.

The double-duty mechanics principle influenced how designers thought about rules efficiency. Rather than adding subsystems for each need, designers recognized existing mechanics could serve multiple purposes through reconceptualization. This lesson appears across post-Whitehack designs treating mechanical parsimony as virtue and multifunctionality as goal.

The freeform class implementation inspired numerous "build your own class" systems in subsequent OSR games. Designers recognized that loose frameworks enabling player definition could provide more character variety than exhaustive class lists while requiring less rules text. The principle appears in games like Macchiato Monsters and various third-wave OSR designs embracing freeform character creation.

The slot economy consolidation influenced resource management system designs broadly. Designers recognized that unified resource pools serving multiple purposes created interesting trade-offs while reducing bookkeeping. This principle influenced inventory systems, ability resources, and other mechanical areas across indie RPGs.

The auction/bidding system for conflicts proved more influential in European design scenes than Anglo-American OSR. Nordic and French designers particularly embraced spending resources for narrative control, integrating similar mechanics into various games. The Anglo-American OSR remained more skeptical, viewing resource-spending-for-outcome as insufficiently "old-school," but individual designers recognized merit and adapted principles.

The HP-as-effort reconceptualization influenced how subsequent games conceptualized hit points. Rather than purely injury abstraction, designers explored HP as general capability, resource, or narrative currency. This philosophical shift appeared in various forms across indie RPG designs grappling with HP's meaning beyond wound tracking.

The influence on The Black Hack and subsequent "X-Hack" games is direct though debated. The Black Hack's usage die mechanic and some implementation details show Whitehack influence, though the relationship remains underacknowledged. This invisible influence characterizes much of Whitehack's legacy—ideas appearing in subsequent games without explicit citation, filtering through community consciousness and emerging in new contexts.

The small-format premium presentation influenced indie RPG publishing aesthetics. Subsequent games increasingly appeared as pocket hardcovers, recognizing format's appeal for quality-focused audiences. This premium-small approach became recognized category balancing portability with perceived value.

The third edition release in 2021 refined rather than revolutionized, demonstrating confidence in core design. Mehrstam clarified ambiguities, expanded examples, improved organization, but maintained fundamental mechanics. This iterative refinement validated original vision while incorporating years of play feedback—model for how minimalist games can evolve without feature creep.
