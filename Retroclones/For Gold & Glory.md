# For Gold & Glory

For Gold & Glory (FG&G), designed and self-published by Justen Brown in 2015, represents the OSR movement's most ambitious and comprehensive retroclone project—a complete recreation of Advanced Dungeons & Dragons 2nd Edition spanning over 400 pages of densely packed rules, tables, and procedures. Where earlier retroclones targeted streamlined B/X or original D&D, For Gold & Glory tackled 2nd Edition's sprawling complexity: extensive character customization through kits and proficiencies, detailed combat systems, comprehensive spell lists, intricate treasure generation, and elaborate subsystems covering everything from psionics to mass combat. By successfully recreating 2E's full scope under the Open Game License, Brown demonstrated that retroclones could preserve even the most complex RPG systems while proving that AD&D 2nd Edition's distinct design philosophy—often overshadowed by 1st Edition nostalgia and 3rd Edition innovation—deserved preservation and continued play.

## What It Emulates

For Gold & Glory recreates Advanced Dungeons & Dragons 2nd Edition as presented in the 1989 Player's Handbook and Dungeon Master's Guide, incorporating elements from subsequent supplements and splatbooks that became standard 2E play. This edition, spanning 1989-2000, represented D&D's mature complexity before d20 System streamlining—a comprehensive ruleset emphasizing character customization, detailed procedures, and modular subsystems that groups could adopt or ignore based on campaign preferences.

Characters use the six standard ability scores (Strength, Intelligence, Wisdom, Dexterity, Constitution, Charisma) with 2E's specific generation methods including 3d6 in order, 4d6 drop lowest, or point-buy systems. Ability scores determine modifiers affecting combat, skills, and class capabilities, with exceptional Strength for Fighter-types (scores like 18/01-18/00) providing graduated bonuses. These scores serve as prerequisites for classes and kits, creating gateway requirements that 2E used to balance powerful character options.

Character classes appear in 2E's four-group structure: Warrior (Fighter, Paladin, Ranger), Wizard (Mage, Specialist Wizards), Priest (Cleric, Druid), and Rogue (Thief, Bard). Each class receives distinct combat progression, saving throws, special abilities, and level advancement rates. Multi-classing (for demi-humans) and dual-classing (for humans) enable complex character combinations, while level caps restrict demi-human advancement in specific classes—legacy balance mechanism from earlier editions.

The kit system constitutes 2E's signature customization framework, absent from earlier D&D editions and greatly simplified in later ones. Kits modify base classes with specialized abilities, restrictions, and flavor—the Fighter might become Gladiator, Swashbuckler, or Cavalier; the Wizard might specialize as Transmuter or Wild Mage; the Cleric might follow specific mythos with unique spell access and restrictions. FG&G implements this system comprehensively, providing extensive kit selection that defined 2E character creation.

Proficiency systems provide 2E's skill framework, distinguishing between weapon proficiencies (determining combat capabilities and penalties) and non-weapon proficiencies (covering skills like Riding, Tracking, Engineering, or Disguise). Characters invest proficiency slots gained at creation and through advancement, creating mechanical definition for capabilities that earlier D&D handled through class features or freeform adjudication. This system balances structure with flexibility, providing mechanical framework while allowing referee discretion.

Combat uses 2E's specific implementation of AD&D mechanics. THAC0 (To Hit Armor Class 0) determines attack success against descending AC values, with lower AC representing better protection. Attacks roll d20, subtract result from THAC0, and compare to target's AC—hit occurs if AC reached or surpassed. Initiative uses d10 with modifiers for weapon speed and spell casting time, creating tactical considerations around weapon choice. Combat rounds last one minute (ten six-second segments), with specific timing for movement, attacks, and spell completion.

Magic follows 2E's Vancian framework with school specialization for Wizards and sphere access for Priests. Specialist Wizards focus on specific schools (Evocation, Necromancy, Illusion, etc.) gaining extra spells and bonuses while prohibited from opposing schools. Priests access spells from granted spheres (major and minor access) based on deity or philosophy, creating divine spellcasters with distinct capabilities beyond generic Cleric. Spell components, casting times, and complex spell interactions receive detailed treatment.

Saving throws use 2E's five categories (Paralyzation/Poison/Death Magic, Rod/Staff/Wand, Petrification/Polymorph, Breath Weapon, Spell) with class-based tables showing target numbers on d20. These saves improve with level at rates specific to each class, maintaining AD&D's differentiated defensive progression.

The extensive subsystems covering followers and strongholds, mass combat, siege warfare, psionics, weather, economics, and campaign management all appear with varying degrees of detail. FG&G preserves 2E's modular approach where referees adopt subsystems matching campaign needs rather than using everything simultaneously.

## How It Differs

While For Gold & Glory maintains extraordinary fidelity to AD&D 2nd Edition's mechanics and scope, Justen Brown's implementation involves necessary modifications, clarifications, and organizational choices that distinguish it from its source material.

The most significant changes address OGL compliance requirements. Trademarked monster names become generic equivalents: Beholders become Observers, Mind Flayers become Illithids, Yuan-ti become Snake-men. Spell names referencing specific campaign settings (particularly Greyhawk) receive neutral replacements. Deity-specific references become generic religious frameworks. These changes, legally required to avoid Product Identity infringement, create subtle flavor differences while maintaining mechanical equivalence.

Organization improves on 2E's sometimes scattered presentation. AD&D 2E spread rules across Player's Handbook, Dungeon Master's Guide, and numerous supplements, sometimes presenting related mechanics in widely separated locations. FG&G consolidates rules into coherent single volume, grouping related systems logically and providing comprehensive indices and cross-references. This reorganization makes specific rules easier to locate despite FG&G's massive scope.

Clarifications address ambiguities and contradictions in 2E source material. AD&D 2E accumulated content over eleven years from multiple authors with varying interpretations of core mechanics. FG&G standardizes resolution mechanics, eliminates contradictions, and provides definitive rulings for edge cases that 2E left unclear. These clarifications don't change fundamental mechanics but remove uncertainties that plagued 2E play.

The writing style updates 2E's occasionally dated or unclear prose. Where 2E assumed familiarity with AD&D conventions and used gaming jargon without explanation, FG&G provides clearer exposition suitable for readers potentially unfamiliar with AD&D tradition. Examples illustrate complex procedures, organization aids navigation, and modern language replaces archaic expressions.

Kit selection receives careful curation, incorporating kits from Complete Handbooks series and other supplements while excluding obviously unbalanced or campaign-specific options. This selective inclusion provides extensive character options without overwhelming players or including every 2E kit ever published (hundreds across all supplements). Brown's curation reflects playtesting and community feedback about which kits provide meaningful choices versus problematic power levels.

Optional rules receive explicit designation rather than being scattered throughout text. 2E often presented optional subsystems without clearly marking them as non-core, leading to confusion about which rules were essential versus modular. FG&G clearly labels optional systems, helping referees understand what's necessary for basic play versus campaign-specific additions.

The psionics system receives streamlined treatment compared to 2E's notoriously complex implementation. While preserving psionic combat, power points, and psychic abilities, FG&G simplifies some procedures that 2E made unnecessarily convoluted. This balance maintains psionics' distinctiveness while improving usability for groups wanting to include psychic powers.

Production as single PDF volume rather than multiple hardbacks changes FG&G's practical usage. Where AD&D 2E required multiple books and cross-referencing between them, FG&G provides comprehensive single-source reference. The digital format enables instant searching and hyperlinking, improving usability over print-only originals. However, the 400+ page length makes complete reading daunting and printing expensive.

## Legal Framework

For Gold & Glory operates under the Open Game License (OGL) version 1.0a, using the d20 System Reference Document as legal foundation while carefully navigating 2E's specific content. Unlike B/X or OD&D where most content could be reproduced mechanically, 2E presented challenges through extensive trademarked names, setting-specific material, and creative content designated Product Identity.

Brown distinguished meticulously between mechanics (Open Game Content) and creative elements (Product Identity). Game mechanics including ability scores, attack rolls, saving throws, spell mechanics, and resolution systems could be reproduced freely. However, specific monster names, spell names referencing Greyhawk deities or wizards, campaign setting details, and creative descriptions required replacement with generic or new terminology.

The Section 15 acknowledges sources through d20 SRD back to D&D creators, establishing legal provenance. However, 2E's relationship to d20 SRD proved less direct than B/X or OD&D—3E diverged significantly from 2E in many mechanics, requiring more original development to accurately recreate 2E's specific implementations rather than simply using SRD content with minor modifications.

FG&G designates its rules text as Open Game Content, allowing third-party publishers to create compatible products using FG&G's mechanical formulations. This openness enables ecosystem development around 2E-style gaming, providing legal framework for adventures, supplements, and settings compatible with FG&G.

The compatibility statement allows publishers to declare products "compatible with For Gold & Glory" without fees or licensing beyond OGL compliance. This permissive approach mirrors other retroclones' community-friendly licensing, prioritizing ecosystem growth over revenue extraction or brand control.

Brown's free PDF distribution model—FG&G is available as no-cost download—removes financial barriers while proving sustainability through optional print-on-demand sales and community support. This freemium approach builds audience through accessibility while enabling those who value physical books to purchase print editions.

The 2023 OGL crisis particularly threatened comprehensive retroclones like FG&G. The enormous development effort required to create 400+ page rule system meant conversion to alternative licensing would be costly and time-consuming. Brown joined broader OSR community in opposing OGL 1.1, recognizing that retroclone viability depended on OGL perpetuity. The eventual Creative Commons protection for OGL 1.0a secured FG&G's long-term future.

## Reception

For Gold & Glory received enthusiastic reception from AD&D 2nd Edition fans who found vindication in comprehensive retroclone of often-overlooked edition. The detailed recreation demonstrated that 2E deserved preservation and continued play alongside more-celebrated B/X and AD&D 1E, validating the preferences of players who considered 2E their definitive D&D experience.

Players praised FG&G's thoroughness and mechanical fidelity. The complete implementation of kits, proficiencies, and subsystems enabled genuine 2E play experience without hunting down out-of-print books or navigating scattered supplements. Long-time 2E referees appreciated having comprehensive single-source reference that consolidated material from dozens of original sources.

The kit system's inclusion earned particular appreciation. Character customization through kits defined 2E identity for many players, distinguishing it from 1E's simpler classes and 3E's feat-based customization. FG&G's preservation of extensive kit options enabled the detailed character creation that 2E fans valued while organizing options more clearly than original Complete Handbooks series.

Free PDF availability proved crucial for adoption. AD&D 2E books were out-of-print and often expensive on secondary markets, creating barriers for new players discovering 2E or veterans wanting to resume play. FG&G's free download eliminated these obstacles, enabling immediate play without financial investment or hunt for vintage books.

Some criticism focused on overwhelming complexity for new players. FG&G's 400+ pages of dense rules intimidated readers unfamiliar with AD&D 2E, who found simpler retroclones like Labyrinth Lord or Swords & Wizardry more accessible. However, defenders argued that comprehensiveness was precisely the point—FG&G served players specifically wanting 2E's detail and customization rather than streamlined alternatives.

The single-volume PDF format received mixed assessment. Players appreciated comprehensive single-source reference and digital searchability, but found 400+ pages difficult to navigate without robust bookmarking and indexing. Print-on-demand copies proved expensive due to length, making physical ownership costly compared to slimmer retroclones.

Community adoption remained smaller than B/X or OD&D retroclones, reflecting 2E's smaller dedicated fanbase within OSR movement. However, FG&G successfully served its target audience—2E enthusiasts seeking legally available, comprehensive ruleset enabling continued 2E play and development.

Third-party publishers slowly embraced FG&G compatibility, producing adventures and supplements targeting 2E-style play. While ecosystem remained modest compared to Labyrinth Lord or OSE, FG&G demonstrated that even complex retroclones could support third-party development and community content creation.

Critical recognition acknowledged FG&G's achievement in comprehensive system recreation. Reviews praised Brown's thoroughness, organization improvements, and preservation of 2E's distinct design philosophy. The project validated retrocloning as preservation methodology for complex multi-book systems, not just streamlined classic editions.

## Publishing Ecosystem

For Gold & Glory's publishing ecosystem remained modest compared to B/X retroclones, reflecting both 2E's smaller dedicated fanbase and FG&G's later release when OSR movement had largely coalesced around earlier editions. However, the ecosystem demonstrated that comprehensive complex retroclones could support creative community and third-party development.

Official support from Brown included supplements, adventures, and additional content expanding FG&G beyond core rules. These releases demonstrated ongoing development and commitment to supporting FG&G as living system rather than static preservation project. The supplements addressed gaps in core rules and provided additional options for customization and campaign development.

Third-party publishers created FG&G-compatible adventures and supplements, targeting audience seeking 2E-style complexity and character options. Small presses produced dungeon crawls, wilderness campaigns, and urban adventures designed for FG&G mechanics while maintaining compatibility with original 2E materials. This dual compatibility—modern FG&G and vintage 2E—provided value to customers owning collections of original 2E adventures.

Conversion guides helped players adapt content between FG&G and other systems. Community members created resources for converting B/X adventures to 2E-style play, adapting 3E content backward to FG&G, and even translating between FG&G and AD&D 1E. These guides expanded available content beyond FG&G-specific products while demonstrating community's technical knowledge and collaborative spirit.

Community contributions included character sheets, reference cards, random generators, and play aids. Fans created tools supporting FG&G play while sharing them freely through forums, blogs, and file-sharing sites. This gift economy complemented commercial ecosystem and built community goodwill.

Digital tools including character generators and automated calculators emerged to handle FG&G's complex calculations for proficiencies, THAC0, and ability score effects. These tools made 2E's mathematical complexity more manageable, addressing valid criticism that manual calculation slowed gameplay. Automation improved FG&G's usability without simplifying mechanics that complexity-oriented players valued.

Virtual tabletop implementations enabled online FG&G play, expanding potential player base beyond local groups. VTT modules automated rules lookups, calculations, and dice rolling while providing shared virtual space for remote play. Online play proved particularly valuable for 2E-focused community where local groups might struggle to find enough players sharing edition preference.

Print-on-demand through DriveThruRPG enabled physical copies despite single-designer indie publication. While print costs remained higher than slimmer retroclones, on-demand model made physical books available without inventory investment or distribution deals. Some players invested in quality bindings through specialty printing services, creating premium physical editions rivaling original AD&D 2E production quality.

Economic sustainability proved challenging for single-designer project producing free core rules. Brown sustained FG&G through optional print sales, supplement revenue, and community support rather than core rulebook profit. This model worked at modest scale but didn't enable full-time development or professional publishing infrastructure. However, sustainability at any scale validated FG&G's viability and proved that even complex retroclone projects could maintain long-term presence.

The ecosystem also included crossover with broader AD&D 2E community. Players using original 2E books often referenced FG&G for clarifications, organized tables, or consolidated rules. This symbiotic relationship between vintage materials and modern retroclone benefited both communities while demonstrating retroclones' value beyond complete replacement of originals.

## Related Retroclones

For Gold & Glory exists within constellation of AD&D retroclones and broader OSR systems, with its 2E focus creating unique position within D&D retroclone spectrum.

OSRIC (2006) targets AD&D 1st Edition rather than 2E, representing earlier more complex D&D edition. Where FG&G emphasizes character customization through kits and proficiencies, OSRIC focuses on 1E's specific mechanical implementations and Gary Gygax's design philosophy. The choice between them reflects edition preference within AD&D family—1E's Gygaxian approach versus 2E's modular customization.

Labyrinth Lord (2007) and Old School Essentials (2018) target streamlined B/X rather than comprehensive AD&D, representing opposite end of D&D complexity spectrum from FG&G. Where B/X retroclones emphasize simple, fast play, FG&G provides detailed customization and complex subsystems. The comparison illustrates fundamental division in OSR between simplicity advocates and complexity enthusiasts.

Adventurer Conqueror King System (2012), while not strict retroclone, shares FG&G's willingness to embrace complexity in service of specific campaign goals. Both systems provide detailed economic systems, domain management rules, and campaign-level mechanics absent from simpler retroclones. ACKS and FG&G appeal to overlapping audiences valuing mechanical depth and systematic campaign procedures.

Hackmaster (2001/2012), though technically parody initially and later distinct game, shares heritage as 2E-influenced complex system. Both FG&G and Hackmaster demonstrate continued appeal of 2E-style gaming while implementing different approaches—FG&G as faithful recreation, Hackmaster as satirical evolution. The comparison shows different paths for preserving 2E legacy.

Castles & Crusades (2004) represents alternative approach to unifying AD&D and d20 mechanics through SIEGE engine. Where FG&G faithfully recreates 2E complexity, C&C streamlines AD&D-style play through unified resolution mechanic. The contrast reflects different design philosophies: preservation versus modernization.

Fantasy Craft (2009) and 13th Age (2013), while not retroclones, appeal to similar audiences seeking detailed character customization and tactical combat depth. These modern games compete with FG&G for players valuing mechanical complexity, illustrating retroclone challenge of attracting players when modern alternatives exist.

The relationship between FG&G and broader OSR movement proves complex. FG&G's thoroughness and complexity contradict OSR's frequent association with rules-light simplicity and "rulings over rules." However, FG&G demonstrates that "old-school" encompasses diverse design approaches including comprehensive detailed systems, not just minimalist frameworks. This expansion of OSR's conceptual boundaries enriched movement and validated diverse play preferences.

FG&G's greatest legacy may be establishing that retroclones could successfully preserve complex multi-book systems, not just streamlined single-volume editions. By demonstrating feasibility of comprehensive 2E recreation, Brown encouraged potential retroclone designers tackling other complex systems previously considered too ambitious for retroclone treatment. The project proved that dedication, thorough research, and careful OGL application could resurrect even D&D's most elaborate editions.

For Gold & Glory represents OSR movement's commitment to preserving complete D&D heritage, not just celebrated editions. By recreating often-overlooked AD&D 2E with fidelity and comprehensiveness, Brown validated 2E players' preferences while demonstrating that retroclones could serve niche audiences as effectively as popular consensus favorites. The system proves that old-school gaming encompasses diversity of complexity levels, play styles, and design philosophies—unified not by specific mechanics but by shared values of referee empowerment, player creativity, and appreciation for D&D's historical evolution.
