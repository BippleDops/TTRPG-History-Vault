# Troika!

**Publisher**: Melsonian Arts Council
**Designer**: [[Daniel Sell]]
**Year**: 2019
**System**: OSR (Fighting Fantasy Heritage)
**Genre**: Psychedelic Science-Fantasy
**License**: Proprietary (Community License)
**Status**: In Print

## Overview

Troika! represents the OSR's most successful synthesis of British gamebook mechanics with contemporary indie sensibilities, creating system that feels simultaneously like rediscovered 1980s artifact and cutting-edge experimental design. Created by Daniel Sell and published through his Melsonian Arts Council imprint, Troika! adapts the Fighting Fantasy and Advanced Fighting Fantasy systems—d6-based UK gamebooks by Steve Jackson and Ian Livingstone—into complete tabletop RPG supporting gonzo, psychedelic science-fantasy adventures. The game's genius lies in recognizing Fighting Fantasy's mechanical elegance while amplifying its implied weirdness into explicit design principle.

The setting, nominally called "The Humpbacked Sky," embraces reality-bending surrealism as baseline rather than exception. Characters might be: time-traveling insurance adjusters, vat-grown assassins, thought thieves, monkey butlers from mechanical kingdoms, or any of dozens of equally bizarre backgrounds. These aren't "weird" options among normal choices—they're all weird, creating world where strangeness is ordinary and conventional fantasy becomes remarkable for its absence. This commitment to absolute weirdness distinguishes Troika! from OSR games that add strange elements to conventional frameworks; here, convention itself is stranger than fiction.

Mechanically, Troika! uses 2d6 for most resolution, creating bell curve probability distribution fundamentally different from d20's flat probability. Rather than linear probability where every result is equally likely (5% each on d20), 2d6 produces central tendency where middle results (6-8) occur far more often than extremes (2 or 12). This creates "competent by default" feel where trained characters usually succeed at reasonable tasks, with spectacular failures or successes being genuinely rare events. The system subverts OSR's typical deadliness through mathematics favoring middling outcomes.

The initiative system represents Troika!'s most distinctive mechanical innovation. Rather than rolling initiative each round, players and GM place tokens in bag—one for each PC, several for enemies, and "End of Round" token. Tokens are drawn randomly, determining who acts in sequence until End of Round token emerges, at which point round ends and tokens are reset. This creates uncertainty about action order, potential for multiple actions before enemies respond, and dramatic tension as End of Round approaches. The system is simultaneously simple and mechanically unique, creating emergent tactical depth from random draws.

Character creation through backgrounds exemplifies Troika!'s approach to player choice. Rather than selecting races and classes, players roll or choose from 36 backgrounds—conceptual packages combining suggested stats, skills, equipment, and narrative hooks. A "Befouler of Ponds" has different capabilities than "Claviger of Engelmere" or "Thought Thief." These aren't mechanical frameworks with interchangeable flavor—they're holistic identities shaping how characters interact with world. The backgrounds communicate setting through character options, making world-building inseparable from character creation.

The skill system, adapted from Advanced Fighting Fantasy, uses simple target number resolution. Characters have skill ranks ranging from 0-6+, roll 2d6, and succeed if result plus skill meets or exceeds target (usually 7-12). The limited skill range means improvement from 0 to 3 represents dramatic capability increase, creating advancement that feels significant without requiring years of play or exponential scaling. This bounded progression maintains OSR's relatively flat power curves while providing meaningful character improvement.

## Historical Context

Troika! emerged in 2019 during British independent RPG publishing's renaissance, when UK designers increasingly drew from distinctly British gaming heritage rather than American D&D tradition. While OSR movement predominantly focused on TSR-era D&D, British designers recognized alternative old-school traditions—Fighting Fantasy gamebooks, Warhammer Fantasy Roleplay, RuneQuest—offered different foundations for retroclone projects. Troika! represented this alternative archaeology, excavating Fighting Fantasy rather than Dungeons & Dragons.

Daniel Sell came to game design through visual arts and publishing, approaching RPG creation as much as art project as commercial product. His Melsonian Arts Council imprint operated as experimental press releasing strange, beautifully-produced gaming materials that blurred boundaries between game books, art books, and literary objects. Previous releases included *The Gardens of Ynn* (procedural point-crawl), *The Stygian Library* (infinite library generator), and various experimental modules. Troika! emerged from this aesthetic context where games were expected to be weird and challenging.

The choice to adapt Fighting Fantasy reflected both nostalgia and strategic positioning. For British gamers of certain age, Fighting Fantasy gamebooks were formative experiences—Choose Your Own Adventure-style books with combat systems using dice. These books taught millions to roleplay alone before they knew tabletop RPGs existed. However, unlike D&D's extensive retroclone coverage, Fighting Fantasy remained relatively unexplored as revival source. Sell recognized opportunity: create OSR-compatible game from distinctly British source material, offering mechanical alternative to d20 while tapping nostalgic recognition.

Advanced Fighting Fantasy (AFF), the actual tabletop RPG version of Fighting Fantasy published in 1989, provided immediate mechanical framework. AFF had adapted gamebook rules for group play, introducing skill system, magic, and referee procedures. However, AFF remained relatively obscure, overshadowed by D&D and Warhammer in UK market. Sell could build on AFF's foundation while claiming creative freedom to modify and expand, avoiding both legal concerns and community expectations about faithful recreation.

The 2019 release timing coincided with broader indie RPG trends toward surrealism, gonzo fantasy, and rejection of Tolkienesque conventions. Games like *Vörk Borg* (2020), *Troika!* itself, and various "weird" OSR modules demonstrated appetite for fantasy that embraced strangeness rather than seeking verisimilitude. The aesthetic aligned with contemporary art movements, underground comics, and internet surrealism, creating cultural context where Troika!'s weirdness felt timely rather than alienating.

The physical production emphasized art object over functional rulebook. Sell commissioned artwork from multiple artists creating dissonant, surreal aesthetic. Layout mixed prose with poetry, illustrations bled across pages, and organization prioritized atmosphere over usability. The resulting book felt more like grimoire or artists' chapbook than typical RPG manual. This presentation positioned Troika! as cultural artifact within broader independent arts context, appealing beyond pure gaming audiences.

The initial release through Kickstarter combined crowdfunding with limited physical distribution, creating artificial scarcity that built mystique. Early adopters shared images of strange backgrounds and surreal artwork, generating word-of-mouth through aesthetic appeal. The game became "cool"—something to be seen with, discussed in indie circles, displayed conspicuously. This cultural positioning, as much as mechanical quality, drove adoption beyond purely utilitarian "I need an RPG system" decisions.

## What It Clones

Troika! clones Fighting Fantasy and Advanced Fighting Fantasy's mechanical frameworks while dramatically expanding setting implications latent in original material. Fighting Fantasy gamebooks established 2d6 resolution, Skill-Stamina-Luck attributes, simple combat, and spell lists. Advanced Fighting Fantasy added skill system, character creation, and multiplayer procedures. Troika! inherits these foundations while rebuilding everything around them toward stranger purposes.

The core attributes—Skill, Stamina, Luck—come directly from Fighting Fantasy. Skill determines combat ability and serves as baseline for skill tests. Stamina represents hit points and physical endurance. Luck provides resource spending for fortunate breaks. These three stats replace D&D's six abilities, creating simpler character framework focused on competence (Skill), endurance (Stamina), and fortune (Luck). The reduction to three attributes enables faster character creation and simpler gameplay while maintaining meaningful differentiation.

The 2d6 resolution system follows Fighting Fantasy's bell curve probability. Most tests involve rolling 2d6 plus skill versus target number (typically 7-12). The central tendency means average characters (Skill 3-4) succeed at moderate tasks (target 9) more often than fail, creating competent-by-default gameplay contrasting with d20's swingy unpredictability. This mathematical shift fundamentally changes how games feel—characters are heroes who usually succeed at reasonable tasks, not incompetents hoping for lucky rolls.

Combat follows Fighting Fantasy's basic structure: opposed rolls comparing Attack Strength (2d6 + Skill + weapon bonus) determine who damages whom each round. However, Troika! implements combat through bag-based initiative system rather than Fighting Fantasy's simple alternating turns. This innovation maintains Fighting Fantasy's opposed roll core while creating novel tactical dynamics through uncertain action ordering.

The magic system adapts Advanced Fighting Fantasy's spell list approach but expands dramatically in weirdness. Spells have relatively conventional effects (damage, protection, detection) but described with surreal flavor that makes magic feel alien and unpredictable. Casting requires Spell skill test, with failure potentially producing mishaps. The resource management comes through Stamina costs for spells rather than Vancian slots, enabling casters to push limits through health expenditure—evocative choice between capability and endurance.

The skill system directly inherits AFF's approach: characters have ranks in various skills (Fighting, Tracking, Swimming, Spell casting, etc.), roll 2d6 + skill, compare to difficulty. However, Troika! significantly expands skill variety and weird specificity—skills like "Astrology," "Evaluate," "Etiquette," "Mathmology" create character differentiation through unusual competencies. The skills communicate world assumptions—that astrology works, that evaluation is learned skill, that alien etiquettes matter.

Character advancement follows Fighting Fantasy's initially flat progression. Characters don't gain levels dramatically increasing power—they improve through incremental skill increases, higher attributes, and better equipment. This bounded progression maintains Fighting Fantasy's accessible difficulty across adventures while enabling meaningful character growth. The contrast with D&D's exponential advancement is stark—Troika! characters get better but never become godlike.

The adventure structure assumes point-crawl or situation-based design rather than dungeon-crawling. Fighting Fantasy gamebooks featured interconnected locations with choice-based navigation, not gridded dungeons. Troika! inherits this structure, assuming adventures present interesting locations and situations connected by travel choices rather than tactical mapping exercises. This subtly shifts play style toward investigation and problem-solving over tactical combat.

## Innovations and Differences

The bag-based initiative system represents Troika!'s most mechanically innovative element. At round start, players and GM place tokens in bag—one per PC, one per opponent (or group of similar opponents), and End of Round token. Tokens draw randomly determine action order. When End of Round draws, round immediately ends regardless of who hasn't acted. This creates multiple innovative dynamics: uncertainty about when you'll act, possibility of going multiple times before enemies respond or vice versa, dramatic tension as End of Round approaches, and natural resolution preventing endless combat rounds.

The background system transforms character creation into worldbuilding exercise. Rather than generic classes with flavor text, backgrounds are holistic identities: "Journeyman of the Sublime Society of Beef Steaks" isn't fighter-with-description, it's complete conceptual package suggesting history, goals, social connections, and bizarre world where such societies exist. The 36 backgrounds collectively communicate setting more effectively than chapters of exposition—players learn world through character options available, inferring cosmology from professions' existence.

The Skill-Stamina-Luck attribute reduction simplifies D&D's six-ability framework to essential elements. Skill replaces Strength-Dexterity-Constitution as physical capability; Stamina is endurance; Luck is fortune. The simplification eliminates dump stats, reduces calculations, and focuses character differentiation on meaningful choices rather than ability score distribution. Every attribute matters—Skill for all tests, Stamina for survival, Luck for desperate saves—creating streamlined character sheet without weak attributes.

The artwork and aesthetic presentation create dissonant, dreamlike atmosphere rarely achieved in RPG books. Multiple artists with inconsistent styles, surreal imagery, and deliberate incoherence make book feel like artifact from parallel dimension. This isn't just decoration—it communicates Troika!'s core principle that coherent worldbuilding is unnecessary, that contradiction and strangeness are features not bugs. The aesthetic itself becomes game design, telling players what kind of play system supports.

The advancement through adventures rather than experience points fundamentally changes player motivation. Characters improve by completing adventures (gaining skill points to distribute), not by optimizing XP accumulation. This shifts focus from mechanical optimization ("how to gain most XP") toward engaging with fiction ("what adventures to pursue"). The change aligns with story game sensibilities while maintaining old-school framework of character improvement through play.

The spell list balances mechanical functionality with surreal flavor. "Purple Lens" creates force field but described as "great purple eye" manifesting; "True Seeing" reveals reality but might show "the true and terrible form of things." This approach makes magic feel genuinely magical rather than scientific, maintaining utility while preserving sense of dealing with incomprehensible forces. The contrast with D&D's engineering-precise spell effects is deliberate—magic should be weird, not predictable.

The setting's lack of comprehensive geography or history inverts typical fantasy worldbuilding. Rather than detailed maps and timelines, Troika! provides fragments: some backgrounds reference places, some spells imply cosmologies, some equipment suggests technologies, but nothing coheres into unified whole. This fragmentation is intentional—it enables GMs to fill gaps according to taste while maintaining tonal consistency through aesthetic rather than canonical facts.

The modularity enables genre flexibility rare in setting-specific games. While default aesthetic is psychedelic science-fantasy, the mechanical framework accommodates various settings. Designers create Troika! hacks for horror, historical fantasy, space opera, demonstrating system's flexibility. The backgrounds-as-character-creation approach particularly enables setting variety—replace 36 backgrounds with setting-specific options, and Troika! becomes new game without mechanical changes.

The luck mechanic transforms resource-spending into narrative control. Players spend Luck points to reroll tests or damage, buying fortune at attribute cost. This creates interesting choices: spend Luck for important success, or conserve for crucial survival? Unlike Fate points or similar mechanics in story games, Luck is zero-sum resource that permanently decreases when spent (until restored through play or rest). The mechanical limitation prevents Luck from becoming unlimited narrative control while maintaining player agency.

## Community and Culture

The Troika! community coalesced around aesthetic appreciation as much as gameplay, creating culture where discussing beautiful strangeness matters as much as mechanical optimization. Community spaces feature artwork sharing, background proposals, setting fragments, and surreal actual play reports. The culture emphasizes creativity and weirdness—contributing strange ideas becomes social currency, with most bizarre concepts earning respect rather than dismissal.

The third-party content ecosystem exploded immediately following release, with designers creating adventures, background compendiums, complete setting books, and system hacks. The Troika! Community License enables commercial publication while maintaining quality control through submission requirements. This managed openness creates thriving commercial ecosystem while preserving brand coherence—published Troika! content maintains aesthetic standards rather than fragmenting into disconnected supplements.

The UK indie RPG scene particularly embraced Troika!, viewing it as British OSR done right—weird, literary, aesthetically sophisticated. The game regularly appears at UK conventions, game stores, and indie publishing circles, becoming shorthand for certain approach to fantasy gaming. This British cultural position distinguishes Troika! from American-dominated OSR, creating alternative lineage and aesthetic tradition.

The artistic community engages deeply, with visual artists creating fan art, character portraits, and illustrated backgrounds. This artistic engagement extends Troika!'s cultural presence into illustration communities, gallery spaces, and art contexts beyond pure gaming. The blurred boundary between game and art object enables Troika! to occupy cultural niches typically closed to gaming products.

The actual play scene showcases Troika!'s emergent weirdness. Streams and podcasts demonstrate how random backgrounds combine with gonzo scenarios to produce unexpected narratives. The visibility of play outcomes—where mechanical simplicity enables focus on fictional elements—helps potential players understand how strangeness translates from concept to table experience.

The relationship to Fighting Fantasy nostalgia community creates interesting dynamic. Older UK players recognize Fighting Fantasy heritage, creating intergenerational appeal. Younger players discover Fighting Fantasy through Troika!, reversing usual nostalgia flow. This bidirectional influence revitalizes Fighting Fantasy's cultural presence while legitimizing Troika! through historical lineage.

The DIY ethos encourages players to create content for personal use. The mechanical simplicity enables easy homebrew—creating new backgrounds, spells, or enemies requires minimal system mastery. This accessibility democratizes content creation while generating enormous variety in table experiences. Two Troika! campaigns likely feature completely different backgrounds, spells, and scenarios despite using same core rules.

## Legacy and Influence

Troika!'s legacy centers on legitimizing Fighting Fantasy mechanics as alternative OSR foundation and establishing aesthetic incoherence as valid design principle. The game demonstrated that OSR could draw from non-D&D sources while maintaining old-school principles, encouraging designers to explore regional gaming traditions rather than solely American lineages. The success inspired retroclones of other British games and validated mechanical diversity within OSR movement.

The bag-based initiative inspired similar randomized action-order systems across indie RPGs. Designers recognized advantages: eliminates initiative calculation, creates dramatic uncertainty, prevents action-order optimization, naturally limits round length. Variations appear in multiple subsequent games, from direct adoption to conceptual inspirations for alternative ordering systems.

The background-as-character-creation approach influenced how games communicate settings. Rather than separate worldbuilding sections, designers recognized character options could convey cosmology, technology levels, social structures, and tone. This integration of setting into mechanics appears across post-Troika! indie designs treating character creation as worldbuilding tool.

The aesthetic presentation legitimized art-book approaches to RPG publishing. Subsequent games felt empowered to prioritize atmosphere over organization, commission multiple artists with clashing styles, and treat books as art objects rather than pure functional text. This elevated RPG design discourse toward considering books' physical and aesthetic dimensions as design elements rather than incidental packaging.

The commercial success through Kickstarter and direct sales demonstrated market for weird, aesthetically ambitious RPGs. Publishers learned that strangeness and high production values could command premium prices and build devoted audiences. This validation encouraged riskier aesthetic choices across indie publishing.

The community license provided alternative to pure openness (CC licensing) or complete restriction (proprietary no-derivatives). The managed marketplace model where commercial publication requires submission and approval creates quality-controlled ecosystem while enabling professional creators to profit. This middle path inspired similar licensing approaches balancing openness with brand management.

For specific games, Troika!'s influence is acknowledged in numerous gonzo OSR games embracing surrealism and aesthetic incoherence. *Vörk Borg*, *Gradient Descent*, various psychedelic adventures cite Troika! as validating weirdness as default rather than deviation. The broader principle—that fantasy need not seek internal consistency, that contradictions can enhance rather than undermine setting—influenced how designers approach worldbuilding.

The success of 2d6 resolution encouraged subsequent designers to consider dice systems beyond d20. Designers recognized bell curve probability creates different play feel than flat distribution, leading to experimentation with alternative dice mechanics even in D&D-adjacent games.
