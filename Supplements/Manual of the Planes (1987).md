# Manual of the Planes (1987)

**Type**: Sourcebook
**Parent Game**: [[Advanced Dungeons & Dragons (1977)]]
**Publisher**: [[TSR]]
**Designer**: Jeff Grubb
**Published**: 1987
**Page Count**: 128 pages
**Historical Significance**: 5/5
**Innovation Score**: 4/5
**Critical Reception**: Foundational and Influential

## Overview

Manual of the Planes, published in 1987, stands as one of AD&D's most significant and enduring supplements. Designer Jeff Grubb systematized D&D's multiverse into the "Great Wheel" cosmology—a comprehensive framework organizing the game's various planes of existence into coherent, interconnected structure. The manual detailed the 17 Outer Planes aligned to the moral and ethical axes, the four Inner Elemental Planes and their para- and quasi-elemental variants, the Astral and Ethereal transitional planes, and the mechanics for traveling between them.

More than just a gazetteer of exotic locations, Manual of the Planes established metaphysical framework that would define D&D's cosmic structure for decades. The Great Wheel cosmology, with its elegant geometric organization of aligned planes, became the default multiverse model through AD&D 1st and 2nd Editions and heavily influenced 3rd Edition's planar design. Even when 4th Edition radically restructured the planes, 5th Edition returned to the Great Wheel as the assumed baseline, testament to the model's enduring appeal and utility.

The supplement provided DMs with tools for planar adventures—alternate realities offering radically different environments, challenges, and narrative possibilities beyond the Material Plane's constraints. By codifying previously scattered planar information into systematic reference, Grubb created framework enabling campaigns like [[Planescape Campaign Setting (1994)]] and establishing planes as fundamental D&D component rather than occasional exotic diversion.

## Historical Context

### Planar Content Before Manual of the Planes

D&D's planar concept evolved gradually:

**Original D&D** (1974):
- Minimal planar references
- Clerics drawing power from "other planes"
- No systematic cosmology
- Planes mentioned but undefined

**AD&D Core Books** (1977-1979):
- **Players Handbook**: Mentioned planes tangentially
- **Dungeon Masters Guide**: Scattered planar references
- **Deities & Demigods**: Gods residing in planes
- No comprehensive cosmology

**Scattered Supplements**:
- [[Eldritch Wizardry (1976)]]: Astral and Ethereal planes mentioned
- Dragon Magazine articles: Various planar discussions
- Adventure modules: Occasional planar excursions
- Inconsistent and contradictory information

**Need for Systematization**:
By 1987, planar content existed but lacked:
- Coherent organization
- Consistent rules for planar travel
- Comprehensive plane descriptions
- Unified cosmic structure

Manual of the Planes addressed these gaps, creating definitive reference.

### Jeff Grubb's Design Approach

Jeff Grubb brought specific vision to the project:

**Background**:
- TSR staff designer since 1982
- Created Spelljammer setting
- Interest in cosmic-scale worldbuilding
- Systematic thinker

**Design Philosophy**:

**Geometric Organization**:
- Planes arranged by alignment axes
- Visual clarity (the Great Wheel diagram)
- Logical progression between planes
- Memorable structure

**Accessibility**:
- Complex cosmology made comprehensible
- Visual aids and diagrams
- Clear organization
- DM-friendly presentation

**Playability**:
- Not just cosmology theory
- Practical adventuring information
- Monster encounters
- Usable at game table

**Inspiration Sources**:
- Dante's Inferno
- Norse mythology (World Tree)
- Greek mythology (underworld)
- Various religious cosmologies
- Fantasy literature

Grubb synthesized diverse influences into distinctly D&D framework.

### Post-Gygax TSR (1987)

Manual of the Planes emerged in changing TSR:

**Gary Gygax Departed** (1985-1986):
- Gygax no longer with company
- New creative leadership

**Lorraine Williams Era**:

The supplement represents post-Gygax TSR finding its creative identity.

## The Great Wheel Cosmology

### Cosmic Structure

Manual of the Planes' defining contribution:

**The Great Wheel Diagram**:
A circular arrangement of 17 Outer Planes:

**Organizational Axes**:

**Law-Chaos Axis** (horizontal):
- **Lawful planes**: Left side of wheel
- **Neutral planes**: Top and bottom
- **Chaotic planes**: Right side

**Good-Evil Axis** (vertical):
- **Good planes**: Upper half
- **Neutral planes**: Left and right
- **Evil planes**: Lower half

**The 17 Outer Planes**:

**Lawful Good**:
- **The Seven Heavens** (Mount Celestia): Lawful good paradise
- **The Twin Paradises** (Bytopia): Industrious good
- **The Blessed Fields** (Elysium): Neutral good bliss

**Neutral Good**:
- **The Wilderness** (Beastlands): Primal goodness
- **Olympus** (Arborea): Chaotic good freedom

**Chaotic Good**:
- **The Heroic Domains** (Ysgard): Valor and glory
- **The Windswept Depths** (Limbo): Primordial chaos

**Chaotic Neutral**:
- **The Ever-Changing** (Pandemonium): Maddening winds
- **The Infinite Abyss**: Demonic chaos

**Chaotic Evil**:
- **The Tarterian Depths** (Carceri): Prison plane
- **The Gray Waste** (Hades): Apathy and despair

**Neutral Evil**:
- **The Bleak Eternity** (Gehenna): Cruel evil
- **The Nine Hells** (Baator): Lawful evil hierarchy

**Lawful Evil**:
- **The Infernal Battlefield** (Acheron): Eternal war
- **The Peaceable Kingdoms** (Arcadia): Lawful neutrality

**Lawful Neutral**:
- **The Clockwork Universe** (Mechanus): Ultimate law

**True Neutral**:
- **The Outlands**: Hub of the Great Wheel, containing Sigil

**Design Elegance**:

**Visual Clarity**:
- Wheel diagram immediately comprehensible
- Alignment relationships obvious
- Adjacent planes share characteristics
- Progression clear

**Narrative Utility**:
- Alignment tangible and geographic
- Moral journeys literalized
- Cosmic conflicts spatially represented
- Mythology and philosophy merged

**Game Functionality**:
- DMs could place deities and creatures logically
- Planar adventures had structure
- Alignment consequences given weight
- Cosmic scope accessible

**Influence**:
The Great Wheel became iconic:
- Default D&D cosmology through multiple editions
- Inspired Planescape's planar adventures
- Influenced other games' cosmologies
- Recognized even by non-D&D players

### The Inner Planes

Elemental cosmology detailed:

**Four Primary Elements**:

**Plane of Fire**:
- Infinite flame and heat
- City of Brass (efreet metropolis)
- Salamanders and fire elementals
- Extreme temperatures deadly to mortals

**Plane of Water**:
- Endless ocean
- Marids and water elementals
- Underwater civilizations
- Pressure and drowning hazards

**Plane of Earth**:
- Solid matter throughout
- Dao and earth elementals
- Tunneling and crushing dangers
- Gems and minerals

**Plane of Air**:
- Empty sky forever
- Djinn and air elementals
- Falling and suffocation risks
- Floating islands and wind currents

**Para-Elemental Planes** (between primary elements):
- **Ice** (Air/Water border)
- **Magma** (Fire/Earth border)
- **Ooze** (Water/Earth border)
- **Smoke** (Air/Fire border)

**Quasi-Elemental Planes** (between elements and energy):

**Positive Energy Border**:
- **Lightning** (Air/Positive)
- **Radiance** (Fire/Positive)
- **Mineral** (Earth/Positive)
- **Steam** (Water/Positive)

**Negative Energy Border**:
- **Vacuum** (Air/Negative)
- **Ash** (Fire/Negative)
- **Dust** (Earth/Negative)
- **Salt** (Water/Negative)

**Energy Planes**:
- **Positive Energy Plane**: Life force, eventually fatal to mortals
- **Negative Energy Plane**: Death energy, immediately dangerous

**Design Philosophy**:
- Organized chaos of elemental forces
- Building blocks of reality
- Extreme environments for exotic adventures
- Source of elemental creatures

### Transitional Planes

The Astral and Ethereal as cosmic highways:

**The Ethereal Plane**:

**Characteristics**:
- Misty, ghostly realm
- Overlaps Material Plane
- Coexistent geography
- Used for travel to Inner Planes

**Border Ethereal**:
- Close to Material Plane
- Can observe Material (but not interact)
- Ghosts and phase spiders common
- Invisibility and intangibility

**Deep Ethereal**:
- Removed from Material
- Color pools leading to other planes
- Ethereal cyclones (dangerous storms)
- Lost travelers and strange encounters

**Uses**:
- Bypassing Material Plane obstacles
- Travel to Elemental Planes
- Ghostly hauntings explained
- Ethereal magic (etherealness, etc.)

**The Astral Plane**:

**Characteristics**:
- Timeless silvery void
- Psychic movement (thought-based)
- No gravity
- Endless expanse

**Color Pools**:
- Gates to other planes
- Colored by destination
- Two-way travel
- Main method of planar transit

**Psychic Winds**:
- Mental storms
- Blow travelers off course
- Can damage psyche
- Navigation hazard

**Denizens**:
- **Githyanki**: Dominant astral culture
- **Astral Dreadnoughts**: Apex predators
- **Dead gods**: Corpses of deities floating
- Travelers and explorers

**Uses**:
- Highway to Outer Planes
- Instant travel across distances
- Repository of divine corpses
- Setting for cosmic conflicts

## Mechanical Systems

### Planar Travel Methods

The manual detailed how to reach planes:

**Spell-Based Travel**:
- **Plane Shift**: Direct but imprecise
- **Gate**: Powerful, creates portal
- **Astral Spell**: Enter Astral Plane
- **Etherealness**: Enter Ethereal
- Various specialized spells

**Physical Gates**:
- Permanent planar portals
- Often hidden or guarded
- May require keys or conditions
- Two-way or one-way

**Natural Vortices**:
- Naturally occurring planar breaches
- Unpredictable timing
- Dangerous passages
- May be unstable

**Conduits**:
- Permanent connections between planes
- Rivers flowing between planes (River Styx)
- Mountains touching multiple planes
- World Trees spanning cosmology

**Astral Travel**:
- Enter Astral, find color pool
- Navigate to destination pool
- Exit into target plane
- Safest but time-consuming

### Planar Survival Rules

Challenges of planar existence:

**Gravity Variations**:
- **Normal**: Like Material Plane
- **Heavy**: Movement and actions harder
- **Light**: Jump farther, fall slower
- **None**: Weightlessness
- **Subjective Directional**: Thought determines "down"

**Time Differences**:
- **Normal Flow**: As Material Plane
- **Timeless**: No aging, healing, hunger
- **Erratic**: Time flows unpredictably
- **Flowing differently**: Faster or slower than Material

**Environmental Hazards**:
- **Extreme temperatures**: Fire/Ice planes
- **Lack of air**: Vacuum, water, earth
- **Energy damage**: Positive/Negative planes
- **Alignment effects**: Opposing alignment hostile

**Magical Alterations**:
Spells function differently:
- **Enhanced**: More powerful
- **Diminished**: Less effective
- **Altered**: Work differently
- **Null**: Don't function at all

**Movement Restrictions**:
- **Psychic navigation**: Astral movement
- **Swimming**: Water plane
- **Tunneling**: Earth plane
- **Flying essential**: Air plane

These rules made planar adventures mechanically distinct and challenging.

### Planar Encounters

The manual provided extensive encounter tables:

**Inhabitants by Plane**:
- Appropriate creatures for each plane
- Alignment-based denizens
- Outsiders (celestials, fiends, etc.)
- Planar travelers

**Hazards**:
- Environmental dangers
- Psychic effects
- Magical anomalies
- Reality distortions

**Organizations**:
- Planar civilizations
- Factions and groups
- Divine servants
- Mortal settlements

This content enabled DMs to populate planar adventures with appropriate challenges.

## Conclusion

Manual of the Planes stands among AD&D's most significant achievements—a supplement that systematized D&D's multiverse, created elegant organizing framework, and enabled entirely new campaign types. Jeff Grubb's Great Wheel cosmology proved so successful that it became D&D's default cosmic structure, surviving edition changes and even returning after being temporarily replaced.

The supplement demonstrated that complexity could be made accessible through good organization and visual design. The Great Wheel diagram's geometric elegance made comprehensible what might otherwise have been overwhelming, while the systematic approach to planar traits and travel provided practical tools for actual play. This combination of cosmic scope and usability at the game table exemplifies excellent game design.

More broadly, Manual of the Planes expanded D&D's identity. It proved the game could support metaphysical adventures, cosmic-scale conflicts, and philosophical campaigns alongside traditional dungeon crawling. The planes became fundamental D&D component—where gods reside, where alignment has geographic meaning, where the truly epic adventures occur.

For modern D&D, the Manual's legacy persists in every planar reference, every mention of the Outer Planes, every campaign venturing beyond the Material. Its cosmology structures how D&D thinks about the multiverse, how settings integrate planes, and how high-level play achieves truly cosmic scope. Few supplements can claim such lasting, fundamental influence on their game.
