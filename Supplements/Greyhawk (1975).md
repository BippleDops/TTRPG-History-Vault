# Greyhawk (1975)

**Type**: Rules Expansion Supplement
**Parent Game**: [[Dungeons & Dragons (1974)]]
**Publisher**: [[TSR]]
**Designer**: [[Gary Gygax]]
**Published**: 1975
**Page Count**: 68 pages
**Historical Significance**: 5/5
**Innovation Score**: 5/5
**Critical Reception**: Foundational

## Overview

Greyhawk, officially titled "Supplement I: Greyhawk," stands as the first supplement ever published for Dungeons & Dragons and therefore the first supplement for any tabletop roleplaying game. Released in 1975, approximately one year after the original D&D boxed set, Greyhawk fundamentally transformed the game by adding the Thief class, introducing variable weapon damage, expanding the magic system, and establishing the precedent that D&D would be a living, evolving game rather than a finished product.

The supplement's significance extends far beyond its mechanical additions. Greyhawk established the business model of ongoing support for RPGs through supplements, demonstrated that players wanted more complexity and options than the original three-class system provided, and proved that D&D had commercial legs as an expandable product line. It also introduced the World of Greyhawk as Gary Gygax's personal campaign setting, which would later become D&D's first official published setting.

Greyhawk's innovations became so fundamental to D&D that modern players often don't realize they weren't in the original game. Variable weapon damage, the Thief class, and many iconic spells debuted here, reshaping D&D's identity and establishing patterns that would persist for decades. The supplement represents the beginning of D&D's transformation from Gygax and Arneson's collaborative original vision into primarily Gygax's evolving design philosophy.

## Historical Context

### Post-D&D Launch Environment (1974-1975)

When the original D&D boxed set launched in January 1974, neither [[Gary Gygax]] nor [[TSR]] knew what they had created. The game sold slowly at first, distributed primarily through hobby shops and wargaming circles. However, by late 1974, sales were accelerating rapidly, and players were creating house rules, writing to Gygax with questions, and demanding more content.

The early D&D community was intensely creative and fragmentary:
- **Gaming clubs** ran D&D with wildly different house rules
- **Fanzines** began publishing variant classes and rules
- **Players wrote letters** to TSR requesting clarifications and additions
- **Gygax's own campaign** had evolved significantly from the published rules
- **Commercial success** exceeded expectations, suggesting room for expansion

This environment created pressure and opportunity for official supplements to codify rules, address ambiguities, and provide new content that would both satisfy existing players and attract new ones.

### The Original D&D's Limitations

The 1974 D&D boxed set, while revolutionary, had significant gaps:

**Class Limitations**: Only three classes (Fighting-Man, Cleric, Magic-User) with limited differentiation
**Combat Simplicity**: All weapons dealt 1d6 damage regardless of type
**Spell Scarcity**: Limited spell selection, especially at higher levels
**Magic Item Brevity**: Relatively few magic items detailed
**Rules Ambiguities**: Many situations lacked clear mechanical guidance

Gygax's own Greyhawk campaign had already addressed many of these issues through house rules, creating a tested foundation for the supplement. Players in his group were already playing Thieves, using variable weapon damage, and benefiting from expanded spell lists before Greyhawk's publication.

### Gary Gygax's Creative Vision

By 1975, Gygax's design philosophy was crystallizing:
- **Tactical complexity** through meaningful mechanical choices
- **Character differentiation** beyond class levels
- **Simulation elements** making weapons and equipment feel distinct
- **Dungeon delving focus** with specialized roles
- **Expanded options** for experienced players seeking depth

Greyhawk represents Gygax's first opportunity to officially expand D&D according to his evolving vision, setting precedents for how he would approach the game's development throughout the 1970s.

## Content Overview

### Structure and Organization

Greyhawk's 68 pages are organized into distinct sections:

**Part I: New Classes and Ability Score Changes** (pages 1-8)
- The Thief class in complete detail
- The Paladin as a Fighting-Man sub-class
- Revised ability score bonuses and minimums

**Part II: Monsters and Treasures** (pages 9-44)
- Expanded monster listings
- New monsters including many that became D&D staples
- Greatly expanded magic item tables

**Part III: New Spells** (pages 45-60)
- Additional cleric spells
- Additional magic-user spells
- Illusionist spell list

**Part IV: Weapons and Combat** (pages 61-68)
- Variable weapon damage tables
- Polearm differentiation and combat rules
- Additional combat clarifications

The supplement assumes ownership of the original D&D boxed set and directly references its page numbers, establishing the pattern of supplements as additions rather than standalone products.

### Production Values

Greyhawk followed the original D&D's minimal production aesthetic:
- **Format**: Digest-sized booklet (approximately 5.5" x 8.5")
- **Cover**: Two-color printing with crude fantasy illustration
- **Interior**: Black and white text with minimal art
- **Binding**: Saddle-stitched (stapled)
- **Presentation**: Dense text in two columns

The spartan presentation reflected TSR's limited budget and positioned the supplement as functional rules expansion rather than premium product. This contrasts sharply with later supplements, which would feature improved production values as TSR's resources grew.

## Mechanical Additions

### The Thief Class

Greyhawk's most significant addition was the Thief class, which would become one of D&D's four foundational classes alongside Fighter, Cleric, and Magic-User.

**Thief Abilities** (introduced here):
- **Pick Locks**: Percentage-based skill improving with level
- **Find/Remove Traps**: Separate percentage for detection and disarming
- **Pick Pockets**: Percentage chance with consequences for failure
- **Move Silently**: Stealth percentage-based skill
- **Climb Walls**: Including sheer surfaces, percentage-based
- **Hide in Shadows**: Percentage-based concealment
- **Hear Noise**: Better than other classes at detecting sounds
- **Backstab**: Bonus to-hit and damage multiplier when attacking from behind

**Design Implications**:
- **Percentage skills**: First use of percentile dice for task resolution in D&D
- **Level progression**: Skills improved with level, providing clear advancement
- **Niche protection**: Abilities unique to the class, creating party roles
- **Skill-based gameplay**: Alternative to combat effectiveness

The Thief introduced concepts that would profoundly influence RPG design: skill systems, percentage-based resolution, stealth gameplay, and the "roguish" character archetype. Its inclusion acknowledged that dungeon exploration needed mechanical support beyond combat and magic.

**Thief in Practice**:
- Initially weak in combat (d4 hit die)
- Became more effective through successful skill use
- Encouraged creative problem-solving
- Required DM adjudication for many situations
- Balanced through risk-reward mechanics (pickpocketing could anger NPCs)

### The Paladin

Greyhawk introduced the Paladin as a sub-class of Fighting-Man, establishing several important precedents:

**Paladin Requirements**:
- **Exceptional ability scores**: Strength, Intelligence, Wisdom, Charisma all 17+
- **Lawful Good alignment**: Strict behavioral code
- **Tithing**: 10% of income to charity or lawful good institution

**Paladin Abilities**:
- **Detect Evil**: 60' range at will
- **Lay on Hands**: Healing equal to paladin's level once per day
- **Cure Disease**: Once per week at 5th level
- **Immunity**: To disease
- **Turn Undead**: As a Cleric two levels lower
- **Summon Warhorse**: Special mount at 4th level
- **Circle of Power**: Dispel evil in 10' radius at 8th level

**Design Philosophy**:
- **Aspirational class**: Difficult to qualify for, representing elite status
- **Behavioral restrictions**: Alignment and code limited player actions
- **Hybrid capabilities**: Combat prowess plus limited divine powers
- **Fantasy archetype**: Modeled on Arthurian knights and paladins

The Paladin established that D&D classes could have strict requirements and behavioral codes, influencing later classes like the Monk, Ranger, and Druid. It also introduced the concept of "prestige" through difficulty of access.

### Variable Weapon Damage

One of Greyhawk's most impactful changes was introducing variable weapon damage, replacing the original D&D's uniform 1d6 for all weapons.

**New Damage Values**:
- **Daggers**: 1d4 damage
- **Hand axes, maces**: 1d6 damage
- **Swords, battle axes**: 1d8 damage
- **Two-handed swords**: 1d10 damage
- **Polearms**: Variable damage types
- **Flails, morning stars**: Special properties

**Rationale**:
- **Simulation**: Different weapons should feel mechanically distinct
- **Tactical choices**: Weapon selection becomes meaningful decision
- **Character differentiation**: Equipment contributes to playstyle
- **Historical reflection**: Weapon effectiveness varied significantly

**Impact on Play**:
- Two-handed swords became mechanically superior (before later balancing)
- Weapon choice mattered beyond flavor
- Encouraged discussion of weapon properties and historical accuracy
- Created framework for ongoing weapon rules evolution

This change proved controversial among players who preferred original D&D's simplicity, leading to ongoing debates about simulation versus abstraction that continue in RPG design today.

### Polearm Differentiation

Greyhawk introduced extensive polearm rules, reflecting Gygax's interest in medieval weapons:

**Polearms Detailed**:
- Awl pike
- Bardiche
- Bec de corbin
- Bill-guisarme
- Fauchard
- Glaive
- Glaive-guisarme
- Guisarme
- Halberd
- Lucern hammer
- Military fork
- Partisan
- Ranseur
- Spetum
- Voulge

Each polearm received:
- **Specific damage values** against different armor types
- **Length categories** affecting combat order
- **Special properties** for setting against charges
- **Historical notes** on usage

This level of detail fascinated some players (particularly wargamers) while overwhelming others, establishing a tension between simulationist and gamist approaches that would characterize much of AD&D's development.

### Expanded Magic Items

Greyhawk massively expanded the magic item tables:

**New Categories**:
- **Miscellaneous magic**: Dozens of new wondrous items
- **Swords**: Named intelligent swords with personalities
- **Armor and shields**: Expanded varieties and bonuses
- **Rings**: Additional ring types
- **Rods, staves, wands**: New implementations

**Notable Introductions**:
- **Bag of Holding**: Became iconic D&D item
- **Figurines of Wondrous Power**: Miniature statuettes becoming creatures
- **Deck of Many Things**: Random, game-altering artifact
- **Intelligent swords**: Weapons with personalities, alignment, and purposes

The expanded magic item tables made treasure more varied and interesting, supporting the game's focus on dungeon exploration and reward. Many items introduced here remain D&D staples fifty years later.

### New Spells

Greyhawk added numerous spells across all levels:

**Cleric Spells**:
- **Cure Light Wounds**: Healing magic (surprisingly not in original D&D)
- **Cure Serious Wounds**: More powerful healing
- **Raise Dead**: Resurrection magic
- **Quest**: Magically compel service

**Magic-User Spells**:
- **Knock**: Open locks magically
- **Fireball**: Iconic area damage spell
- **Lightning Bolt**: Linear area damage
- **Confusion**: Disable multiple enemies
- **Wall of Iron**: Permanent barrier creation
- **Power Word Kill**: Instant death effect

**Illusionist Spells**:
- Complete spell list for Illusionist class
- Specialized in deception and sensory manipulation
- Parallel to Magic-User but distinct

Many of these spells became fundamental to D&D's identity. Fireball, in particular, became perhaps the game's most iconic spell, synonymous with D&D magic in popular culture.

## Setting Contributions

### The World of Greyhawk (Implicit Introduction)

While Greyhawk wasn't explicitly a setting book, it implicitly introduced Gary Gygax's campaign world through:

**Setting References**:
- Monsters and NPCs from Gygax's campaign
- Geographic references in examples
- Names and proper nouns establishing setting flavor
- Implied cosmology through spell and monster descriptions

**Greyhawk Castle**:
- The supplement's namesake was Gygax's legendary megadungeon
- Referenced as example and inspiration
- Established dungeon-crawling as D&D's default mode
- Created mystique around Gygax's personal campaign

**Cultural Foundation**:
The supplement established Greyhawk as the "default" D&D setting in players' minds, even though the actual World of Greyhawk wouldn't be published until 1980. This created a distinct identity separate from [[Dave Arneson]]'s Blackmoor, beginning the Gygax-Arneson creative divergence.

### Implied Genre and Tone

Greyhawk's content shaped expectations for D&D's genre:

**Sword and Sorcery Emphasis**:
- Thief class evoked Leiber's Gray Mouser
- Polearms reflected historical medieval combat
- Magic items suggested pulp fantasy treasures
- Monsters included many from mythology and literature

**Dungeon Focus**:
- Abilities and items optimized for dungeon exploration
- Thief skills specifically designed for underground environments
- Treasure tables assumed dungeon-delving campaigns
- Combat rules emphasized tactical positioning in confined spaces

**Power Level**:
- New spells and items increased character power
- Paladin represented elite status
- Magical arms race between PCs and threats
- Escalation toward more powerful challenges

## Cultural Impact

### Establishing the Supplement Model

Greyhawk's commercial success proved that RPG supplements were viable:

**Business Implications**:
- **Ongoing revenue**: Games could generate sales beyond initial purchase
- **Customer retention**: Supplements kept players engaged
- **Product lines**: Established pattern of numbered supplements
- **Market expectations**: Players now expected ongoing support

This model would dominate RPG publishing for decades, with some arguing it created unsustainable expansion expectations while others praised it for keeping games alive and evolving.

### The "Complete" Game Debate

Greyhawk immediately raised questions that persist in RPG discourse:

**Was Original D&D Complete?**
- Some players felt Greyhawk "fixed" problems with the original
- Others saw it as power creep and unnecessary complexity
- Debate over what constitutes a "finished" RPG

**Optional vs. Standard**:
- Were Greyhawk's additions optional rules or corrections?
- How should groups decide what to use?
- Balancing compatibility with earlier campaigns

These debates would intensify with each subsequent supplement and remain relevant to modern RPG publishing, particularly around edition changes and errata.

### Player Community Response

The early D&D community reacted strongly to Greyhawk:

**Positive Reception**:
- **Thief class**: Immediately popular, seen as filling obvious gap
- **Variable damage**: Appreciated by simulationists and tactical players
- **More content**: Players wanted expansion and got it
- **Gygax credibility**: His playtest experience lent authority

**Concerns and Criticism**:
- **Complexity creep**: Some preferred original D&D's simplicity
- **Balance questions**: Were new options too powerful?
- **Cost**: Requiring purchases beyond original set
- **Fragmentation**: Not all groups used same supplements

These mixed reactions established patterns that would recur throughout D&D's history: tension between simplicity and complexity, accessibility and depth, compatibility and innovation.

## Reception and Legacy

### Commercial Performance

Greyhawk succeeded commercially beyond expectations:

**Sales Success**:
- Sold out initial print run quickly
- Multiple reprintings throughout 1975-1976
- Demonstrated market appetite for D&D expansion
- Justified investment in future supplements

**Market Impact**:
- Proved D&D was more than fad
- Attracted attention from hobby industry
- Encouraged TSR to expand product line
- Made D&D seem like growing phenomenon

### Critical Assessment (Historical)

Contemporary responses in gaming publications:

**Positive Reviews**:
- Praised Thief class as obvious addition
- Appreciated expanded magic items and spells
- Valued clarifications and additions to combat
- Recognized quality of Gygax's design work

**Reservations**:
- Polearm rules seen as excessively detailed
- Variable weapon damage created balance concerns
- Some felt supplement undermined original game
- Questions about ongoing supplement treadmill

### Long-Term Influence

Greyhawk's impact on RPG history is difficult to overstate:

**Design Influence**:
- **Thief class**: Became standard in RPGs, inspiring rogues, scouts, specialists
- **Skill systems**: Percentage-based resolution influenced countless games
- **Variable damage**: Standard approach in most RPGs
- **Supplement model**: Established ongoing support as industry norm

**D&D Evolution**:
- **Advanced D&D**: Greyhawk's additions heavily influenced AD&D
- **Basic D&D**: Different evolution path with alternative treatment of Thief
- **Later editions**: All included Thieves and variable weapon damage
- **Setting development**: Led to published Greyhawk campaign setting (1980)

**Industry Precedent**:
- **Expansion model**: Other RPGs followed supplement pattern
- **Living games**: Established RPGs as ongoing rather than finished products
- **Designer authority**: Gygax's role as authoritative designer strengthened
- **Commercial viability**: Proved RPG market could support product lines

## Design Philosophy and Approach

### Gygax's Emerging Vision

Greyhawk reveals Gygax's developing design philosophy:

**Simulation vs. Abstraction**:
- Variable weapon damage prioritized simulation
- Polearm detail reflected historical interest
- Balanced against playability concerns
- Tension between realism and fun

**Tactical Depth**:
- More combat options through weapon variety
- Positional tactics via backstab and weapon reach
- Meaningful equipment choices
- Strategic resource management

**Character Specialization**:
- Thief introduced clear niche protection
- Paladin established prestige through restrictions
- Classes became more distinct
- Party composition increasingly important

**Escalating Power**:
- New spells increased magical power
- Better magic items enhanced capabilities
- Higher-level abilities more impressive
- Foundation for ongoing power escalation

These principles would characterize much of Advanced D&D and influence RPG design broadly.

### Playtesting and Development

Greyhawk benefited from extensive playtesting:

**Gygax's Campaign**:
- All additions tested in actual play
- Greyhawk Castle provided testing ground
- Player feedback shaped final rules
- Multiple iterations before publication

**Community Input**:
- Correspondence with players informed design
- Questions revealed areas needing clarification
- House rules from other groups considered
- Early D&D community collaborative

This playtest-driven approach contrasted with some later supplements that received less rigorous testing, contributing to Greyhawk's relative polish and balance.

## Controversies and Discussions

### The Arneson Question

Greyhawk's authorship was purely Gygax, raising questions about D&D's creative direction:

**Diverging Visions**:
- [[Dave Arneson]]'s [[Blackmoor (1975)]] would offer different additions
- Greyhawk represented Gygax's interpretation of D&D's future
- Original collaboration began fragmenting
- Creative control shifting to Gygax

**Legal and Credit Issues**:
- Arneson not involved in Greyhawk
- Began patterns of credit disputes
- Question of who "owned" D&D's direction
- Foreshadowed later Gygax-Arneson conflicts

This creative divergence would have profound implications for D&D's development and the eventual Gygax-Arneson legal disputes.

### Thief Class Balance

The Thief sparked immediate debate:

**Power Concerns**:
- **Combat weakness**: d4 hit die made Thieves fragile
- **Skill unreliability**: Percentage chances meant frequent failure
- **Level dependence**: Low-level Thieves barely functional
- **DM discretion**: Success often dependent on interpretation

**Counter-arguments**:
- Unique capabilities justified by niche
- Skill improvements with level provided progression
- Backstab made Thieves dangerous in right circumstances
- Utility exceeded combat contribution

These debates influenced how later editions balanced the Rogue/Thief, with different approaches to skill reliability, combat effectiveness, and niche protection.

### Variable Damage Complexity

Variable weapon damage remained contentious:

**Simulationist Perspective**:
- Different weapons should feel different
- Historical accuracy matters
- Tactical choices enhanced gameplay
- Realism improves immersion

**Gamist Perspective**:
- Unnecessary complexity
- Balance issues between weapons
- Slowed gameplay with lookups
- Original simplicity preferable

This debate exemplifies broader questions about RPG design priorities that remain relevant: simulation detail vs. playability, tactical depth vs. streamlined play, historical accuracy vs. game balance.

### Polearm Obsession

Greyhawk's extensive polearm rules became notorious:

**Criticism**:
- Overwhelming detail for marginal utility
- Difficult to remember differences
- Slowed combat resolution
- Reflected Gygax's personal interests more than player needs

**Defense**:
- Appealed to wargamers and history enthusiasts
- Optional rules for groups wanting detail
- Represented commitment to authenticity
- Showcased D&D's flexibility

The polearm rules became a running joke in RPG circles, symbolizing excessive detail and rules for rules' sake. However, they also represent the hobby's wargaming roots and appeal to certain player types.

## Production and Publication

### Creation Timeline

**1974**: Original D&D published January
**Mid-1974**: Gygax continues developing Greyhawk campaign with house rules
**Late 1974**: Decision to publish first supplement
**Early 1975**: Greyhawk written and prepared for publication
**1975**: Greyhawk published (exact month uncertain, likely spring/summer)

The rapid turnaround reflected TSR's small size, Gygax's productivity, and the material's existing playtesting in his campaign.

### Physical Production

**Publisher**: TSR Hobbies
**Format**: 68-page digest-sized booklet
**Cover**: Wraparound cover with fantasy illustration
**Interior**: Black and white, minimal artwork
**Binding**: Saddle-stitched
**Original price**: Approximately $5.00

The modest production values reflected TSR's limited resources in 1975, before D&D's explosive growth made elaborate production possible.

### Distribution and Availability

**Initial Release**:
- Hobby shops and gaming stores
- Mail order through TSR
- Convention sales
- Limited distribution compared to later supplements

**Reprintings**:
- Multiple printings through 1970s
- Remained in print until Advanced D&D superseded it
- Later printings had minor corrections and errata

**Modern Availability**:
- Out of print as physical product
- Available in PDF through DriveThruRPG and other digital retailers
- Premium reprints occasionally produced
- Historical significance maintains collector interest

## Related Products

### Immediate Successors

Greyhawk's success led directly to:

**[[Blackmoor (1975)]]**: Supplement II by Dave Arneson, offering alternative additions
**[[Eldritch Wizardry (1976)]]**: Supplement III adding psionics, druids, and demons
**Supplement IV - Gods, Demi-Gods & Heroes (1976)**: Mythological deities and heroes

Each subsequent supplement assumed ownership of previous ones, creating a cumulative product line.

### Long-Term Legacy Products

**World of Greyhawk (1980)**: First official campaign setting, expanding the implicit world
**Advanced Dungeons & Dragons (1977-1979)**: Incorporated Greyhawk's additions as baseline
**Greyhawk Adventures (1988)**: 2nd Edition update
**Greyhawk Campaign Setting (1998)**: 3rd Edition version

The Greyhawk name maintained value and recognition for decades, becoming synonymous with "classic" D&D.

## Conclusion

Greyhawk represents a pivotal moment in tabletop roleplaying history. As the first supplement for the first RPG, it established business models, design patterns, and player expectations that would shape the hobby for decades. Its mechanical additions—particularly the Thief class, variable weapon damage, and expanded spell lists—became so fundamental to D&D that they're now considered baseline rather than additions.

Beyond its immediate mechanical impact, Greyhawk demonstrated that D&D was a living, evolving game rather than a static product. It proved that players wanted more options, more complexity, and ongoing support. It established Gary Gygax as D&D's primary creative voice, beginning the transition from the Gygax-Arneson collaboration toward Gygax's sole creative control.

The supplement's influence extends beyond D&D to the entire RPG industry. The model of ongoing supplementation, the concept of optional rules expanding core games, and the pattern of designer-driven expansions all trace back to Greyhawk's success. Every RPG supplement published since owes something to Supplement I's pioneering example.

For historians and players today, Greyhawk remains essential to understanding D&D's development. It captures the moment when D&D began transforming from its original wargaming-influenced form into the more complex, class-based, dungeon-delving game that would define fantasy RPGs. Its additions feel simultaneously dated (polearm minutiae) and timeless (the Thief's percentage-based skills), reflecting both its historical moment and its lasting influence.
