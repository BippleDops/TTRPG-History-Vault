# Unearthed Arcana (1985)

**Type**: Rules Expansion Supplement
**Parent Game**: [[Advanced Dungeons & Dragons (1977)]]
**Publisher**: [[TSR]]
**Designer**: [[Gary Gygax]]
**Published**: August 1985
**Page Count**: 128 pages
**Historical Significance**: 4/5
**Innovation Score**: 2/5
**Critical Reception**: Controversial and Problematic

## Overview

Unearthed Arcana, published in August 1985, represents both Gary Gygax's last major contribution to AD&D and one of the game's most controversial supplements. A compilation of new character options including the Barbarian and Cavalier base classes, weapon specialization rules, exotic playable races, additional spells, and magical items, it offered a massive expansion of player choices. However, the supplement became infamous for accelerating power creep, introducing poorly balanced mechanics, and undermining the careful design of the core AD&D books.

The book arrived at a turbulent time for both Gygax and TSR. Gygax was being pushed out of the company he co-founded, fighting legal and financial battles, and watching his creative control slip away. Unearthed Arcana reads as a compilation of house rules, variant systems, and experimental mechanics—some playtested extensively in Gygax's home campaign, others seemingly rushed to publication. The result felt less like carefully designed expansion and more like a grab bag of options varying wildly in quality and balance.

Despite (or perhaps because of) its problems, Unearthed Arcana sold extremely well and influenced how D&D approached optional rules, character customization, and power progression. Its weapon specialization system became foundational to later editions, while its class designs highlighted what not to do when balancing character options. The supplement represents a cautionary tale about ambitious expansions without adequate playtesting, development, and editorial oversight.

## Historical Context

### Gary Gygax's Departure from TSR (1985)

Unearthed Arcana emerged during Gygax's final months at TSR:

**Corporate Conflict**:
- Gygax fighting Blume brothers for TSR control
- Legal battles consuming time and energy
- Company management position threatened
- Creative focus diminished by business concerns

**Final Projects**:
- Unearthed Arcana published August 1985
- [[Oriental Adventures (1985)]] same year (primarily David Cook)
- Last official AD&D work before departure
- October 1985: Lorraine Williams gains TSR control
- December 1985: Gygax effectively removed from TSR

**Design Process**:
- Rushed to publication during crisis period
- Limited playtesting beyond Gygax's campaign
- Compilation of existing house rules
- Less refinement than ideal
- Editorial oversight compromised

The supplement thus represents end of Gygax era, for better and worse.

### AD&D's Evolution by 1985

AD&D had been available for years:

**Core Books Complete** (1977-1979):
- Monster Manual (1977)
- Players Handbook (1978)
- Dungeon Masters Guide (1979)
- System stable and established

**Supplement History**:
- [[Deities & Demigods (1980)]]
- Fiend Folio (1981)
- Monster Manual II (1983)
- Multiple modules and accessories
- Growing complexity through additions

**Player Expectations**:
- Demand for more character options
- Power escalation expected
- Customization valued
- Supplements seen as necessary expansions

**Compatibility Concerns**:
- Each supplement adding complexity
- Tracking what was "official"
- Campaign consistency challenges
- DM burden increased

Unearthed Arcana pushed these trends further, expanding options while exacerbating balance issues.

### Competition and Market Pressure

By 1985, TSR faced competition:

**Alternative Games**:
- RuneQuest offering different experience
- GURPS development underway
- Champions for superheroes
- Call of Cthulhu gaining popularity
- Palladium's Rifts and other systems

**Player Sophistication**:
- Long-term campaigns seeking depth
- High-level play more common
- Customization expectations increasing
- House rules proliferating

**Business Needs**:
- TSR financial difficulties
- Need for high-margin products
- Established fanbase to monetize
- Supplement sales crucial

Unearthed Arcana addressed market demand for expansion while reflecting TSR's financial pressures.

## Content Overview

### Structure and Organization

Unearthed Arcana's 128 pages pack extensive content:

**Part I: Character Classes** (pages 4-28)
- Barbarian base class
- Cavalier base class
- Thief-Acrobat sub-class
- Revised class mechanics
- Multi-class and dual-class modifications

**Part II: Character Races** (pages 29-39)
- Drow (dark elf) PC race
- Svirfneblin (deep gnome)
- Half-ogre
- Duergar (gray dwarf)
- Additional racial options

**Part III: Spells** (pages 40-85)
- Cantrips for all spell-casting classes
- New spells (dozens across all levels)
- Expanded spell selections
- Variant casting rules

**Part IV: Combat** (pages 86-95)
- Weapon specialization system
- Revised weapon vs. armor table
- Combat maneuvers
- Additional combat options

**Part V: Equipment and Magic Items** (pages 96-128)
- New weapons and equipment
- Magical items
- Artifacts and relics additions
- Miscellaneous gear

The organization was functional but reflected compilation nature rather than cohesive design vision.

## Mechanical Additions

### The Barbarian Class

Unearthed Arcana's Barbarian became iconic despite problems:

**Barbarian Characteristics**:
- **Hit Dice**: d12 (best in game)
- **Combat**: Superior to Fighter
- **Armor Limitations**: Light armor only
- **Skills**: Wilderness abilities
- **Illiteracy**: Mechanically enforced (controversial)

**Special Abilities**:

**Rage** (prototype):
- Bonus to hit and damage
- Improved AC
- Limited uses per day
- Temporary hit points
- Foundation for later editions' rage

**Wilderness Skills**:
- Climb cliffs and trees
- Hide in natural terrain
- First aid
- Survival expertise
- Animal empathy

**Physical Prowess**:
- Superior movement speed
- Jumping and leaping bonuses
- Endurance and stamina
- Natural environment advantages

**Cultural Restrictions**:
- Cannot use most magic items
- Limited spell tolerance
- Suspicion of civilization
- Code of conduct requirements

**Balance Issues**:

**Overpowered Elements**:
- d12 hit dice excessive
- Combat bonuses stacked on Fighter base
- Rage provided significant advantage
- Better than Fighter in most scenarios

**Weird Restrictions**:
- Enforced illiteracy seemed punitive and stereotypical
- Magic item restriction frustrating
- Cultural requirements limiting
- "Noble savage" trope problematic

**Legacy**:
Despite problems, Barbarian concept endured:
- Appeared in 3rd Edition as official class
- 5th Edition Barbarian refined mechanics
- Rage became signature ability
- Core D&D class today

### The Cavalier Class

Perhaps Unearthed Arcana's most problematic addition:

**Cavalier Characteristics**:
- **Superior Fighter**: Better than base Fighter at everything
- **Mounted Combat**: Specialized bonuses
- **Honor Code**: Strict behavioral requirements
- **Social Status**: Nobility and breeding
- **Weapon Bonuses**: Built-in specialization

**Special Abilities**:

**Combat Superiority**:
- Better hit dice than Fighter (eventually)
- Bonus to hit with favored weapons
- Damage bonuses
- Armor class improvements
- Multiple attacks earlier

**Mounted Excellence**:
- Superior horseback riding
- Charge bonuses
- Mounted combat advantages
- Warhorse bond

**Social Benefits**:
- Automatic leadership
- Followers and hirelings
- Social status recognition
- Wealth and property

**Honor Requirements**:
- Lawful good alignment only
- Chivalric code mandatory
- Penalties for code violation
- Could lose class abilities

**Why Cavalier Was Problematic**:

**Power Creep Exemplar**:
- Strictly better than Fighter
- No significant drawbacks
- Honor code rarely enforced
- All benefits, minimal costs

**Class Redundancy**:
- Paladin already filled mounted knight niche
- Fighter subclass would have made more sense
- Required Fighter be simultaneously nerfed and available
- Created weird class hierarchy

**Elitism**:
- Required noble birth
- Reinforced class privilege
- Limited accessibility
- Snobbish tone

**Design Failure**:
Cavalier demonstrated how not to design classes:
- Power without proportionate cost
- Overlapping niches
- Better version of existing class
- Privilege over interesting mechanics

**Reception**:
- Widely criticized even in 1985
- Many DMs banned Cavalier
- Rarely appeared in later editions
- Cautionary example for designers

The Cavalier's failure influenced AD&D 2nd Edition's more conservative approach to class design.

### Weapon Specialization

One of Unearthed Arcana's most influential additions:

**System Overview**:
- Fighters (and sub-classes) could specialize in weapons
- Required spending weapon proficiency slots
- Provided significant bonuses

**Specialization Levels**:

**Specialized**:
- +1 to hit
- +1 to damage (melee) or +2 damage (missile)
- Additional attacks per round (eventually)

**Double Specialized** (later addition):
- +2 to hit
- +2 damage (melee) or +3 damage (missile)
- Even more attacks

**Benefits**:
- Made weapon choice meaningful
- Fighter differentiation
- Tactical specialization
- Character customization

**Problems**:

**Power Creep**:
- Significant combat advantage
- Made non-specialized fighters weak by comparison
- Escalated power expectations
- Created specialist arms race

**Class Imbalance**:
- Only Fighters could specialize
- Made Fighters significantly better at combat
- Widened gap between martial and caster classes
- Required Fighter specialization to remain competitive

**Optimization Pressure**:
- Best weapons obvious (long sword, long bow)
- Encouraged min-maxing
- Reduced weapon diversity
- "Trap" weapon choices

**Legacy**:

Despite problems, weapon specialization persisted:
- **2nd Edition**: Included with modifications
- **3rd Edition**: Weapon Focus and Specialization feats
- **5th Edition**: Fighting styles and weapon mastery
- Core concept of martial specialization established

The system's endurance despite balance issues showed appeal of customization and specialization.

### Playable Drow

Making drow (dark elves) playable characters proved controversial:

**Drow Characteristics**:
- **Ability bonuses**: Superior to other elves
- **Magic resistance**: Significant percentage
- **Spell-like abilities**: Darkness, faerie fire, levitate, etc.
- **Infravision**: Superior darkvision
- **Weaknesses**: Sunlight vulnerability

**Drizzit Effect** (before Drizzit):
- Allowed playing iconic villain race
- "Good drow" concept
- Broken from evil society trope
- Overused in campaigns

**Balance Issues**:

**Overpowered**:
- Abilities far exceeded other races
- Magic resistance broke many encounters
- Spell-like abilities huge advantage
- Cost (level adjustments) insufficient

**Sunlight Weakness**:
- Intended balancing factor
- Often ignored or trivialized
- Underground campaigns bypassed entirely
- Not proportionate to benefits

**Setting Issues**:
- Drow rarity made PC drow absurd
- Surface world reaction poorly addressed
- Evil society origins created roleplaying challenges
- Lore consistency problems

**Cultural Impact**:
- Enabled R.A. Salvatore's Drizzt Do'Urden (1988)
- "Drizzt clone" became derogatory term
- Overused character concept
- Eventually led to restrictions/bans

**Legacy**:
Later editions handled drow carefully:
- Higher level adjustments (3E)
- Rare player option
- 5E made available but emphasized rarity
- Lore evolved to allow surface drow communities

### Cantrips

Minor spells for beginning casters:

**Concept**:
- 0-level spells
- Minimal magical effects
- Unlimited or frequent use
- Utility rather than combat
- Flavor and world-building

**Cantrip Categories**:

**Mage Cantrips**:
- Clean, color, dry
- Flavor food, freshen
- Tie, untie
- Dozens of minor effects

**Priest Cantrips**:
- Purify, bless minor objects
- Ceremonial effects
- Guidance and omens
- Healing minor injuries (tiny amounts)

**Illusionist Cantrips**:
- Minor sensory tricks
- Harmless phantasms
- Entertainment magic
- Distraction effects

**Design Purpose**:
- Make low-level casters feel magical
- Utility beyond combat
- World-building through minor magic
- Reduce reliance on combat spells

**Reception**:
- Generally well-received
- Added flavor to campaigns
- Some DMs found bookkeeping excessive
- Became standard in later editions

**Influence**:
- **3rd Edition**: 0-level spells standard
- **5th Edition**: Cantrips core mechanic
- Unlimited-use minor magic became expected
- Changed how magic-users functioned

Cantrips were one of Unearthed Arcana's unambiguous successes.

## Cultural Impact

### Power Creep Acceleration

Unearthed Arcana became synonymous with power creep:

**Definition**:
Power creep occurs when new options consistently exceed existing ones, inflating baseline power and trivializing earlier content.

**How Unearthed Arcana Contributed**:

**Superior Classes**:
- Barbarian better than Fighter
- Cavalier strictly superior to Fighter
- No reason to play base classes

**Racial Power**:
- Drow far exceeded other races
- Half-ogre and other exotic races very strong
- Standard races seemed weak

**Weapon Specialization**:
- Non-specialized fighters weak
- Expected for competitiveness
- Escalated baseline power

**Spell Additions**:
- Many new spells very powerful
- Existing spells outclassed
- Spell selection optimization essential

**Consequences**:

**Campaign Balance**:
- Older characters underpowered
- Monsters designed for base classes too weak
- Adventure modules outdated
- DM adaptation required

**Arms Race Mentality**:
- Players expected newest options
- Supplements necessary to remain competitive
- "Vanilla" characters inferior
- Optimization pressure increased

**Edition Tensions**:
- Contributed to need for 2nd Edition
- Clean slate desired
- Return to simpler, balanced game
- Lessons learned about supplement power

**Industry Lessons**:

**Subsequent Editions**:
- 2nd Edition more conservative initially
- 3rd Edition eventually embraced customization carefully
- 4th Edition fought power creep systematically
- 5th Edition learned to keep options balanced

**Other Games**:
- Power creep recognized as serious problem
- Design practices evolved
- Playtesting standards improved
- Balance as priority established

Unearthed Arcana became cautionary example taught in game design courses.

### Last Hurrah of Gygax Era

The supplement represented end of an era:

**Gygax's Final Statement**:
- Compilation of house rules from his campaign
- Ideas he wanted in print
- Creative vision before departure
- Legacy preserved despite flaws

**Design Philosophy Exemplified**:

**Gygax's Approach**:
- Options and complexity valued
- DM empowerment over rigid balance
- Simulationist detail appreciated
- Player agency through choices

**Strengths Visible**:
- Creative imagination
- Willingness to experiment
- Trust in DM adjudication
- Player enjoyment prioritized

**Weaknesses Apparent**:

**Transition Point**:

**Before Unearthed Arcana**:
- Gygax-influenced AD&D

**After Unearthed Arcana**:

The supplement's mixed reception foreshadowed changing expectations for what D&D should be.

## Controversies and Discussions

### The Cavalier Problem

Cavalier epitomized bad class design:

**Design Failures**:

**No Niche**:
- Fighter already existed
- Paladin covered mounted knight
- No unique role
- Redundancy without purpose

**Strict Superiority**:
- Better hit points
- Better combat bonuses
- Social advantages
- Minimal drawbacks

**Honor Code Fiction**:
- Rarely enforced
- Players ignored restrictions
- DM burden adjudicating
- Toothless limitation

**Social Elitism**:
- Required nobility
- Reinforced class privilege
- Excluded lower-class characters
- Uncomfortable real-world parallels

**Why Include It?**:
Likely reasons:
- Gygax's campaign used it
- Rushed publication
- Insufficient critical evaluation
- Editorial oversight lacking

**Learning Moment**:
Cavalier taught entire generation of designers what NOT to do.

### Barbarian Illiteracy

Enforced illiteracy raised concerns:

**Mechanic**:
- Barbarians mechanically illiterate
- Could learn literacy (losing abilities)
- Represented "uncivilized" nature
- Cultural restriction

**Problematic Aspects**:

**Stereotype Reinforcement**:
- "Noble savage" trope
- Barbarians as primitive
- Civilization versus savagery binary
- Eurocentric bias

**Mechanical Punishment**:
- Reading common in D&D
- Maps, scrolls, books important
- Illiteracy significant handicap
- Learning literacy too costly

**Historical Inaccuracy**:
- Many "barbarian" cultures had writing
- Literacy not civilization marker
- Oral traditions sophisticated
- False dichotomy

**Modern Perspective**:
Later editions made literacy:
- Background choice, not class feature
- Setting-specific
- Not punitive
- Optional rather than mandatory

### Drow Player Character Issues

Playable drow created ongoing problems:

**Initial Issues**:

**Power Level**:
- Clearly overpowered
- Magic resistance broke encounters
- Spell-like abilities excessive
- Sunlight weakness insufficient counter

**Setting Consistency**:
- Drow extremely rare on surface
- Evil society made good drow absurd
- Cultural isolation poorly addressed
- Lore contradictions

**Drizzit Clones**:
After R.A. Salvatore's Drizzt Do'Urden:
- Every campaign had "good drow"
- Backstory formulaic
- Overused to point of parody
- Original concept became cliché

**DM Headaches**:
- How do surface societies react?
- Constant suspicion and hostility?
- Ignore race entirely?
- No good solutions

**Long-Term**:
- Higher level adjustments in 3E
- Discouraged but available
- 5E made more balanced
- Still controversial choice

## Conclusion

Unearthed Arcana stands as one of D&D's most instructive failures—commercially successful yet design-problematic, influential despite obvious flaws, memorable for wrong reasons. It gave the game weapon specialization, cantrips, and demonstrated the appeal of character customization, while also exemplifying power creep, poor class design, and the dangers of rushing supplements to publication.

The supplement emerged at crucial transition: Gary Gygax's departure from TSR, AD&D's maturation into established system, and the hobby's growing sophistication about game design. Its mixed legacy reflects these tensions—valuable ideas poorly executed, ambitious vision undermined by insufficient development, creator's final statement made under duress.

For game design students, Unearthed Arcana provides case study in what happens when ambition exceeds execution, when house rules become published content without adequate testing, and when power creep goes unchecked. The Cavalier demonstrates how not to design classes; playable drow show the dangers of overpowered options; weapon specialization illustrates ideas that work despite balance problems.

For D&D historians, the supplement marks end of the Gygax era and beginning of corporate TSR's stewardship. It captures the original game's freewheeling creativity and its growing pains, showing both the appeal of Gygax's design philosophy and its limitations when commercial product standards applied.

Modern readers can extract value from Unearthed Arcana by understanding its context, appreciating its innovations (cantrips, specialization concepts), learning from its failures (Cavalier, power creep), and recognizing that even deeply flawed products can influence and improve the games that follow. The supplement's greatest legacy may be teaching the industry—through negative example—how to design, develop, and publish better expansions.
