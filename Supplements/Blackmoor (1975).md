# Blackmoor (1975)

**Type**: Rules Expansion and Adventure Supplement
**Parent Game**: [[Dungeons & Dragons (1974)]]
**Publisher**: [[TSR]]
**Designer**: [[Dave Arneson]], David A. Ritchie
**Published**: 1975
**Page Count**: 56 pages
**Historical Significance**: 5/5
**Innovation Score**: 4/5
**Critical Reception**: Influential but Uneven

## Overview

Blackmoor, officially titled "Supplement II: Blackmoor," holds a unique place in RPG history as both the second D&D supplement and the first published adventure module for any tabletop roleplaying game. Released in late 1975, it represented [[Dave Arneson]]'s vision for D&D's expansion, offering a markedly different approach from [[Gary Gygax]]'s [[Greyhawk (1975)]]. Where Greyhawk focused on polished mechanical additions, Blackmoor presented more experimental rules, narrative elements, and a glimpse into Arneson's legendary campaign world.

The supplement introduced the Monk and Assassin classes, provided the first underwater adventuring rules, and included "The Temple of the Frog," which became the first published adventure scenario in RPG history. Beyond these firsts, Blackmoor showcased Arneson's more loose, story-focused design philosophy, contrasting with Gygax's increasingly systematic approach and foreshadowing the creative tensions that would ultimately fracture their partnership.

Blackmoor's legacy is complex. While less polished than Greyhawk and occasionally contradictory in its rules, it introduced concepts and approaches that would profoundly influence RPG design. Its emphasis on adventure scenarios, exotic settings, and experimental mechanics demonstrated that D&D could be more than dungeon crawling, expanding the game's possibilities even as it revealed the growing divergence between its two creators' visions.

## Historical Context

### The Arneson-Gygax Dynamic (1975)

By late 1975, the relationship between D&D's co-creators was already showing strain:

**Creative Divergence**:
- [[Gary Gygax]] was based in Lake Geneva, WI, developing his systematic Greyhawk campaign
- [[Dave Arneson]] remained in Minnesota, running his more improvisational Blackmoor campaign
- Geographic separation meant less collaboration
- Different design philosophies becoming apparent
- Communication primarily through letters and phone calls

**Business Tensions**:
- Gygax increasingly the public face of D&D
- Arneson's contributions less visible to players
- Royalty and credit arrangements beginning to cause friction
- Gygax's hands-on role at [[TSR]] versus Arneson's distance

**Supplement II as Statement**:
- Blackmoor gave Arneson opportunity to present his vision
- Showcase his campaign innovations
- Demonstrate alternative to Gygax's approach
- Establish his legacy in D&D's development

### Blackmoor Campaign History

Arneson's Blackmoor campaign predated D&D itself and contained the seeds of the roleplaying game concept:

**Origins (1970-1971)**:
- Evolved from Napoleonic naval wargaming campaign
- Shifted to medieval fantasy setting called "Blackmoor"
- First game reportedly in 1971
- Players included Dave Megarry, David Wesely, and others

**Proto-D&D Elements**:
- Individual character focus rather than armies
- Character advancement through experience
- Dungeon exploration (Blackmoor dungeon beneath Castle Blackmoor)
- Referee-created scenarios and challenges
- Character death and continuity

**Gygax Collaboration**:
- Arneson demonstrated game to Gygax in 1972
- Gygax expanded and systematized Arneson's concepts
- Original D&D (1974) emerged from collaboration
- Blackmoor elements incorporated but often modified

The supplement offered players their first glimpse into this legendary campaign that had birthed the RPG concept.

### Post-Greyhawk Environment

Greyhawk's 1975 success created expectations:

**Player Anticipation**:
- Demand for more content and expansions
- Interest in both Gygax and Arneson's visions
- Curiosity about Blackmoor's legendary status
- Desire for new classes, rules, and options

**Commercial Pressure**:
- TSR wanted to capitalize on supplement model
- Need to release content regularly
- Market interest in D&D growing rapidly
- Competition from emerging RPG designers

**Design Space**:
- Greyhawk covered obvious additions (Thief, variable damage)
- Blackmoor needed different focus
- Opportunity for more experimental content
- Less polished acceptable for supplement versus core rules

## Content Overview

### Structure and Organization

Blackmoor's 56 pages organize content less systematically than Greyhawk:

**Part I: Monk and Assassin Classes** (pages 1-8)
- The Monk class in complete detail
- The Assassin class and assassination mechanics
- Advancement tables and special abilities

**Part II: New Monsters and Treasures** (pages 9-20)
- Additional monsters for D&D
- Expanded treasure tables
- Unique Blackmoor creatures

**Part III: Special Mechanics** (pages 21-32)
- Underwater adventuring rules
- Disease mechanics
- Hit location system
- Insanity rules
- Gambling and revenue mechanics

**Part IV: Temple of the Frog** (pages 33-51)
- First published adventure module
- Maps and encounter areas
- NPCs and treasure
- Background and plot hooks

**Part V: Dungeon Master's Aids** (pages 52-56)
- Additional tables and systems
- Campaign management advice
- Miscellaneous rules

The organization reflects Arneson's more improvisational style, with sections flowing less methodically than Gygax's tightly structured supplements.

## Mechanical Additions

### The Monk Class

Blackmoor's Monk was perhaps the most unusual class yet introduced:

**Monk Characteristics**:
- **Ability Requirements**: Strength 15+, Wisdom 15+, Dexterity 15+
- **Armor Restriction**: No armor or shields
- **Weapon Limitations**: Specific weapon list only
- **Alignment**: Must be Lawful

**Special Abilities**:
- **Unarmed Combat**: Increasing damage with level (eventually matching armed fighters)
- **Armor Class Improvement**: AC improved by level even unarmored
- **Movement Speed**: Faster than other classes
- **Falling Damage Resistance**: Reduced damage from falls
- **Stunning Strike**: Chance to stun opponents
- **Deflect Missiles**: Eventually deflect or catch arrows
- **Speak with Animals**: Limited animal communication
- **Quivering Palm**: Death touch at high levels

**Design Philosophy**:
- Based on martial arts fiction and mythology
- Anachronistic in pseudo-medieval setting
- Exotic alternative to European-focused classes
- Power balanced through restrictions and requirements

**Monk Controversies**:
The Monk immediately sparked debate:
- Cultural appropriation concerns (Asian mysticism as exotic power source)
- Anachronism in medieval European fantasy
- Balance issues (incredibly powerful or frustratingly limited depending on campaign)
- Unclear mechanics requiring extensive DM interpretation

Despite controversies, the Monk became a fan favorite and appeared in every subsequent edition of D&D, though with significant modifications.

### The Assassin Class

The Assassin represented a darker approach to character classes:

**Assassin Characteristics**:
- **Alignment Required**: Evil only
- **Thief Abilities**: Many thief-like skills
- **Combat**: Better fighting ability than Thieves
- **Assassination**: Mechanics for surprise killing

**Special Abilities**:
- **Assassination Table**: Percentage chance for instant death on surprise
- **Disguise**: Percentage-based disguise skill
- **Poison Use**: Access to various poisons
- **Thieves' Abilities**: Pick locks, move silently, etc. (at lower percentages)
- **Spy Network**: Gain informants and contacts

**Assassins' Guild Structure**:
Blackmoor detailed elaborate guild hierarchy:
- Advancement required killing higher-ranked assassins
- Political intrigue within guild
- Assassination missions and fees
- Reputation and ranking system

**Design Implications**:
- First mandatory evil class
- Supported player-versus-player conflict
- Raised questions about party cohesion
- Challenged assumptions about heroic adventuring

The Assassin proved controversial for different reasons than the Monk:
- Enabling evil player characters
- Encouraging betrayal and PvP
- Moral questions about roleplaying murderers
- Balance issues with instant-kill mechanics

Both classes would appear in Advanced D&D with modifications, demonstrating their impact despite concerns.

### Underwater Adventuring Rules

Blackmoor provided D&D's first comprehensive underwater combat and exploration rules:

**Swimming and Movement**:
- Swimming speed based on strength
- Encumbrance effects on swimming
- Drowning rules and breath-holding
- Water pressure and depth limits

**Underwater Combat**:
- Weapon effectiveness changes underwater
- Slashing weapons less effective
- Piercing weapons function normally
- Special underwater weapons
- Spell modifications underwater

**Aquatic Encounters**:
- Swimming monsters and their tactics
- Underwater treasure and ruins
- Environmental hazards
- Pressure and temperature effects

**Magic Underwater**:
- Verbal components impossible underwater
- Fire spells useless or modified
- Water-based spells enhanced
- Magical breathing and water-walking

These rules, while crude by later standards, established frameworks that would be refined in subsequent editions and demonstrated D&D's expanding scope beyond dungeon corridors.

### Disease and Insanity Mechanics

Blackmoor introduced mechanics for two afflictions rarely addressed in early D&D:

**Disease System**:
- Various diseases with different effects
- Incubation periods
- Progressive worsening symptoms
- Cure disease spells and methods
- Death from untreated disease

**Insanity Rules**:
- Mental breakdown from stress or magic
- Different types of madness
- Temporary versus permanent insanity
- Recovery methods and timelines
- Behavioral effects on characters

**Design Significance**:
These systems acknowledged that adventuring involved risks beyond combat damage. While primitive, they influenced later games like [[Call of Cthulhu (1981)]], which would make sanity mechanics central, and D&D's own refinement of disease rules in later editions.

### Hit Location System

An optional system for more detailed combat:

**Body Part Targeting**:
- Arms, legs, torso, head as separate hit locations
- Called shots to specific locations
- Different armor for different body parts
- Specific injuries based on location

**Injury Effects**:
- Limb injuries affecting actions
- Head wounds potentially fatal
- Bleeding and long-term injuries
- Healing requirements for specific wounds

**Implementation Issues**:
- Significantly slowed combat
- Complex record-keeping
- Balance problems with targeting vulnerabilities
- Most groups ignored or heavily modified

The hit location system exemplified Blackmoor's experimental nature. While rarely used as written, it demonstrated alternatives to D&D's abstract hit point system and influenced later games seeking more detailed combat.

## Setting Contributions

### The Temple of the Frog

The supplement's centerpiece was the first published RPG adventure:

**Setting Details**:
- Temple located in Blackmoor campaign's Great Swamp
- Isolated monastery controlled by mysterious figure
- Amphibian-worshipping cult
- Hidden science-fiction elements

**The Big Secret**:
The Temple's shocking revelation was science-fiction intrusion into fantasy:
- **Stephen the Rock**: The temple's leader is actually from a crashed spaceship
- **Advanced technology**: Laser weapons, power armor, and computers disguised as magic
- **Genre blending**: Medieval fantasy meets science fiction
- **Metamorphosis Alpha connection**: Linked to TSR's sci-fi RPG

This genre-mixing was revolutionary and controversial:
- Some players loved the unexpected twist
- Others felt it violated fantasy purity
- Established precedent for Expedition to the Barrier Peaks and other sci-fi/fantasy crossovers
- Demonstrated Arneson's experimental, boundary-pushing approach

**Adventure Structure**:
- Open-ended rather than linear
- Multiple entry points
- Factional conflicts exploitable by clever players
- Sandbox elements within dungeon
- NPCs with agendas beyond "attack intruders"

As the first published adventure module, Temple of the Frog established patterns and raised questions:
- **Maps and keys**: Standard format for dungeon presentation
- **DM guidance**: Balance between detail and flexibility
- **Read-aloud text**: Limited use, mostly DM-facing information
- **Encounter design**: Mix of combat, social, and exploratory challenges

### Blackmoor Campaign World (Glimpses)

While not a full setting book, Blackmoor revealed aspects of Arneson's world:

**Geographic References**:
- Great Swamp where Temple is located
- Castle Blackmoor and its dungeon
- Town of Blackmoor
- Surrounding wilderness

**Cultural Elements**:
- Multiple factions and power groups
- Monks as wandering martial artists
- Assassins' guild as shadow government
- Eclectic mix of cultures and influences

**Tone and Style**:
- More pulp adventure than pure medieval
- Science fiction elements acceptable
- Moral ambiguity (Assassins, duplicitous NPCs)
- Experimentation encouraged

The Blackmoor glimpses suggested a more freewheeling, imaginative campaign world than the increasingly codified Greyhawk, reflecting the different design philosophies of D&D's co-creators.

## Cultural Impact

### First Adventure Module

Temple of the Frog's status as the first published adventure module gave it historical importance beyond its quality:

**Industry Impact**:
- Proved adventure modules were commercially viable
- Established format for presenting adventures
- Demonstrated market for pre-written scenarios
- Led directly to TSR's module product line

**DM Liberation**:
- Not all DMs needed to create everything from scratch
- Professional design could supplement homebrew
- Learning tool for new DMs
- Time-saver for busy DMs

**Standardization Beginning**:
- Map and key format became standard
- Encounter notation conventions established
- Treasure placement methodology
- Monster deployment patterns

The module line that followed—beginning with the G-series (Against the Giants) in 1978 and expanding massively—traces directly to Temple of the Frog's pioneering example.

### Expanding D&D's Boundaries

Blackmoor demonstrated D&D's flexibility:

**Genre Flexibility**:
- Science fiction/fantasy crossover
- Martial arts in European fantasy
- Dark themes (Assassins, murder)
- Underwater adventures

**Mechanical Experimentation**:
- Hit locations for detailed combat
- Disease and insanity for realism
- Gambling and business mechanics
- Assassination rules

**Play Style Variety**:
- Intrigue and espionage (Assassins)
- Wilderness exploration (underwater rules)
- Dungeon delving (Temple of the Frog)
- Political maneuvering (guild structures)

This expansiveness influenced how players viewed D&D's possibilities, encouraging homebrew experimentation and creative interpretation.

### The Arneson Legacy

Blackmoor represented Arneson's primary contribution to published D&D beyond the original 1974 set:

**Design Philosophy Showcase**:
- Narrative over mechanical precision
- Player creativity encouraged
- DM empowerment over rigid rules
- Experimental over conservative

**Creative Vision**:
- Story-focused adventures
- Character-driven scenarios
- Moral ambiguity
- Genre-blending freedom

**Historical Preservation**:
- Documented Arneson's campaign innovations
- Preserved ideas that influenced original D&D
- Showcased alternative to Gygax's evolving vision
- Maintained Arneson's voice in D&D's development

Sadly, this would be Arneson's last major design contribution to D&D. Advanced D&D would primarily reflect Gygax's vision, with Arneson's influence fading until their eventual legal disputes.

## Reception and Legacy

### Contemporary Reception

Blackmoor received more mixed reviews than Greyhawk:

**Positive Responses**:
- Monk class immediately popular despite controversies
- Temple of the Frog praised as innovative
- Underwater rules filled obvious gap
- Appreciated Arneson's creative boldness

**Criticisms**:
- Less polished than Greyhawk
- Contradictory or unclear rules
- Hit location system too complex
- Assassin mechanics enabling problematic play
- Organization and editing issues

**Overall Assessment**:
While Greyhawk felt essential, Blackmoor felt experimental—valuable but requiring DM curation and interpretation.

### Long-Term Influence

Despite unevenness, Blackmoor influenced RPG design significantly:

**Adventure Module Industry**:
- Temple of the Frog pioneered massive product category
- Adventure modules became RPG industry staple
- Pre-written scenarios legitimized as product type
- Format conventions established

**Class Design**:
- Monk demonstrated exotic classes could work
- Assassin showed alignment-restricted classes viable
- Both appeared in AD&D and later editions
- Inspired countless homebrew classes

**Genre Blending**:
- Barrier Peaks (1980) expanded on sci-fi/fantasy mixing
- Gamma World connections established
- Influenced setting design accepting multiple genres
- Demonstrated D&D's flexibility

**Mechanical Concepts**:
- Hit locations influenced later detailed combat systems
- Disease/insanity mechanics refined in later games
- Underwater rules became standard consideration
- Assassination mechanics evolved in various games

### Arneson-Gygax Divergence

Blackmoor crystallized the growing differences between D&D's creators:

**Gygax Approach** (Greyhawk):
- Systematic and comprehensive
- Mechanical precision valued
- Tactical combat focus
- Dungeon crawling emphasis
- Polished and professional

**Arneson Approach** (Blackmoor):
- Experimental and narrative
- Story over mechanics
- Character-driven scenarios
- Diverse adventure types
- Raw creativity over polish

**Impact on D&D's Future**:
- Advanced D&D would follow Gygax's approach
- Basic D&D would maintain simpler, more accessible style
- Arneson's contributions would fade from official D&D
- Legal disputes would eventually result from this divergence

The creative tension visible in comparing Greyhawk and Blackmoor reflected fundamental differences in how the game's co-creators envisioned its future.

## Controversies and Discussions

### The Monk's Cultural Issues

The Monk class raised cultural sensitivity questions:

**Orientalism Concerns**:
- Asian mysticism as exotic other
- Superficial treatment of martial arts traditions
- Western fantasy fetishizing Eastern culture
- Anachronism highlighting otherness

**Game Design Issues**:
- Mechanically unusual and hard to balance
- Required DM buy-in to unusual abilities
- Party fit challenging
- Power level controversial

**Historical Context**:
- 1970s martial arts movie popularity
- Limited cultural sensitivity awareness
- Dungeons & Dragons as eclectic fantasy pastiche
- [[Oriental Adventures (1985)]] would expand and complicate these issues

Modern perspectives view the Monk's introduction with mixed feelings: appreciating diverse character options while recognizing problematic orientalism.

### The Assassin Problem

The Assassin class generated ongoing debate:

**Moral Concerns**:
- Mandatory evil alignment
- Glorifying murder for hire
- PvP conflict encouragement
- Party cohesion challenges

**Mechanical Issues**:
- Instant-kill mechanics frustrating
- Guild advancement requiring PvP
- DM burden managing secret assassinations
- Balance problems

**Campaign Impact**:
- Some campaigns banned Assassins
- Others embraced intrigue opportunities
- DM skill crucial to positive experience
- Social contract tests

The Assassin's controversial nature led to significant changes in later editions, eventually becoming a Rogue subclass rather than standalone class.

### Science Fiction Contamination

Temple of the Frog's sci-fi elements polarized players:

**Pro-Mixing Argument**:
- Creative and unexpected
- Genre mashup possibilities exciting
- Arneson's original vision included genre fluidity
- Pulp fantasy tradition (like Barsoom) mixed genres

**Anti-Mixing Argument**:
- Violated fantasy purity
- Laser guns broke immersion
- Felt like gimmick
- Preference for consistent genre

**Historical Significance**:
Despite controversy, the sci-fi/fantasy mixing influenced:
- **Expedition to the Barrier Peaks** (1980): Famous crashed spaceship dungeon
- **Gamma World**: Post-apocalyptic setting with fantasy elements
- **Spelljammer**: Space fantasy setting
- Broader acceptance of genre mixing in RPGs

### Editing and Clarity Issues

Blackmoor suffered from organizational problems:

**Common Complaints**:
- Rules contradictions
- Unclear mechanical resolution
- Missing information
- Poor organization

**Explanations**:
- Rapid development and publication
- Arneson's loose, improvisational style
- Less editorial oversight than later products
- Experimental nature accepted imperfection

These issues became more pronounced as TSR's production values improved, making Blackmoor feel increasingly dated even in the late 1970s.

## Conclusion

Blackmoor occupies a paradoxical position in RPG history. Less polished than Greyhawk, more experimental than systematic, it nonetheless introduced concepts and products that shaped the entire industry. As the first published adventure module, it pioneered what would become RPGs' most profitable product category. Through the Monk and Assassin, it expanded character possibilities and raised questions about cultural representation and moral play that remain relevant. Its genre-blending science fiction elements demonstrated the flexibility of fantasy roleplaying.

More poignantly, Blackmoor represents Dave Arneson's creative voice in D&D's development—loose, narrative-focused, experimental, and willing to break conventions. Where Greyhawk showed Gygax's systematic approach becoming D&D's dominant mode, Blackmoor preserved an alternative vision of what the game could be. That this vision would largely disappear from official D&D after Advanced D&D's publication makes Blackmoor both historically significant and bittersweet.

For modern readers, Blackmoor requires context to appreciate. Its mechanics are often crude, its organization weak, and its assumptions dated. Yet within these imperfect pages lies the creative spark that helped birth roleplaying games: the belief that imagination, not rigid rules, should drive play; that unexpected combinations create memorable moments; and that the game belongs to players and DMs to reshape as they see fit.

Blackmoor's true legacy isn't in its specific rules or its particular adventure, but in demonstrating that RPG supplements could do more than add classes and spells—they could tell stories, blend genres, experiment with mechanics, and preserve designers' unique visions. Every innovative supplement since owes something to Blackmoor's willingness to try new ideas, even when imperfectly executed.
