# Eldritch Wizardry (1976)

**Type**: Rules Expansion Supplement
**Parent Game**: [[Dungeons & Dragons (1974)]]
**Publisher**: [[TSR]]
**Designers**: [[Gary Gygax]], Brian Blume
**Published**: April 1976
**Page Count**: 60 pages
**Historical Significance**: 4/5
**Innovation Score**: 3/5
**Critical Reception**: Controversial but Influential

## Overview

Eldritch Wizardry, officially "Supplement III: Eldritch Wizardry," represents D&D's most controversial early supplement. Published in April 1976 as the third official expansion, it introduced psionics, the Druid class, comprehensive demon and devil hierarchies, and powerful artifacts. Unlike the relatively straightforward [[Greyhawk (1975)]] or experimental [[Blackmoor (1975)]], Eldritch Wizardry pushed D&D toward darker, more complex territory that would prove both influential and divisive.

The supplement's psionics system—mental powers operating outside traditional magic—became legendary for both its ambition and its implementation problems. Poorly balanced, confusingly written, and requiring extensive DM interpretation, psionics nonetheless captured players' imaginations and became a persistent (if troubled) part of D&D's identity. The Druid brought nature-focused divine magic distinct from clerics, while the detailed demon and devil hierarchies established the game's distinctive approach to evil outsiders.

Most controversially, Eldritch Wizardry's cover featured a nude woman bound to an altar, and its content dealt explicitly with demons, devils, and dark magic during the period leading into the Satanic Panic of the 1980s. This created both marketing challenges and cultural notoriety that would haunt TSR for years. Despite these issues, the supplement's innovations—particularly the Druid class and the demon/devil cosmology—became fundamental to D&D's identity.

## Historical Context

### D&D's Growing Pains (1976)

By 1976, D&D was experiencing explosive growth:

**Commercial Success**:
- Sales accelerating beyond TSR's initial expectations
- [[Greyhawk (1975)]] and [[Blackmoor (1975)]] performed well
- New players entering hobby continuously
- Distribution expanding beyond wargaming shops

**Community Fragmentation**:
- House rules proliferating wildly
- Different groups playing essentially different games
- Fanzines publishing variants and additions
- No standardization across campaigns

**Design Evolution**:
- Original D&D feeling increasingly incomplete
- Players demanding more sophisticated options
- [[Gary Gygax]] developing Advanced D&D concepts
- Supplements testing ideas for eventual AD&D

Eldritch Wizardry emerged in this environment of rapid expansion and evolving design, representing Gygax's increasingly ambitious vision for the game's mechanical complexity and thematic scope.

### Brian Blume's Involvement

Eldritch Wizardry marked Brian Blume's first significant design contribution:

**Blume Background**:
- Son of Melvin Blume (TSR investor and co-owner)
- Relatively inexperienced game designer
- Interest in psionics and science fiction
- Access to TSR through family connections

**Collaborative Dynamics**:
- Blume reportedly responsible for much of psionics system
- Gygax contributed Druid, demons/devils, and artifacts
- Collaboration apparently less smooth than hoped
- Quality control issues reflecting rushed development

**TSR Politics**:
- Blume family increasingly influential at TSR
- Design assignments partly nepotistic
- Gygax's focus increasingly divided
- Foreshadowed later TSR internal conflicts

The supplement's uneven quality partly reflects this awkward collaboration between Gygax's design experience and Blume's ambitious but crude ideas.

### Cultural Environment

1976 America's cultural context shaped Eldritch Wizardry's reception:

**Occult Fascination**:
- Pop culture interest in occult and supernatural
- Movies like The Exorcist (1973) and The Omen (1976)
- Dungeons & Dragons associated with fantasy and the exotic
- Market for "edgy" content

**Growing Conservative Reaction**:
- Religious conservative movement gathering steam
- Concern about youth culture and moral decay
- Groundwork being laid for Satanic Panic
- D&D beginning to attract concerned attention

Eldritch Wizardry's demons, devils, and provocative cover would later be cited as evidence of D&D's alleged evil influence, making it a focal point in the [[The Satanic Panic (1980s)]].

Eldritch Wizardry's 60 pages cover extensive ground:

**Part I: Psionics** (pages 1-16)
- Psionic ability determination
- Psionic powers by category
- Psionic combat system
- Attack and defense modes

- Druid class complete description
- Bard class sketch
- Class advancement tables

**Part III: Demons and Devils** (pages 27-38)
- Demon hierarchy and types
- Devil hierarchy and types
- Summoning and control rules

- Legendary weapons described
- Powerful magical artifacts
- World-changing items
- Curses and dangers

**Part V: New Monsters and Magic** (pages 55-60)
- Additional monsters
- Miscellaneous new spells
- Magic item additions

The organization reflects the supplement's grab-bag nature, collecting various high-powered additions without strong thematic coherence.

## Mechanical Additions

### Psionics System

Eldritch Wizardry's psionics represented D&D's first "second magic system":

**Psionic Ability**:
- Random determination at character creation
- Very rare (small percentage of characters)
- Based on exceptional mental ability scores
- Separate power pool (psionic strength points)

**Power Categories**:

**Telepathy** (mind-to-mind communication):
- Mind reading
- Telepathic projection
- Mental detection
- Thought control

**Clairvoyance** (sensory powers):
- Precognition
- Clairaudience
- Clairvoyance
- Object reading

**Psychokinesis** (matter manipulation):
- Telekinesis
- Molecular manipulation
- Disintegration
- Animation

**Other Disciplines**:
- Astral projection
- Dimension travel
- Probability manipulation
- Energy control

**Psionic Combat**:
- Separate attack and defense modes
- Mind versus mind battles
- Complex resolution mechanics
- Often instantly decisive

**System Problems**:

**Balance Issues**:
- Psionics often overpowered or useless
- No middle ground in effectiveness
- Bypassed many game limitations
- Created party tension

**Complexity**:
- Confusing rules and exceptions
- Difficult to adjudicate
- Required extensive DM mastery
- Slowed gameplay significantly

**Randomness**:
- Random psionic ability felt arbitrary
- Some characters massively advantaged
- No player choice in acquisition
- Created party imbalance

**Unclear Writing**:
- Rules contradictory or ambiguous
- Missing information
- Poor organization
- Required extensive interpretation

**Legacy**:
Despite problems, psionics persisted through D&D's history:
- **AD&D**: Included with modifications
- **2nd Edition**: Multiple attempts to fix system
- **3rd Edition**: Expanded Psionics Handbook (2004)
- **5th Edition**: Psionics subclasses and optional rules

The system's enduring appeal despite mechanical problems speaks to players' fascination with mental powers, though every edition wrestled with implementation.

### The Druid Class

Eldritch Wizardry's most successful addition was the Druid:

**Druid Characteristics**:
- **Divine caster**: Like Clerics but nature-focused
- **Neutral alignment**: Required true neutrality
- **Weapon restrictions**: Limited to "natural" weapons
- **Armor limitations**: Leather armor only (metal prohibited)

**Druid Abilities**:

**Nature Magic**:
- Unique spell list emphasizing natural forces
- Weather control
- Plant growth and manipulation
- Animal friendship
- Elemental summoning

**Special Powers**:
- **Shapechanging**: Transform into animals
- **Nature Knowledge**: Identify plants and animals
- **Woodland Movement**: Pass through undergrowth unhindered
- **Immunity to Charm**: By woodland creatures

**Hierarchical Advancement**:
Druids featured unique advancement:
- **Limited high levels**: Only certain number of high-level Druids allowed
- **Challenge system**: Advancement by defeating higher-level Druids
- **Grand Druid**: Single highest-ranked Druid
- **Competition built-in**: Created interesting campaign dynamics

**Design Success**:
The Druid succeeded where psionics failed:
- Clear mechanical niche
- Balanced against other classes
- Flavorful and thematically distinct
- Provided nature-based alternative to Cleric

**Influence**:
Druids became D&D staples:
- Appeared in all subsequent editions
- Inspired nature-based classes in other RPGs
- Created template for divine magic variants
- Established neutral divine caster archetype

The Druid's nature focus also expanded D&D's thematic range beyond dungeon-crawling urban fantasy into wilderness and natural settings.

### Demons and Devils

Eldritch Wizardry provided comprehensive hierarchies of evil outsiders:

**Demon Hierarchy** (Chaotic Evil):

**Minor Demons**:
- Manes (least demons)
- Dretches and lesser fiends
- Servants and soldiers

**Named Demon Princes**:
- **Orcus**: Demon Prince of Undead
- **Demogorgon**: Prince of Demons
- **Juiblex**: Faceless Lord
- **Yeenoghu**: Demon Prince of Gnolls
- Others with distinct portfolios

**Demon Characteristics**:
- Chaotic and destructive
- Hate devils and fight them eternally
- Summoned through dark magic
- Dangerous even to summoners

**Devil Hierarchy** (Lawful Evil):

**Lesser Devils**:
- Lemures (least devils)
- Imps and lesser ranked devils
- Organized by infernal bureaucracy

**Greater Devils**:
- Pit Fiends (most powerful)
- Hierarchical ranks and positions
- Political intrigue and schemes

**Archdevils**:
- **Asmodeus**: Lord of the Nine Hells
- **Baalzebul** and other archdevils
- Rule layers of Hell
- Ancient and powerful

**Devil Characteristics**:
- Lawful and scheming
- Eternal conflict with demons (Blood War)
- Bound by contracts and agreements
- Corruptors of mortals

**Game Mechanics**:

**Summoning Rules**:
- Magical circles and components
- Risk of summoner losing control
- Demons/devils attempt to break free
- Bargaining and contracts

**Combat Statistics**:
- Powerful opponents for high-level parties
- Special abilities and immunities
- Magic resistance
- Regeneration and resistances

**Design Significance**:

**Alignment Exemplars**:
- Demons as chaotic evil incarnate
- Devils as lawful evil incarnate
- Made alignment tangible and significant
- Created moral cosmology

**The Blood War**:
- Eternal conflict between demons and devils
- Explained why they didn't overwhelm mortals
- Created campaign possibilities
- Influenced later planar cosmology

**Controversy**:
The explicit demon and devil content created problems:
- Religious concerns about occult material
- Satanic Panic citations
- Some retailers refused to stock supplement
- Required TSR to defend content

Later editions would rename demons to "tanar'ri" and devils to "baatezu" to avoid controversy, though 5th Edition returned to original names.

### Artifacts and Relics

Eldritch Wizardry introduced legendary items:

**Sample Artifacts**:

**The Eye and Hand of Vecna**:
- Body parts of legendary lich
- Required grafting to user's body
- Immense power at terrible cost
- Could corrupt even good characters

**The Wand of Orcus**:
- Demon prince's legendary weapon
- Powerful necromantic abilities
- Drew Orcus's attention to wielder
- Nearly impossible to destroy

**Blackrazor, Whelm, and Wave**:
- Legendary intelligent weapons
- Appeared in White Plume Mountain module
- Distinct personalities and goals
- Could dominate wielders

**Artifact Mechanics**:
- **No set abilities**: DM customized each artifact
- **Mixed blessings**: Powers came with curses and dangers
- **Campaign-shaking**: Changed entire campaign dynamics
- **Nearly indestructible**: Required quests to destroy

**Design Philosophy**:
Artifacts represented:
- Campaign capstones and goals
- Legendary treasures from D&D lore
- DM storytelling tools
- Epic-level challenges and rewards

Artifacts influenced how later editions handled legendary items and campaign-defining treasures.

### The Bard (Prototype)

Eldritch Wizardry included an early Bard class sketch:

**Original Bard Concept**:
- Multi-class progression through Fighter, Thief, Druid
- Requires levels in each before becoming Bard
- Combines abilities from all three
- Extremely difficult to achieve

**Bard Abilities** (as sketched):
- Fighting capability from Fighter levels
- Thief skills from Thief levels
- Druidic magic from Druid levels
- Musical and lore abilities
- Charm and influence powers

**Implementation Problems**:
- Incredibly complex progression
- Required years of play to achieve
- Poorly defined mechanics
- More concept than playable class

**Later Refinement**:
- AD&D 1st Edition refined concept
- 2nd Edition made Bard standard class
- Later editions simplified significantly
- Core concept of versatile performer/caster persisted

## Cultural Impact

### The Infamous Cover

Eldritch Wizardry's cover became its most notorious element:

**Cover Image**:
- Painted by Steve Swenston (credited) or possibly Darlene (disputed)
- Depicted nude female figure bound to altar
- Demonic or evil ritual implied
- Provocative and controversial

**Immediate Controversy**:
- Some retailers refused to stock
- Parents complained about content
- Contributed to D&D's "satanic" reputation
- TSR defended as fantasy art

**Long-Term Impact**:
- Frequently cited in Satanic Panic materials
- Embarrassment to TSR in conservative markets
- Influenced later TSR cover policies
- Became collector's item for notoriety

**Modern Perspective**:
- Viewed as exploitative and sexist
- Example of problematic early RPG content
- Reflected 1970s fantasy art conventions
- Part of hobby's problematic legacy

The cover ensured Eldritch Wizardry would be remembered even if its mechanical content had been forgotten.

### Demons, Devils, and the Satanic Panic

Eldritch Wizardry's demonic content created lasting problems:

**Religious Concerns**:
- Explicit demon summoning rules
- Devil hierarchies detailed
- Appeared to teach occult practices
- Confused fantasy game with real beliefs

**Satanic Panic Citations**:
- Frequently referenced in anti-D&D literature
- Patricia Pulling's BADD (Bothered About Dungeons & Dragons) cited it
- Churches used as evidence of D&D's evil
- Media coverage highlighted demonic content

**TSR's Response**:
- Initially defended content as fantasy
- Later became more cautious
- 2nd Edition renamed demons/devils
- Reduced explicit occult content

**Long-Term Effects**:
- Influenced entire RPG industry
- Made publishers cautious about occult content
- Created market for "Christian-friendly" alternatives
- Shaped public perception of D&D for decades

The controversy was disproportionate to the actual content (fantasy game mechanics), but cultural context transformed game rules into moral panic fuel.

### Psionics in RPG Culture

Despite implementation problems, psionics influenced RPG culture:

**Player Fascination**:
- Mental powers captured imagination
- Offered alternative to spell-casting
- Science fiction crossover appeal
- Distinct character concept

**Design Challenge**:
- Every edition attempted to fix psionics
- Never fully satisfied all players
- Became test of design sophistication
- Symbol of D&D's complexity

**Genre Expansion**:
- Encouraged psychic themes in campaigns
- Influenced science-fantasy crossover
- Appeared in countless homebrew systems
- Inspired dedicated psionic classes

**Other Games**:
- Other RPGs implemented psionics
- Often more successfully than D&D
- Became expected option in sci-fi RPGs
- Influenced broader RPG mechanical space

Psionics' persistence despite problems demonstrates concept's appeal transcending execution.

## Reception and Legacy

### Contemporary Reviews

Initial reactions were deeply mixed:

**Positive Aspects Noted**:
- Druid class welcomed
- Artifacts interesting for high-level play
- Demon/devil detail appreciated by many
- Expanded D&D's scope

**Significant Criticisms**:
- Psionics incomprehensible and broken
- Uneven quality across sections
- Controversial content created problems
- Editing and organization poor

**Overall Assessment**:
Less universally praised than Greyhawk, more controversial than Blackmoor, but influential despite flaws.

### Influence on Advanced D&D

Eldritch Wizardry heavily influenced AD&D:

**AD&D Players Handbook** (1978):
- Druid included as standard class
- Psionics included in appendix
- Bard class refined
- Artifacts concept expanded

**AD&D Monster Manual** (1977):
- Demons detailed extensively
- Devils given comprehensive treatment
- Demon princes and archdevils included
- Hierarchy preserved

**AD&D Dungeon Masters Guide** (1979):
- Artifacts expanded significantly
- Summoning rules refined
- Psionics attempted again
- Demon/devil cosmology elaborated

Much of Eldritch Wizardry's content, refined and improved, became fundamental to Advanced D&D.

### Long-Term Legacy

**Successful Contributions**:
- **Druid**: Permanent D&D class
- **Demon/devil cosmology**: Became standard
- **Artifacts**: Influenced legendary items
- **Nature magic**: Expanded beyond Cleric healing

**Problematic Legacy**:
- **Psionics**: Never fully worked but kept trying
- **Controversial content**: Contributed to moral panic
- **Balance issues**: Created ongoing problems
- **Bard complexity**: Required multiple fixes

**Design Lessons**:
- Ambition doesn't ensure success
- Balance crucial for new systems
- Cultural context matters for content
- Clear writing essential for complex mechanics

## Controversies and Discussions

### Psionic Implementation Failures

Psionics became legendary for problems:

**Why Psionics Failed**:

**Mechanical Issues**:
- Random determination created imbalance
- Powers scaled poorly
- Psionic combat incomprehensible
- Integration with magic unclear

**Balance Problems**:
- Either useless or overpowered
- No middle ground
- Disrupted party dynamics
- DM headaches

**Writing Quality**:
- Contradictory rules
- Missing information
- Poor examples
- Confusing terminology

**Conceptual Confusion**:
- Relationship to magic undefined
- Power source unexplained
- Setting integration unclear
- Genre confusion (fantasy vs. sci-fi)

**Attempted Fixes**:

Every edition tried to salvage psionics:
- **AD&D**: Appendix with modifications
- **2nd Edition**: Multiple psionic supplements
- **3rd Edition**: Psionics Handbook as separate system
- **4th Edition**: Psionic power source
- **5th Edition**: Subclasses and optional features

None fully satisfied all players, though 3rd Edition's approach arguably worked best.

**Community Response**:
- Many DMs banned psionics
- Others homebrewed extensively
- Dedicated fans defended concept
- System requirements beyond many groups

### Gender and Representation Issues

The cover and some content raised representation concerns:

**The Cover Image**:
- Exploitative female nudity
- Damsel in distress/victim imagery
- Male gaze perspective
- No narrative justification

**Broader Pattern**:
- Early D&D art often objectified women
- Female characters less common in content
- Gender stereotypes reinforced
- Primarily male-focused design

**Modern Reassessment**:
- Recognized as problematic
- Part of industry's evolving standards
- Example used in diversity discussions
- Historical embarrassment for TSR/WotC

**Evolution**:
Later D&D products improved representation, though progress was gradual and ongoing work continues.

### Demon/Devil Rename Debate

2nd Edition's renaming sparked discussion:

**1st Edition Names**: Demons and Devils
**2nd Edition Names**: Tanar'ri and Baatezu

**Rationale for Change**:
- Reduce controversy
- Distance from real-world religious concepts
- Create unique D&D identity
- Avoid Satanic Panic associations

**Arguments For Renaming**:
- Reduced retailer resistance
- Original names culturally loaded
- Unique names better for IP
- Practical marketing decision

**Arguments Against**:
- Bowing to censorship
- Names less evocative
- Confused existing fans
- Didn't actually reduce controversy much

**Resolution**:
3rd Edition used both names, 5th Edition returned to demons/devils as primary names with planar names as alternatives.

## Conclusion

Eldritch Wizardry represents ambitious overreach—ideas ahead of execution ability, concepts exceeding mechanical implementation, cultural boldness inviting backlash. Its psionics system became legendary for wrong reasons, its cover created lasting controversy, and its uneven quality reflected rushed development. Yet within these failures lay influential successes.

The Druid proved that divine magic could extend beyond Clerics, establishing nature-focused spirituality as viable character concept. The comprehensive demon and devil hierarchies created D&D's distinctive cosmology of evil, influencing countless campaigns and establishing the Blood War as central metaphysical conflict. Artifacts demonstrated that legendary items could transcend typical magic item mechanics, becoming campaign centerpieces and storytelling devices.

Most significantly, Eldritch Wizardry showed both D&D's potential and its limits. The game could absorb new systems, explore darker themes, and expand beyond its wargaming origins—but not without careful design, thorough playtesting, and clear writing. The supplement's mixed legacy taught lessons that would shape Advanced D&D's development and influence RPG design broadly.

For modern readers, Eldritch Wizardry is a fascinating artifact: a window into D&D's rapid evolution, the cultural environment of mid-1970s America, and the growing pains of an industry learning to design roleplaying games. Its failures are as instructive as its successes, and its controversial elements reflect historical moment when the hobby's innocence about cultural context was colliding with mainstream attention's harsh realities.
