# TTRPG History: The d20 Era (2000-2008)

The d20 Era represents one of the most transformative periods in TTRPG history, defined by the Open Gaming License's revolutionary impact on publishing, D&D Third Edition's mechanical unification, and the spectacular boom-and-bust cycle that reshaped the industry. This eight-year period saw hundreds of publishers enter the market, a golden age of third-party content, fierce edition wars, and ultimately a market consolidation that left the landscape fundamentally altered.

---

## Overview

The d20 Era (2000-2008) was characterized by:
- **Open Gaming License revolution** democratizing game publishing
- **D&D Third Edition** unifying mechanics and modernizing design
- **d20 System explosion** with hundreds of games and supplements
- **Market saturation** as publishing barriers collapsed
- **Edition wars** between D&D 3.5, 4E, and Pathfinder
- **Digital distribution emergence** with PDF sales and online tools
- **MMO competition** as World of Warcraft drew players away
- **Market consolidation** as bubble burst left fewer but stronger publishers

This era proved both the power and peril of open licensing, demonstrated D&D's continued market dominance even as alternatives flourished, and set the stage for the OSR revival and 5E's eventual mainstream breakthrough.

---

## Timeline of the Era

### 2000-2001: Third Edition Launch

- **January 2000**: [[Dungeons & Dragons Third Edition (2000)|D&D Third Edition]] announced with [[Open Gaming License]]
- **August 2000**: Player's Handbook, DMG, and Monster Manual released
- **2000**: Immediate commercial and critical success
- **2000**: d20 System Trademark License published
- **2001**: [[d20 Modern]] announced, expanding beyond fantasy
- **2001**: Third-party publishers begin d20 product flood
- **2001**: [[d20 System]] becomes industry standard

### 2002-2003: Market Explosion

- **2002**: Hundreds of d20 products released
- **2002**: Every genre covered: sci-fi, horror, modern, historical
- **2002**: Quality varies wildly from excellent to cash-grab
- **2003**: [[D&D 3.5 Edition]] released, controversial "half edition"
- **2003**: Market saturation becoming apparent
- **2003**: [[Mongoose Publishing]] and others dominate d20 market
- **2003**: [[Conan RPG (2003)|Conan]], [[Slayer's Guide series]], genre proliferation

### 2004-2005: Peak and Problems

- **2004**: D&D 3.5 stabilizes market temporarily
- **2004**: [[World of Warcraft]] launches, draws players from tabletop
- **2005**: Market oversaturation clear
- **2005**: d20 glut leads to declining sales
- **2005**: Distributors and retailers struggling with inventory
- **2005**: Industry contraction begins
- **2005**: Best d20 products still selling, mediocre lines cancelled

### 2006-2007: Contraction and Innovation

- **2006**: Publishers diversifying away from pure d20
- **2006**: Indie games gaining prominence: indie design renaissance
- **2007**: [[Wizards announces D&D Fourth Edition]]
- **2007**: Industry anticipates major shift
- **2007**: d20 market declining as 4E approaches
- **2007**: [[Paizo]] announces Pathfinder, continuation of 3.5
- **2007**: Edition war brewing

### 2008: Edition Wars Begin

- **June 2008**: [[D&D Fourth Edition]] released
- **2008**: Dramatic mechanical departures from 3.5
- **2008**: Community splits between 4E adopters and 3.5 loyalists
- **2008**: [[Pathfinder RPG]] beta released by Paizo
- **2008**: GSL (Game System License) replaces OGL for 4E
- **2008**: Third-party publishers choosing sides
- **2008**: OSR movement gaining momentum in response

### Landmark Titles

**[[Dungeons & Dragons Third Edition (2000)]]**
- Complete mechanical overhaul of AD&D
- Unified core mechanic: d20 + modifiers vs. target number
- Skills, feats, and modular character building
- Prestige classes and multiclassing flexibility
- Challenge Ratings and encounter building tools
- Ascending Armor Class replacing THAC0
- Most successful RPG relaunch in history
- Brought lapsed players back and attracted new audiences

**[[Open Gaming License (2000)]]**
- Revolutionary open content licensing
- Allowed third-party d20 System products
- System Reference Document (SRD) freely available
- Democratized game publishing
- Created unprecedented market expansion
- Inspired by open source software movement
- Ryan Dancey's vision of network effects
- Most influential licensing decision in TTRPG history

**[[D&D 3.5 Edition (2003)]]**
- Refinement and balancing of 3.0 rules
- Controversial "cash grab" perception
- Fixed problematic mechanics and class balance
- Improved Monster Manual organization
- Split community briefly but became standard
- Extended Third Edition's commercial viability
- Created edition upgrade precedent

**[[d20 Modern (2002)]]**
- D&D mechanics applied to contemporary settings
- Wealth system replacing gold pieces
- Action points for cinematic heroics
- Generic classes (Strong Hero, Smart Hero, etc.)
- Foundation for Urban Arcana, d20 Past, d20 Future
- Demonstrated d20 versatility beyond fantasy
- Moderate success, proved concept viable

**[[Mutants & Masterminds (2002)]]**
- d20 System superhero roleplaying
- Power Level system balancing heroes
- Point-buy powers instead of random
- Streamlined combat for superheroic scale
- Critical and commercial success
- Proved d20 worked for non-fantasy genres
- Green Ronin's flagship product

**[[Conan RPG (2003)]]**
- Mongoose Publishing's Robert E. Howard adaptation
- Hyborian Age setting faithfully realized
- "Sorcery is evil" mechanics
- Gritty, lethal combat
- Demonstrated licensed properties' d20 viability
- High production values and strong writing

**[[True20 (2005)]]**
- Streamlined d20 System removing hit points
- Toughness saves replacing HP damage tracking
- Simplified and faster play
- Used in Blue Rose, Freeport, others
- Alternative d20 approach gaining traction
- Influenced later retroclones and variants

**[[Star Wars Saga Edition (2007)]]**
- WotC's revision of d20 Star Wars
- Streamlined mechanics and faster play
- Destiny Points and Force Powers improved
- Better vehicle combat and Jedi balance
- Learned from 3.5 development
- Influenced D&D 4E design thinking

**[[Castles & Crusades (2004)]]**
- Early OSR retro-clone
- AD&D feel with d20 mechanics
- "SIEGE Engine" simplifying resolution
- Troll Lord Games' flagship
- Attracted old-school players unsatisfied with 3E
- Presaged OSR explosion to come

**[[Arcana Evolved (2005)]]**
- Monte Cook's alternative Player's Handbook
- Diamond Throne setting integrated
- Completely new classes and magic system
- OGL enabling D&D alternative
- Demonstrated OGL's transformative potential
- "What if D&D was designed differently?"

**[[Iron Heroes (2005)]]**
- Mike Mearls' low-magic d20 variant
- Token-based class mechanics
- Tactical depth and martial focus
- Showed d20 mechanical flexibility
- Critical acclaim, moderate commercial success
- Influenced 4E and later design

**[[D&D Fourth Edition (2008)]]**
- Complete redesign of D&D
- Powers system: at-will, encounter, daily
- Roles: defender, striker, leader, controller
- Tactical grid-based combat emphasis
- Minions, skill challenges, and other innovations
- Digital integration planned (D&D Insider)
- Controversial departure from 3.5
- Split community dramatically

### Major Publishers

**[[Wizards of the Coast]] (TSR acquired 1997)**
- Published D&D 3E, 3.5, and 4E
- Created Open Gaming License
- Market leader throughout era
- Magic: The Gathering funding RPG development
- Corporate resources and marketing reach
- Hasbro acquisition (1999) brought mainstream distribution
- Tensions between corporate and creative priorities
- Digital initiatives: D&D Insider, online tools

**[[Paizo Publishing]] (Founded 2002)**
- Originally Wizards' magazine publisher (Dragon, Dungeon)
- Licenses terminated 2007 when 4E announced
- Launched Pathfinder Adventure Paths
- Developed [[Pathfinder RPG]] as 3.5 continuation
- Became community champion against WotC corporate approach
- "Edition neutral" initially, then 3.5 exclusive
- Built loyal fanbase through excellent adventures
- Would challenge D&D dominance post-2008

**[[Green Ronin Publishing]] (Founded 2000)**
- Founded by Chris Pramas and industry veterans
- Death in Freeport launched company
- Mutants & Masterminds (2002) major success
- True20 alternative d20 System
- Licensed properties: Song of Ice and Fire, Dragon Age
- Quality over quantity approach
- Thrived through d20 boom and bust
- Maintained independence and creative vision

**[[Mongoose Publishing]] (Founded 2001)**
- UK-based prolific d20 publisher
- Hundreds of products: Conan, Slayer's Guides, genre books
- High volume, variable quality
- Conan RPG flagship success
- Traveller license acquired and revived
- Survived market contraction through diversification
- Demonstrated international publisher viability

**[[Necromancer Games]] (Founded 1999)**
- "Third Edition Rules, First Edition Feel"
- Old-school adventures for d20 System
- Clark Peterson and Bill Webb's vision
- Tomb of Abysthor, Rappan Athuk, others
- Presaged OSR movement
- High-quality adventure focus
- Eventually partnered with Frog God Games

**[[Fantasy Flight Games]] (Founded 1995, d20 era growth)**
- High production values and licensed properties
- Midnight campaign setting
- Dragonstar (sci-fi + fantasy fusion)
- Legend of the Five Rings acquired
- Transitioned away from RPGs toward board games
- Demonstrated diversification strategy

**[[Goodman Games]] (Founded 2001)**
- Joseph Goodman's publishing house
- Dungeon Crawl Classics line
- Adventure-focused catalog
- Purple Planet, Castle Whiterock, massive dungeons
- Survived d20 glut through quality
- Would launch DCC RPG post-2008

**[[White Wolf Publishing]] (Continued from Golden Age)**
- Initially skeptical of d20 System
- Created Sword & Sorcery Studios imprint for d20
- Ravenloft, EverQuest, and licensed d20 products
- Maintained World of Darkness separately
- Acquired by CCP Games 2006
- Demonstrated established publishers adopting d20

**[[Troll Lord Games]] (Founded 1999)**
- Castles & Crusades (2004) flagship
- Old-school aesthetic with modern mechanics
- "SIEGE Engine" simplification
- Early OSR movement participant
- Maintained niche market position
- Demonstrated alternative to mainstream d20

**[[Sovereign Stone]] / [[Margaret Weis Productions]] (2000s)**
- Licensed settings: Dragonlance, Serenity, Smallville
- Cortex System developed across properties
- Demonstrated licensed property viability
- Mixed commercial success
- Creative innovation in licensed games

### Key Creators

**[[Jonathan Tweet]]**
- Co-lead designer of D&D Third Edition
- Brought Ars Magica and Over the Edge experience
- Unified mechanics and modular design philosophy
- Skills and feats system architecture
- Progressive politics and inclusive design
- Left WotC after 3.0 published
- Most influential designer of era

**[[Monte Cook]]**
- Co-lead designer of D&D Third Edition
- DMG and high-level gameplay
- Epic Level Handbook
- Left WotC 2001, created Malhavoc Press
- Arcana Evolved, Iron Heroes, Book of Eldritch Might
- Demonstrated OGL empowering designers
- Returned for 3.5, left again before 4E
- Auteur designer model

**[[Skip Williams]]**
- Co-lead designer of D&D Third Edition
- Monster Manual and encounter design
- Decades of Dragon Magazine sage advice
- Technical precision and system mastery
- Stayed at WotC through 3.5
- Ensured mechanical consistency

**[[Ryan Dancey]]**
- Brand manager behind Open Gaming License
- Economic theory: network effects benefit D&D
- Most controversial figure of era
- "D&D will always be most popular if ecosystem thrives"
- Proved correct: d20 boom expanded overall market
- Left WotC 2001 but legacy endured

**[[Andy Collins]]**
- Third Edition development team
- d20 Modern lead designer
- Epic Level Handbook co-author
- Transitioned to D&D 4E development
- Bridged 3E and 4E eras

**[[Rich Baker]]**
- d20 Star Wars lead designer
- Oriental Adventures (3E)
- Alternity and other pre-3E work
- D&D 4E development team
- Decades at WotC/TSR

**[[Mike Mearls]]**
- Iron Heroes designer
- Joined WotC for 4E development
- Eventually lead designer for D&D 5E
- Demonstrated indie-to-corporate pipeline
- Tactical and mechanical design focus

**[[Rob Heinsoo]]**
- D&D 4E co-lead designer
- Powers system architecture
- 13th Age post-WotC
- Innovative mechanical thinking
- Controversial for 4E departures from tradition

**[[James Jacobs]]**
- Dungeon Magazine editor
- Pathfinder creative director at Paizo
- Adventure writing and design
- Built Pathfinder's Golarion setting
- Continuity between D&D 3.5 and Pathfinder

**[[Chris Pramas]]**
- Green Ronin founder
- Mutants & Masterminds designer
- Warhammer Fantasy Roleplay (2E)
- Demonstrated small publisher success model
- Quality-focused catalog

**[[Steve Kenson]]**
- Mutants & Masterminds lead designer
- True20 System creator
- Prolific d20 freelancer
- Mechanical innovation and elegance
- Influenced superhero RPG design permanently

**[[Clark Peterson]] and [[Bill Webb]]**
- Necromancer Games founders
- "Third Edition Rules, First Edition Feel" philosophy
- Adventure design masters
- Old-school aesthetics, modern mechanics
- OSR movement precursors

### Mechanical Innovations

**[[Unified Core Mechanic]] (2000)**
- D&D 3E: d20 + modifiers vs. target number
- Eliminates percentage systems, multiple dice types for resolution
- Ascending Armor Class (higher = better)
- Simplifies learning and play
- Became industry standard for d20 System
- Influenced countless subsequent designs

**[[Skills and Feats]] (2000)**
- Skill points spent on broad capabilities
- Feats as modular character options
- Build-your-own character design
- Eliminated rigid class restrictions
- Hundreds of feats creating customization
- Mathematical optimization culture emerged

**[[Prestige Classes]] (2000)**
- Advanced character classes with prerequisites
- Specialized roles and abilities
- Multiclassing flexibility
- Narrative-mechanical integration
- Encouraged long-term build planning
- Sometimes imbalanced but beloved

**[[Challenge Rating]] (2000)**
- Numerical rating of monster difficulty
- Encounter building tool for DMs
- Experience Point budgets
- Controversial accuracy but useful guideline
- Encounter balance emphasis
- Influenced modern adventure design

**[[Attacks of Opportunity]] (2000)**
- Tactical grid combat emphasis
- Threatened squares and positioning
- Punished careless movement
- Increased combat complexity
- Rewarded tactical thinking
- Controversial for slowing play

**[[Action Economy]] (2000)**
- Standard, move, swift, immediate, free actions
- Clear action budgets per turn
- Eliminated vague "do reasonable things"
- Enabled precise mechanical design
- Sometimes felt restrictive
- Influenced all subsequent editions

**[[Open Gaming License]] (2000)**
- Legal framework, not mechanic, but profoundly influential
- Enabled third-party publishing
- System Reference Document (SRD)
- Created entire market segment
- Network effects and ecosystem growth
- Most important "mechanical" innovation for industry

**[[Powers System]] (2008)**
- D&D 4E: at-will, encounter, daily powers
- Every class uses same structure
- Balanced action options
- MMO-influenced design
- Controversial "sameness" across classes
- Clear, consistent gameplay

**[[Minions]] (2008)**
- 4E: one-hit-point enemies
- Enables hordes without bookkeeping
- Dramatic cinematic combat
- Elegant solution to quantity problem
- Widely adopted even outside 4E

**[[Skill Challenges]] (2008)**
- 4E: mechanical framework for non-combat scenes
- Accumulate successes before failures
- Mixed reviews, execution problems
- Attempt to systematize roleplay
- Influenced later narrative mechanics

**[[Roles]] (2008)**
- 4E: defender, striker, leader, controller
- Party composition and tactical roles
- Balanced class design
- Reduced traditional archetypes
- Video game influence clear

**[[Wealth System]] (2002)**
- d20 Modern: abstract wealth instead of counting money
- Wealth checks vs. purchase DC
- Simplified bookkeeping
- Contemporary setting appropriate
- Influenced later resource abstractions

**[[Action Points]] (2002)**
- d20 Modern: spend for bonuses or rerolls
- Player agency and dramatic moments
- Metagame resource
- Adopted in Eberron and other settings
- Became standard modern RPG feature

### Pivotal Moments

**[[Open Gaming License Release (2000)]]**
- January 2000: OGL announced alongside D&D 3E
- Ryan Dancey's economic vision
- System Reference Document freely available
- Legal framework enabling third-party publishing
- Most transformative licensing decision in game history
- Created d20 boom and entire market segment
- Network effects strengthening D&D's dominance
- Inspired later open licenses in and out of gaming

**[[D&D Third Edition Launch (2000)]]**
- August 2000: Player's Handbook, DMG, MM released
- Massive commercial success exceeding projections
- Brought lapsed players back to hobby
- Unified and modernized D&D mechanics
- Critical acclaim for design quality
- Gen Con 2000 dominance
- Set gold standard for RPG relaunches

**[[d20 Market Flood (2001-2003)]]**
- Hundreds of publishers entered market
- Thousands of d20 products published
- Quality ranged from excellent to abysmal
- Market oversaturation by 2003
- Retailer shelf space crisis
- Distributor inventory problems
- Inevitable bubble burst

**[[World of Warcraft Launch (2004)]]**
- November 2004: WoW released
- Drew millions to online fantasy gaming
- Competition for TTRPG player time
- Some players abandoned tabletop entirely
- Others played both
- Demonstrated video game competition reality
- Industry adapted with digital initiatives

**[[D&D 3.5 Release (2003)]]**
- July 2003: revised core books published
- Three years after 3.0 seemed premature
- "Cash grab" accusations despite improvements
- Fixed balance problems and mechanical issues
- Split community briefly
- Became standard until 4E
- Created expectation of edition revision

**[[Dragon and Dungeon Magazine Cancellation (2007)]]**
- September 2007: print magazines cancelled
- Paizo loses licenses
- Digital Dragon and Dungeon via D&D Insider
- Community backlash significant
- Paizo launches Pathfinder Adventure Paths
- Set stage for Paizo vs. WotC rivalry

**[[D&D Fourth Edition Announcement (2007)]]**
- August 2007: 4E announced for 2008
- Radical mechanical changes revealed gradually
- Community anxiety and speculation
- GSL replacing OGL for 4E
- Third-party publishers uncertain future
- Edition war brewing

**[[D&D Fourth Edition Release (2008)]]**
- June 2008: 4E core books released
- Dramatic mechanical departure from 3.5
- Powers system and tactical focus
- Digital integration via D&D Insider
- Mixed reception: loved and hated
- Community split between adopters and 3.5 loyalists
- Paizo announces Pathfinder as 3.5 continuation

**[[Pathfinder Beta Release (2008)]]**
- August 2008: Pathfinder Roleplaying Game beta
- Paizo's response to 4E
- "3.75" - refined and balanced 3.5
- Community rallied behind alternative
- Would challenge D&D's dominance by 2011
- Demonstrated OGL's lasting power

**[[Game System License vs. OGL (2008)]]**
- GSL released for D&D 4E
- More restrictive than OGL
- Third-party publishers split
- Some adopted GSL, others stayed with OGL/3.5
- Paizo and others chose not to support 4E
- Fragmented market dramatically

**[[d20 Market Crash (2005-2006)]]**
- Oversaturation led to declining sales
- Many publishers went out of business
- Distributors and retailers struggling
- Consolidation to stronger publishers
- Lessons learned about market capacity
- Quality over quantity becoming imperative

## Cultural Context

### Community and Demographics

**Edition Warriors**
- Online forums became battlegrounds
- 3.5 vs. 4E religious fervor
- EN World, RPG.net, Paizo forums, WotC forums
- Designers and developers engaged directly
- "Grognards" vs. "4vengers" vs. "Pathfinders"
- Community identity tied to edition preference
- Toxicity and tribalism problems

**Optimization Culture**
- Character Optimization forums (CO boards)
- "Theoretical optimization" vs. practical play
- Mathematical analysis of builds
- "Tier lists" ranking classes
- "Ivory tower" design philosophy debate
- Min-maxing mainstreamed

**Digital Tools**
- Character builders and generators
- Online dice rollers and virtual tabletops
- PDF distribution via DriveThruRPG, Paizo, others
- D&D Insider subscription service
- Fan wikis and SRD websites
- Digital piracy concerns

**Actual Play Emergence**
- Podcasts beginning to feature actual play
- YouTube channels starting (pre-ubiquity)
- Still niche but foundations laid
- Penny Arcade/PvP podcasts gaining attention
- Would explode post-2008

### Industry Dynamics

**Third-Party Renaissance**
- OGL enabled micro-publishers
- Freelance designers became publishers
- Small press and indie publishers viable
- PDF-only publishers emerged
- Print-on-demand technology assisted
- Barriers to entry collapsed

**Consolidation After Boom**
- Many publishers failed during d20 crash
- Stronger publishers survived and thrived
- Quality over quantity became necessary
- Niche specialization strategies
- Brand loyalty and reputation mattered

**Digital Distribution Revolution**
- PDFs became significant revenue source
- DriveThruRPG and RPGNow dominance
- Instant delivery and global reach
- Lower barriers to publishing
- Piracy concerns but overall positive
- Foreshadowed future digital marketplace

**Licensed Properties**
- Star Wars, Conan, Dragonlance, Ravenloft, EverQuest
- Media tie-ins becoming standard
- Mixed success rates
- High-profile licenses drew mainstream attention
- Demonstrated RPG market maturity

### Global Expansion

**International Markets**
- European publishers active: Mongoose, Pagan, others
- Asian markets growing: Japan, China, Korea
- Translations and localized content
- Regional publishers and distributors
- Global conventions and communities
- Internet enabling worldwide connections

## Design Philosophy Trends

### Mechanical Transparency

**System Mastery**
- Rules providing clear frameworks
- Player knowledge of probabilities
- Optimization as playstyle
- "Rules as written" vs. "rules as intended" debates
- FAQ and errata culture
- Competitive play and tournaments

**Unified Mechanics**
- Consistent resolution systems
- Predictable probability curves
- Mathematical balance emphasis
- "Everything uses the same core mechanic"
- Reduced GM adjudication necessity

### Modularity and Customization

**Build Culture**
- Character builds as creative expression
- Feat chains and prestige class planning
- Multiclassing flexibility
- Hundreds of options creating combinations
- Optimization guides and "builds"
- Video game influence apparent

**Toolkit Philosophy**
- Generic systems applicable across settings
- d20 Modern, d20 Past, d20 Future
- True20 streamlining
- "Use what you want" modular design
- House rules and variants encouraged

### Tactical Depth

**Grid-Based Combat**
- Miniatures and battle maps standard
- Positioning and movement mattering mechanically
- Attacks of opportunity and threatened squares
- Flanking, cover, and terrain
- Skirmish game influence
- Criticized for "wargaming" vs. roleplaying

**Action Economy**
- Clearly defined action types
- Swift, immediate, standard, move, free
- Mechanical precision over narrative flow
- Enabled complex tactical options
- Sometimes restrictive feeling

### Accessibility vs. Complexity

**Beginner Boxes**
- Simplified introductory products
- Quick-start rules and adventures
- Lower page count core books attempted
- Often complexity crept back in
- Tension between depth and accessibility

**Reference Documents**
- SRD as learning tool
- Free access lowering barriers
- Online rules resources
- Community wikis and databases
- Knowledge democratization

## Legacy and Influence

### Lasting Contributions

**Open Gaming License**
- Transformed industry permanently
- Enabled indie revolution
- Retroclones and OSR movement
- Creative Commons inspiration
- Pathfinder's later dominance
- Most important legacy of entire era

**Unified Mechanics**
- D20 + modifiers became standard
- Influenced non-d20 games
- Consistency valued over flavor variation
- Predictable probability
- Player mastery of system mechanics

**Digital Integration**
- PDF distribution normalized
- Online tools and character builders
- Virtual tabletops emerging
- Digital subscriptions (D&D Insider)
- Foreshadowed 5E's digital strategy

**Market Lessons**
- Boom-and-bust cycle cautionary tale
- Quality over quantity imperative
- Market capacity limitations
- Third-party ecosystem value
- Community engagement crucial

### Impact on Future Eras

**OSR Foundation**
- Necromancer Games, Castles & Crusades presaged OSR
- Reaction against 3E complexity and 4E departure
- Retroclones enabled by OGL
- Old-school renaissance explosion post-2008

**Pathfinder's Rise**
- Paizo's challenge to WotC dominance
- Demonstrated edition loyalty power
- 3.5 continuation's commercial viability
- Would briefly outsell D&D

**5E Design Philosophy**
- Lessons learned from 3E complexity
- 4E innovations worth keeping
- Accessibility and streamlining priority
- Community healing after edition wars
- Modular complexity approach

**Indie Game Movement**
- OGL democratized publishing
- Small press viability demonstrated
- Designer-publishers became common
- Story games and OSR both enabled
- Fragmented but diverse market

*The d20 Era proved both the transformative power and market risks of open licensing, forever changed D&D's mechanics and market position, and set the stage for edition wars, the OSR revival, and eventually 5E's mainstream breakthrough. Understanding this boom-and-bust cycle is essential to comprehending modern TTRPG industry dynamics.*
