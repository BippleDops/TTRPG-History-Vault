# David "Zeb" Cook

**Active Years**: 1980-2008
**Birth Year**: 1957
**Nationality**: American
**Status**: Retired

## Biography

David "Zeb" Cook (born 1957) stands as one of the most prolific and influential designers in Dungeons & Dragons history, serving as lead designer for Advanced Dungeons & Dragons Second Edition and creator of the Planescape campaign setting. His career at TSR spanned the company's most creative period, during which he shaped D&D's evolution from Gary Gygax's original vision toward more accessible, diverse gaming experiences.

Born in 1957, Cook discovered Dungeons & Dragons during the game's early years, becoming one of countless teenagers captivated by its creative possibilities. Unlike many players who remained hobbyists, Cook pursued game design professionally, joining TSR in 1980 as the company expanded rapidly during D&D's explosive growth.

Cook's early TSR work demonstrated versatility and productivity. He contributed to numerous products across TSR's lines, learning the business while developing design skills. His early projects included work on the Expert Set rules, various adventure modules, and supplements expanding D&D's scope.

One of Cook's first major designs was Oriental Adventures (1985), a comprehensive supplement adapting D&D to Asian-influenced fantasy settings. Drawing from Japanese, Chinese, and other Asian mythologies, cultures, and martial arts traditions, Oriental Adventures introduced new classes (samurai, monk, ninja), honor systems, ancestor worship, and distinctly Asian monsters and magic. The book won the Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules and demonstrated Cook's ability to create comprehensive, thematically cohesive supplements.

Cook also led development of Star Frontiers, TSR's science fiction RPG set in a galaxy populated by diverse alien species. The game featured streamlined percentile-based mechanics and evoked space opera adventure. While never achieving D&D's popularity, Star Frontiers cultivated dedicated following and demonstrated Cook's range beyond fantasy gaming.

His work on Boot Hill 3rd Edition showed similar versatility, updating TSR's Old West gunfighting game with improved mechanics and expanded setting material. These diverse projects established Cook as TSR's reliable workhorse—capable of designing, developing, and delivering quality products across genres.

The watershed moment came in 1989 when TSR published Advanced Dungeons & Dragons Second Edition with Cook as lead designer. Gary Gygax had departed TSR acrimoniously in 1985, leaving questions about D&D's direction. First Edition AD&D, while commercially successful, suffered from organizational problems, scattered rules, Gygaxian prose that valued erudition over clarity, and baroque complexity that deterred new players.

Cook faced immense pressure and contradictory requirements. He needed to modernize AD&D without alienating existing players, reorganize rules for usability without fundamentally changing mechanics, remove problematic content causing public relations difficulties (demons, devils, assassins during the "Satanic Panic"), maintain compatibility with existing adventures and supplements, and create commercially successful edition justifying development costs.

Second Edition succeeded remarkably. Cook streamlined rules presentation, reorganizing information for easier reference. He clarified ambiguities that had spawned endless rules arguments. He removed or renamed controversial content (demons became tanar'ri, devils became baatezu). He maintained core mechanics ensuring compatibility while refining problematic subsystems.

Critically, Cook introduced modular design philosophy through extensive optional rules. Rather than prescribing single correct way to play, Second Edition presented options for different play styles—detailed combat rules for tactical players, simplified alternatives for narrative-focused groups, optional psionics, optional weapon specialization. This modularity acknowledged D&D's diverse player base and empowered referees to customize experiences.

Second Edition's commercial success validated Cook's approach. The game became TSR's flagship product through the 1990s, supporting numerous campaign settings and generating sustained revenue. While some First Edition devotees criticized changes, most players embraced the improvements.

Following Second Edition's launch, Cook turned to campaign setting development. In 1994, he created Planescape, one of D&D's most philosophically and artistically distinctive settings. Rather than traditional sword-and-sorcery, Planescape explored the multiverse's outer planes—realms where belief shapes reality, philosophy becomes tangible, and alignments represent cosmic forces rather than moral guidelines.

Planescape's Sigil—a city at the center of the multiverse atop an infinitely tall spire—served as campaign hub. Ruled by the mysterious Lady of Pain, Sigil connected all outer planes, making it nexus for planar travel, philosophical debate, and factional conflict. The setting's distinctive cant (slang), Tony DiTerlizzi's evocative artwork, and philosophical depth created unforgettable atmosphere.

The setting integrated mechanics and theme brilliantly. Belief shaped reality literally—priests of obscure gods could grant spells if believers were sufficient, philosophical factions gained powers from their convictions, and travelers' beliefs affected their experiences on different planes. This made philosophy mechanically relevant while maintaining D&D's accessibility.

Planescape represented Cook's mature design philosophy. Rather than mechanics-first design, he created settings where theme, atmosphere, and mechanics reinforced each other. The setting asked profound questions about belief, reality, and meaning while supporting traditional D&D adventuring.

Throughout the 1990s, Cook contributed to numerous TSR products while maintaining Planescape's development. He wrote adventures, designed supplements, and helped steer TSR through financial difficulties that eventually led to Wizards of the Coast's acquisition in 1997.

After the acquisition, Cook continued with Wizards briefly, contributing to Third Edition's development during its early phases. However, by the early 2000s, he transitioned away from tabletop gaming toward video games, joining BioWare to work on their acclaimed RPG titles.

At BioWare, Cook contributed to projects including Neverwinter Nights and other Dungeons & Dragons-licensed video games. His tabletop experience informed his video game work, particularly regarding story structure, character development, and player choice. This transition demonstrated how tabletop design skills transferred to digital media.

Cook retired from active game design in 2008, leaving behind an extraordinary legacy. His work shaped D&D through its most popular edition, created one of its most beloved settings, and influenced countless designers who learned from his approach to accessible complexity and thematic coherence.

## Design Philosophy

David "Zeb" Cook's design philosophy evolved through decades of work, crystallizing into principles about accessibility, modularity, and theme.

**Accessibility Over Gatekeeping**: Games should welcome new players rather than intimidate them. Cook's Second Edition reorganization made AD&D more approachable by clarifying rules, improving organization, and reducing Gygax's deliberately obscure language. He believed complexity should serve gameplay, not demonstrate designer erudition or exclude the uninitiated.

**Modular Design**: Different groups want different experiences. Rather than prescribing single correct playstyle, Cook designed optional subsystems allowing customization. Want detailed weapon rules? Include them. Prefer simple damage resolution? Use basic rules. This philosophy respected diverse play preferences and empowered referees to tailor games.

**Theme Drives Mechanics**: The best designs integrate theme and mechanics so each reinforces the other. Oriental Adventures didn't just reskin D&D with Asian window dressing—it introduced honor systems, clan structures, and martial arts mechanics reflecting Asian literary traditions. Planescape's belief-based mechanics literalized philosophy's importance in the setting. Mechanics should support intended experiences rather than existing independently.

**Imagination Over Simulation**: Cook prioritized imaginative play over realistic simulation. He valued mechanics that facilitated storytelling and creative problem-solving over complex systems attempting to model reality precisely. This approach kept games playable while supporting diverse narratives.

**Reorganization as Design**: How rules are presented affects usability as much as the rules themselves. Cook's Second Edition reorganization—clearer layout, better indexing, logical information grouping—made existing rules more usable without changing them dramatically. Design includes information architecture, not just mechanical innovation.

**Philosophical Depth**: Games can engage with meaningful themes without becoming pretentious. Planescape explored philosophy, belief, and cosmic order while remaining playable and entertaining. Cook demonstrated that commercial gaming products could be intellectually substantive without alienating casual players.

**Cultural Inspiration**: Drawing from diverse cultural traditions enriches fantasy gaming. Oriental Adventures brought Asian mythology and folklore into D&D, expanding beyond European medieval fantasy. This diversity created richer gaming experiences while respecting (if imperfectly by modern standards) source cultures.

**Player Agency and Choice**: Games should present meaningful choices with genuine consequences. Planescape's faction system let players align with philosophical movements affecting their capabilities and campaign role. Oriental Adventures' honor system created mechanical consequences for social choices. Cook valued systems where character decisions mattered.

**Commercial Viability**: Good design must also be commercially successful to impact the hobby. Cook's work balanced creative vision with market reality, creating products that sold well while advancing design sophistication. This pragmatism ensured his innovations reached wide audiences rather than remaining niche experiments.

## Notable Works

### Advanced Dungeons & Dragons 2nd Edition (1989)

Second Edition represents Cook's most significant and challenging design work—updating D&D's flagship product while maintaining continuity and commercial viability.

**Design Context**:

Following Gary Gygax's departure, TSR needed new AD&D edition addressing multiple concerns: First Edition's organizational problems scattered rules across books with poor indexing and unclear presentation. Gygax's baroque prose and obscure vocabulary deterred new players. Public relations issues during "Satanic Panic" required removing controversial demonic content. Competitive pressure from other RPGs required modernization. Commercial pressures demanded successful product justifying development costs.

**Organizational Improvements**:

Cook restructured rules for clarity and accessibility. The Player's Handbook organized information logically—character creation, classes, equipment, spells—allowing sequential reading for new players and quick reference for veterans. The Dungeon Master Guide similarly reorganized DMing guidance, magic items, and campaign management.

Improved indexing and cross-referencing made finding rules dramatically easier. Clearer writing replaced Gygaxian purple prose. Tables and charts received better formatting. These changes weren't mechanically revolutionary but transformed usability.

**Mechanical Refinements**:

Cook maintained First Edition's core mechanics—class-based characters, d20 combat rolls, AC, hit points, saving throws, Vancian magic—ensuring compatibility. However, he refined problematic subsystems.

The initiative system clarified First Edition's confusing simultaneous action rules. While still complex by modern standards, Second Edition's initiative was learnable and functional.

Non-weapon proficiencies provided skill-like system without replacing D&D's traditional approach. This gave characters definition beyond class abilities while maintaining simplicity.

Specialty priests replaced generic clerics for non-human pantheons, allowing customization reflecting different deities' portfolios. This increased clerical diversity without complicated new mechanics.

**Modular Philosophy**:

Second Edition's defining innovation was comprehensive optional rules. The core rules provided baseline play experience. Optional rules let groups customize:
- Detailed weapon proficiency versus simplified weapon groups
- Critical hits and fumbles for dramatic combat
- Complex initiative variants for tactical play
- Character point-based character creation alternatives
- Psionics as optional subsystem
- Numerous other customizable elements

This modularity acknowledged that no single playstyle suited everyone. Groups could adopt options matching their preferences, creating personalized D&D experiences within common framework.

**Content Changes**:

Responding to "Satanic Panic" concerns, Cook removed or renamed controversial elements. Demons became tanar'ri, devils became baatezu, assassins disappeared as player class. While critics saw this as censorship, Cook pragmatically addressed real business concerns threatening D&D's viability.

**Commercial and Design Impact**:

Second Edition succeeded commercially, dominating 1990s roleplaying market. It supported TSR's most creatively diverse period—numerous campaign settings (Planescape, Dark Sun, Ravenloft, Al-Qadim), extensive supplements, organized play, and transmedia expansion into novels and video games.

Design-wise, Second Edition's modular philosophy influenced later editions' approaches to optional rules and customization. The edition demonstrated that refinement and reorganization could be as valuable as radical innovation.

### Planescape Campaign Setting (1994)

Planescape represents Cook's most creatively ambitious work—a setting prioritizing philosophy, atmosphere, and cosmic scope over traditional fantasy adventure.

**The Multiverse Structure**:

Planescape presents AD&D's Great Wheel cosmology as fully realized campaign setting. The Outer Planes—seventeen realms embodying alignment combinations and philosophical principles—become explorable locations rather than abstract concepts. The Inner Planes—elemental sources of reality—offer alien environments for adventure.

Sigil, the City of Doors, sits atop the infinite Spire at the Outlands' center, connecting all planes. This impossible city—ruled by the enigmatic, unstatted Lady of Pain—serves as campaign hub, neutral ground where celestials and fiends, lawful monks and chaotic anarchists, all coexist in tense equilibrium.

**Factions and Philosophy**:

Fifteen philosophical factions compete for influence in Sigil and across the planes. Each faction represents different answers to fundamental questions:
- The Athar reject gods as frauds
- The Believers of the Source pursue spiritual perfection through reincarnation
- The Doomguard embrace entropy and decay
- The Fraternity of Order seeks understanding through law
- The Harmonium enforces peace through authoritarian order

Joining a faction grants mechanical benefits—special abilities, factional resources, political influence—while imposing philosophical restrictions. This makes belief mechanically relevant while creating interesting character choices.

**Planar Cant**:

The setting's distinctive slang—"cutter" (person), "berk" (fool), "the chant" (news/gossip), "addle-coved" (crazy), "barmy" (insane)—creates immediate atmospheric immersion. While sometimes excessive, the cant differentiates Planescape from generic fantasy and signals its unique identity.

**Belief as Reality**:

On the Outer Planes, belief literally shapes reality. Enough believers can empower gods, reshape landscapes, or alter fundamental laws. This philosophical premise becomes mechanical—priests of obscure gods grant spells based on belief, not objective divine power. Travelers' expectations affect their planar experiences.

This concept allows profound exploration of faith, reality, and truth while maintaining D&D's playability. Players engage with genuine philosophical questions while rolling dice and fighting monsters.

**Art and Atmosphere**:

Tony DiTerlizzi's distinctive artwork defined Planescape's visual identity. His surreal, detailed illustrations captured impossible geometries, strange creatures, and otherworldly environments. The art wasn't merely decorative—it communicated setting's tone and themes visually.

The overall aesthetic blended Victorian fantasy, surrealism, punk attitude, and cosmic horror. This created utterly distinctive identity differentiating Planescape from any previous D&D setting.

**Campaign Structure**:

Planescape adventures emphasize exploration, philosophical debates, factional politics, and planar mysteries over straightforward monster-fighting. The setting supports philosophical campaigns engaging with existential questions while maintaining traditional adventuring structure.

**Impact and Legacy**:

While Planescape never achieved Forgotten Realms' commercial dominance, it earned devoted cult following. The setting influenced numerous subsequent designs exploring philosophy, belief, and cosmic scope. Video games like Planescape: Torment (written by Chris Avellone) adapted Cook's setting, introducing it to wider audiences and demonstrating its transmedia potential.

The setting's emphasis on atmosphere, philosophy, and theme-integrated mechanics influenced designers valuing narrative and setting distinctiveness over generic fantasy backdrops.

### Oriental Adventures (1985)

Oriental Adventures pioneered Asian-influenced fantasy gaming in D&D, though its approach reflected 1980s orientalism requiring acknowledgment.

**New Character Classes**:

The book introduced Asian-inspired classes including Samurai (honorable warriors), Kensai (weapon masters), Shukenja (priests), Wu Jen (wizards), Ninja (assassins and spies), Monk (revised from First Edition), Yakuza (organized criminals), and Bushi (general warriors). Each class reflected Asian literary and historical traditions, offering distinct mechanics and cultural roles.

**Honor and Social Systems**:

Honor mechanics created social consequences for characters' behavior. Honorable action increased honor scores, granting mechanical benefits and social standing. Dishonorable action decreased honor, imposing penalties and social ostracism. This integrated Asian literary themes where honor drives behavior and fate.

Ancestor worship, family obligations, and social hierarchies received mechanical representation, creating campaigns where social dynamics mattered as much as combat capability.

**Asian Magic and Monsters**:

The supplement introduced Asian mythology-inspired spells, magical items, and monsters including dragons, spirits, oni, and mythological creatures from Chinese, Japanese, and other Asian traditions. This expanded D&D's Eurocentric monster roster significantly.

**Martial Arts**:

Detailed martial arts rules allowed unarmed combat specialization reflecting wuxia fiction and kung fu cinema. Characters could develop fighting styles granting special techniques and abilities.

**Critical Perspective**:

While groundbreaking for 1985, Oriental Adventures exhibited orientalism—treating diverse Asian cultures as monolithic "Oriental" category, emphasizing exotic elements, and sometimes trafficking in stereotypes. Modern perspective recognizes these limitations while acknowledging the book's attempts to expand fantasy gaming's cultural scope.

The book influenced numerous Asian-inspired fantasy games and demonstrated that D&D could adapt to non-European settings, paving the way for more culturally diverse gaming.

## Impact and Legacy

David "Zeb" Cook's influence on D&D and broader RPG design operates through multiple channels:

**Defining 1990s D&D**: Second Edition served as D&D's flagship throughout the 1990s, shaping an entire generation's understanding of the game. Players who started during this era learned Cook's streamlined, modular approach rather than Gygax's baroque original.

**Modular Design Philosophy**: Cook's optional rules approach influenced how designers think about customization and diverse play styles. The concept that games should accommodate different preferences through modular systems became standard industry practice.

**Thematic Setting Design**: Planescape demonstrated how settings could prioritize theme, atmosphere, and philosophical depth while remaining commercially viable. This influenced subsequent settings emphasizing distinctive identity over generic fantasy.

**Accessible Complexity**: Cook showed how to simplify presentation without dumbing down content. His reorganization work influenced how designers think about information architecture, layout, and writing clarity.

**Philosophy in Gaming**: Planescape brought genuine philosophical engagement into mainstream gaming without becoming pretentious or inaccessible. This validated intellectual ambition in commercial game products.

**Cultural Expansion**: Oriental Adventures, despite limitations, expanded fantasy gaming beyond European medievalism, influencing later culturally diverse settings and games.

**Video Game Bridge**: Cook's transition to BioWare helped transfer tabletop design sensibilities to video game RPGs, influencing how digital games handle character development, choice, and narrative.

**Sustainable Productivity**: Cook's decades-long productivity at TSR demonstrated professional game design as sustainable career, influencing generations of designers pursuing game design professionally.

## Retirement and Legacy

David "Zeb" Cook retired from active game design in 2008, leaving behind an extraordinary body of work. His contributions shaped D&D through its most popular edition, created settings that remain beloved decades later, and demonstrated approaches to design that continue influencing the hobby.

Second Edition's success established template for how game editions can evolve—refining without revolutionizing, accommodating diverse play styles, prioritizing accessibility. Planescape proved that commercial products could be artistically and philosophically ambitious while remaining playable and entertaining.

Cook's career demonstrates how sustained, professional game design shapes the hobby. Rather than single breakthrough innovations, his influence came through decades of quality work addressing diverse challenges—edition design, setting creation, cultural expansion, mechanical refinement, and helping transition tabletop sensibilities to video games.

For current and future designers, Cook's work offers valuable lessons: accessibility need not mean simplification, modularity can accommodate diversity, theme and mechanics should reinforce each other, philosophy can enhance entertainment, and professional design requires balancing creative vision with commercial reality.

Though retired from active design, Zeb Cook's influence continues through the games, settings, and design approaches that shaped modern tabletop roleplaying.
