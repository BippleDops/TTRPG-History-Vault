# Steve Perrin

**Active Years**: 1978-2010s
**Birth Year**: 1946
**Nationality**: American
**Status**: Retired

## Biography

Steve Perrin (born 1946) stands as one of roleplaying's pioneering designers, best known as co-creator of RuneQuest and designer of Champions. His innovations—skill-based character development, percentile resolution, hit location systems, and point-buy superhero construction—fundamentally shaped how RPGs handle character advancement, task resolution, and tactical combat beyond D&D's class-based framework.

Born in 1946, Perrin came to roleplaying from wargaming background, playing Avalon Hill historical simulations and miniatures games before discovering Dungeons & Dragons. When D&D emerged in the mid-1970s, Perrin was among the first wave of players exploring its possibilities. However, he quickly identified limitations in D&D's class-based approach and level-based advancement that felt restrictive compared to the character freedom he envisioned.

Perrin connected with Chaosium, Greg Stafford's nascent game company focused on Glorantha—Stafford's mythic Bronze Age fantasy world. Stafford had created board games set in Glorantha (White Bear and Red Moon, Nomad Gods) but wanted a roleplaying game exploring the setting more intimately. He recruited Perrin and others to develop what would become RuneQuest.

RuneQuest, published in 1978, represented radical departure from D&D's conventions. Rather than classes limiting character capabilities, RuneQuest offered skill-based development where any character could learn any skill. Rather than level-based advancement granting sudden capability jumps, skills improved gradually through use and training. Rather than abstract hit points, hit location systems tracked damage to specific body parts, creating anatomical detail and tactical complexity.

The game used percentile dice (d100) for resolution, providing granular probability curves and intuitive success chances. A 45% skill meant exactly what it implied—45% chance of success. This transparency made the system accessible while supporting sophisticated probability management.

RuneQuest's innovation extended beyond mechanics to Glorantha's integration with system. Rune magic connected characters to cosmic powers and cultures. Cults provided community, training, and magical capabilities tied to mythological entities. Bronze Age technology created different equipment dynamics than medieval fantasy. This tight integration demonstrated how setting and system could reinforce each other.

While never matching D&D commercially, RuneQuest cultivated devoted following. It demonstrated viable alternatives to D&D's approach, influencing designers worldwide. Perrin's skill-based system became the Basic Roleplaying system (BRP) used across Chaosium's games—Call of Cthulhu, Stormbringer, Elfquest, Elric, and numerous others. This made BRP one of gaming's most widely used systems.

Perrin contributed significantly to Call of Cthulhu's early development, helping adapt BRP to horror gaming. His work ensured Call of Cthulhu maintained RuneQuest's skill-based approach while emphasizing investigation and cosmic horror. Call of Cthulhu became Chaosium's most successful product, proving BRP's versatility across genres.

Following RuneQuest's success, Perrin tackled superhero gaming—a genre RPGs had barely addressed. In 1981, he designed Champions for Hero Games, creating the definitive superhero roleplaying game. Champions introduced point-buy character construction where players built powers, abilities, and limitations from pools of points. This allowed creating any conceivable superhero concept from street-level vigilantes to cosmic entities within unified system.

The Hero System underlying Champions used 3d6 for resolution, creating bell curve probability distribution. Character capabilities defined everything—attributes, skills, powers—numerically, allowing precise customization. The system's granularity and flexibility made it beloved among players wanting exact character concepts realized mechanically.

Champions succeeded commercially and critically, winning the Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules in 1982. It established superhero gaming as viable RPG genre and demonstrated point-buy construction's potential. The Hero System was eventually genericized, allowing use across any genre, similar to how BRP served multiple settings.

Throughout the 1980s and early 1990s, Perrin continued designing and developing games. He contributed to Thieves' World, the shared-world anthology adapted as RPG setting. He worked on Stormbringer, adapting Michael Moorcock's Elric saga to BRP. He revised RuneQuest through multiple editions. He freelanced for various companies, bringing his expertise to diverse projects.

Perrin's productivity and versatility made him one of the hobby's most prolific designers. His work spanned fantasy, horror, science fiction, superhero, and other genres, demonstrating that solid system design transcended setting. Whether Bronze Age mythology, cosmic horror, or four-color superheroes, Perrin's mechanical approaches—skill-based advancement, percentile resolution, tactical detail—proved adaptable.

By the 1990s and 2000s, Perrin's direct design work decreased as he moved toward consulting, development, and occasional projects. However, his influence continued through RuneQuest's multiple editions, Champions' ongoing success, BRP's use across Chaosium's catalog, and countless designers who learned from his approaches to skills, advancement, and character freedom.

Perrin's career demonstrates how early design innovations echo through decades. His skill-based systems influenced games like GURPS, Vampire: The Masquerade's skill approach, d20 Modern's skill emphasis, and countless others moving away from class restrictions. His hit location systems appeared in games valuing tactical combat simulation. His point-buy superhero construction established genre conventions still visible in modern superhero games.

Though retired from active design, Steve Perrin's foundational contributions to skill-based gaming, percentile mechanics, tactical combat, and superhero game design ensure his place among roleplaying's most influential pioneers.

## Design Philosophy

Steve Perrin's design philosophy emerged from wargaming background and commitment to simulation, player freedom, and tactical depth.

**Skill-Based Freedom**: Classes artificially limit character concepts. Any character should potentially learn any skill, with choices reflecting character development rather than class restrictions. This freedom allows precise character concepts—a scholarly warrior, athletic wizard, or socially adept thief become possible when skills aren't class-gated. Perrin believed player agency in character development created investment and eliminated frustration from class limitations.

**Gradual Advancement**: Level-based systems create discontinuous progression where characters gain sudden capability jumps. Skill-based improvement through use creates organic development reflecting training and experience. Characters gradually become more capable, with advancement feeling earned rather than granted arbitrarily upon reaching experience thresholds. This approach also eliminates level-based power disparities that strain verisimilitude.

**Percentile Transparency**: Percentile systems provide intuitive probability understanding. A 35% skill means exactly 35% success chance—no complex lookup tables or modifier arithmetic. This transparency lets players make informed decisions about risk and resource allocation. Rolling d100 also provides granular probability space allowing precise differentiation between skill levels.

**Special Success and Fumble**: Binary success/failure creates limited outcome space. Special successes (rolling under skill value but also under threshold like 05% or 10%) reward skilled characters with exceptional results. Fumbles (rolling 95-00 or similar) introduce catastrophic failure possibility keeping even routine actions interesting. This graduated outcome system creates richer narrative possibilities and mechanical variety.

**Hit Locations**: Abstract hit points don't capture combat's tactical reality. Tracking damage by body location creates anatomical consequences—leg damage reduces movement, arm damage impairs weapon use, head damage risks unconsciousness. This detail rewards tactical targeting and makes combat outcomes more varied and realistic. While more complex than abstract damage, hit locations create engaging tactical depth.

**Armor as Damage Reduction**: Rather than making characters harder to hit (like D&D's Armor Class), armor should reduce damage from successful hits. This reflects physical reality—armor doesn't prevent being struck, it mitigates injury. This approach also makes all attacks potentially successful while maintaining armor's protective value.

**Point-Buy Construction**: Fixed class abilities limit character concepts. Point-buy systems let players build precise character concepts by allocating points across abilities, advantages, and disadvantages. This creates flexibility supporting any conceivable character within system parameters. For superheroes especially, point-buy allows building characters from reality-warping cosmic beings to street-level martial artists within unified framework.

**Advantages and Disadvantages**: Characters aren't just capability numbers—they have positive and negative traits affecting effectiveness and roleplaying. Perrin's systems incorporate advantages (wealth, allies, reputations) and disadvantages (code against killing, psychological limitations, physical impairments) providing mechanical benefits/penalties while encouraging characterization.

**Simulation Through Detail**: Games should simulate genre conventions and physical reality within abstraction constraints. Detailed rules for combat maneuvers, environmental effects, equipment variations, and tactical options create simulation supporting genre appropriate play. While some find this detail overwhelming, Perrin believed it rewarded mastery and created satisfying tactical gameplay.

**Tactical Positioning**: Combat occurs in space with terrain, positioning, and range affecting outcomes. Perrin's games emphasize movement, cover, flanking, and tactical decisions rather than abstract combat resolution. This creates engaging combat requiring thought rather than mere dice rolling.

**Universal Systems**: Good mechanics should work across settings and genres. Perrin's BRP system powered fantasy (RuneQuest), horror (Call of Cthulhu), science fiction (various games), and other genres successfully. Similarly, the Hero System expanded from Champions to generic use. This universality demonstrates system quality and provides mechanical consistency across game lines.

**Player Agency Over Balance**: Perrin prioritized player freedom to make choices (even potentially bad ones) over enforcing balance through restrictions. If players want to build ineffective characters, that's their choice. Systems should provide information about mechanical consequences but trust players to make informed decisions.

## Notable Works

### RuneQuest (1978, 2nd Edition 1980)

RuneQuest represented revolutionary alternative to D&D's class-based approach, establishing skill-based fantasy gaming and creating the Basic Roleplaying system.

**Skill-Based System**:

Characters begin with skills based on characteristics (attributes) and cultural background. Any character can learn any skill, though cultural and professional training affect starting competencies. Skills improve through successful use—after adventures, players roll against skills used successfully during play. If the roll exceeds current skill value, the skill increases by percentage points. This "learn by doing" approach creates organic character development.

Skills cover combat, magic, knowledge, social abilities, crafts, and every conceivable activity. Want your warrior to learn poetry? Train the Art skill. Want your priest to pick locks? Learn Manipulation. This freedom eliminates frustration from class restrictions while requiring meaningful choices about where to invest limited training time.

**Percentile Resolution**:

All skill checks use d100 rolls under skill percentage. A character with Sword 55% rolls d100—55 or under succeeds, 56 or above fails. This intuitive system makes probability transparent and allows precise skill differentiation.

Critical successes (rolling under 05% or 10%) and fumbles (rolling 95-00) add exceptional outcomes and catastrophic failures. Special successes (rolling under 1/5 of skill value) provide superior results between normal success and criticals.

Opposed rolls (comparing roll results when both participants succeed) add tactical depth to contested actions like combat, debate, or social manipulation.

**Hit Locations and Tactical Combat**:

Combat targets specific body locations determined by d20 roll: head, chest, abdomen, arms, legs. Each location has separate hit points based on body size and toughness. Damage reduces location hit points—when location reaches zero, it's disabled; negative values risk permanent injury or death.

This creates tactical targeting decisions. Attack the head for instant incapacitation but lower hit chance? Strike center mass for reliability? Disable limbs to reduce opponent's effectiveness? These choices add depth to combat beyond "I attack."

Armor protects specific locations, reducing damage by armor points. Full plate on chest and head but leather on limbs creates varied protection requiring tactical decisions about where to strike.

**Rune Magic and Cults**:

Magic ties to Glorantha's mythology through Runes—cosmic powers like Death, Life, Fire, Air, Earth, Water, Truth, Illusion, Disorder. Characters have Rune affinities affecting magic and personality.

Cults dedicated to deities provide community, training, and magic. Joining Orlanth's cult grants access to Storm magic and Air powers. Joining Humakt's cult provides Death magic and warrior training. Cult membership isn't merely mechanical—it implies religious devotion, community responsibilities, and mythological connections.

This integration makes magic meaningful beyond spell lists. Magic connects characters to world mythology, creating narrative and mechanical consequences for religious choices.

**Bronze Age Setting**:

Glorantha's Bronze Age technology creates different equipment and tactical environment than medieval fantasy. Bronze weapons, light armor, chariots, and javelins create distinct combat feel. This demonstrated how mechanical and setting details reinforce each other.

**Impact**:

RuneQuest proved skill-based systems could work in fantasy gaming, influencing countless subsequent designs. The BRP system became Chaosium's house system, powering numerous games. RuneQuest maintained devoted following through multiple editions and publishers, remaining in print decades later.

### Champions (1981, various editions)

Champions defined superhero roleplaying through point-buy character construction and the Hero System's granular customization.

**Point-Buy Character Construction**:

Rather than random generation or class selection, Champions provides point pools for building characters. Standard superheroes might receive 250-300 character points. Players spend points on:
- Characteristics (Strength, Dexterity, Constitution, Body, Intelligence, Ego, Presence, Comeliness)
- Powers (energy blast, flight, super strength, telepathy, invisibility, etc.)
- Skills (professional abilities, knowledge, languages)
- Perks (wealth, contacts, reputation)

This allows building any conceivable superhero from street-level vigilante (fewer points in raw power, more in skills and equipment) to cosmic entity (massive points in godlike powers).

**Powers and Limitations**:

Powers use modular construction. Want energy blast? Purchase blast power at desired damage level. Want lightning bolts specifically? Add "electrical" special effect. Want it to only work outdoors? Add limitation reducing cost. Want it to also blind targets? Add advantage increasing cost.

This granular construction allows precise power definitions. Superman's heat vision becomes Energy Blast with "no range modifier" advantage and "must see target" limitation. Spider-Man's webs become Entangle with "limited charges" and "sticky" variations.

Limitations reduce power costs—ethical codes, psychological complications, physical limitations, power restrictions. This encourages characterization while providing mechanical benefits for accepting restrictions.

**3d6 Resolution**:

The Hero System uses 3d6 for task resolution, creating bell curve probability. Target numbers derive from characteristics and skills. This differs from d20's linear probability, creating more consistent results with occasional extreme outcomes.

**Tactical Combat**:

Champions features detailed tactical combat with movement rates, range modifiers, area effects, knockback from attacks, stunning, and complex maneuver options. Combat occurs on hex grids with precise positioning and measurement.

This detail creates comic book combat feel—superheroes throwing each other through buildings, energy blasts affecting multiple targets, speedsters moving at incredible rates, and dramatic combat reversals.

**Stun and Body Damage**:

Attacks inflict Stun (stunning effectiveness) and Body (actual injury). Characters can be stunned unconscious while taking minimal body damage, or remain conscious while suffering serious injury. This creates comic book combat dynamics where heroes trade powerful blows while remaining functional.

**Campaign Frameworks**:

Champions provides extensive guidance for superhero campaigns—villain creation, team dynamics, base design, government agencies, public opinion mechanics. This helps referees create four-color superhero worlds supporting genre-appropriate play.

**Impact**:

Champions became definitive superhero RPG, maintaining market leadership for decades. The Hero System genericized into universal system similar to BRP's evolution. The point-buy approach influenced numerous subsequent games including GURPS, though Champions' implementation remained distinct.

The game demonstrated that complex character construction could create satisfying customization supporting precise character concepts. This validated detailed, simulation-focused design approaches valuing mechanical depth over simplicity.

### Contributions to Call of Cthulhu and BRP

While Sandy Petersen designed Call of Cthulhu, Perrin's BRP system and development contributions shaped the game fundamentally. His skill-based approach proved perfect for investigation-focused horror where characters needed diverse capabilities beyond combat. The percentile system's transparency helped players understand risk when confronting cosmic horrors. His work helped establish Call of Cthulhu as Chaosium's flagship product and most successful implementation of BRP.

Perrin's development of BRP into generic system usable across genres demonstrated system versatility. This approach influenced how designers think about universal systems versus setting-specific mechanics.

## Impact and Legacy

Steve Perrin's influence on RPG design operates through multiple channels spanning decades:

**Skill-Based Gaming**: Perrin's RuneQuest demonstrated viable alternatives to class-based characters, influencing games like Call of Cthulhu, GURPS, World of Darkness, BRP derivatives, numerous indie games, and even D&D's skill system additions in later editions. His approach validated player freedom in character development.

**Percentile Mechanics**: BRP's percentile system became one of gaming's most widely used resolution methods. Its transparency and granularity influenced designers valuing intuitive probability and precise skill differentiation. Games from Call of Cthulhu to Warhammer Fantasy Roleplay to modern BRP derivatives use percentile resolution.

**Hit Location Systems**: Perrin pioneered detailed hit location mechanics creating tactical combat depth through anatomical targeting. This influenced games like GURPS, Cyberpunk, and numerous others valuing combat simulation and tactical positioning.

**Gradual Advancement**: Learn-by-doing skill improvement created organic character development alternative to level-based systems. This approach appeared in Call of Cthulhu, later RuneQuest editions, and influenced designers thinking about progression mechanics.

**Point-Buy Superhero Design**: Champions established conventions for superhero gaming that persist today. Point-buy construction, powers with advantages/limitations, detailed combat simulation, and granular customization became genre standards.

**Basic Roleplaying System**: BRP's evolution into Chaosium's house system demonstrated universal system viability decades before "generic" systems became common. BRP powered dozens of games across diverse genres, proving good mechanics transcend setting.

**Tactical Combat Philosophy**: Perrin's emphasis on positioning, hit locations, range, and detailed maneuvers influenced games valuing simulation over abstraction. This created design philosophy alternative to abstract combat resolution.

**Universal Systems**: Both BRP and the Hero System evolved into generic systems usable across genres. This demonstrated that well-designed core mechanics could support diverse settings, influencing later universal systems like GURPS, Savage Worlds, Fate, and others.

**Design Longevity**: Perrin's systems remain in use decades after creation. RuneQuest continues through new editions, Champions maintains devoted following, BRP powers active game lines. This longevity testifies to fundamental design quality and adaptability.

## Retirement and Legacy

Steve Perrin's active design career wound down in the 2000s-2010s, but his mechanical innovations continue influencing the hobby through games using his systems and designers inspired by his approaches.

RuneQuest continues through new editions from different publishers, maintaining Perrin's core skill-based approach while updating for contemporary audiences. Champions remains Hero Games' flagship product with devoted community. BRP continues powering Chaosium games and independent projects using its open license.

Perhaps more importantly, Perrin's philosophical contributions—skill-based freedom, learn-by-doing advancement, percentile transparency, tactical detail, point-buy construction—became part of design vocabulary. Designers regularly reference his work when discussing character development systems, combat mechanics, or universal system design.

For players who value simulation, tactical depth, and character freedom, Perrin's games remain gold standard. For designers exploring skill-based mechanics or tactical combat, his work provides foundational examples of how these approaches can create satisfying gameplay.

Though retired from active design, Steve Perrin's place among RPG gaming's pioneers remains secure. His innovations opened design paths beyond D&D's class-based model, proved that detailed simulation could coexist with playability, and created systems robust enough to remain relevant decades after their creation. Few designers can claim similar fundamental impact on the hobby's development.
