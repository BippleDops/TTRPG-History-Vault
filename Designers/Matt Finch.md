# Matt Finch

**Active Years**: 2006-present
**Birth Year**: 1964
**Nationality**: American
**Status**: Active

## Biography

Matt Finch (born 1964) is an American game designer, theorist, and key figure in the Old School Renaissance movement, best known as creator of Swords & Wizardry and co-creator of OSRIC, two foundational OSR retroclones. Beyond his design work, Finch's theoretical writings—particularly the Quick Primer for Old School Gaming—articulated the philosophical underpinnings of old-school play, defining the movement's principles and helping establish OSR as distinct approach to roleplaying rather than mere nostalgia.

Born in 1964, Finch discovered Dungeons & Dragons in the late 1970s during the game's first wave of popularity. He learned to play with the original white box D&D and early supplements, experiencing the game before Advanced D&D codified and systematized the rules. This early exposure to D&D's more freeform, rulings-based approach profoundly influenced his design philosophy and understanding of what made old-school gaming distinctive.

Finch pursued education and career outside gaming, working as an attorney while maintaining active involvement in gaming as a hobby. This professional background gave him analytical skills and systematic thinking that would later inform his game design work. His legal training particularly influenced his ability to write clearly about complex subjects and analyze game systems with precision.

Throughout the 1980s, 1990s, and early 2000s, Finch continued gaming while D&D evolved through Advanced D&D Second Edition and Third Edition. Like many players who started with original D&D, Finch appreciated newer editions' improvements but felt something essential had been lost—the referee's primacy in adjudication, the emphasis on player ingenuity over character optimization, and the simplicity that allowed creativity rather than restricting it through comprehensive rules.

The catalyst for Finch's entry into game design came with the publication of the Open Gaming License in 2000. The OGL allowed publishers to create content compatible with D&D Third Edition and, crucially, to use substantial portions of the core mechanics in their own games. This created legal framework for what would become retroclones—games recreating older D&D editions' mechanics using OGL-licensed material.

In 2006, Finch collaborated with Stuart Marshall on OSRIC (Old School Reference and Index Compilation), one of the first retroclones. OSRIC recreated AD&D First Edition's mechanics using the OGL, providing legally publishable reference for AD&D rules. More importantly, it established that the OGL could support games emulating pre-3E D&D, opening the door for the Old School Renaissance.

OSRIC served multiple purposes beyond providing AD&D rules clone. It created legal framework for publishers to produce adventures and supplements compatible with First Edition AD&D without licensing restrictions. It preserved First Edition's mechanics in actively maintained, print-on-demand available format. And it demonstrated that old-school gaming had sufficient audience to sustain commercial products.

Following OSRIC's success, Finch created Swords & Wizardry (2008), his own retroclone focused on original D&D (the 1974 white box edition). While OSRIC targeted AD&D's complexity, Swords & Wizardry embraced original D&D's elegant simplicity. The game streamlined mechanics to essentials, used ascending Armor Class (mathematical innovation making calculations intuitive), and provided modular approach allowing referees to adopt or ignore optional subsystems.

Swords & Wizardry came in multiple versions: White Box edition emulating original D&D's three-class structure, Core Rules adding more classes and options, and Complete Rules providing comprehensive but still streamlined old-school experience. This modular approach demonstrated how retroclones could serve different preferences within old-school gaming while maintaining compatibility.

The game's success established Finch as major OSR figure. Swords & Wizardry became one of the most popular retroclones alongside Labyrinth Lord and OSRIC, spawning extensive third-party support, organized play events (Swords & Wizardry Appreciation Day), and active community. The game demonstrated that streamlined old-school mechanics could attract contemporary audiences seeking alternatives to complex modern systems.

Beyond game design, Finch's most influential contribution came through theoretical writing. His Quick Primer for Old School Gaming (2008) articulated OSR philosophy with clarity and insight that defined the movement. The Primer identified key principles distinguishing old-school play: rulings over rules (referee adjudication based on situation rather than comprehensive rules), player skill over character abilities (success depends on player ingenuity, not character builds), heroic not superheroic (characters are capable but vulnerable), dangers are real (death is possible and meaningful), and exploration and wonder (discovery and mystery drive play).

These principles resonated deeply with OSR community, providing conceptual framework for what made old-school gaming distinctive beyond mechanical nostalgia. The Primer influenced countless designers and referees, shaping how OSR games were designed and played. It transformed OSR from retrocomputing exercise into coherent design philosophy.

Finch followed the Primer with Mythmere's Adventure Design Deskbook (2010), providing practical guidance for designing old-school adventures. The book offered systematic methodology for creating dungeons, wilderness adventures, and mysteries that rewarded player creativity and clever problem-solving. It addressed common adventure design challenges—pacing, difficulty calibration, providing meaningful choices—with wisdom from decades of refereeing.

Throughout the 2010s, Finch continued designing and writing while collaborating with Frog God Games, major OSR publisher. He contributed to numerous Frog God products, designed adventures and supplements, and helped establish Frog God's reputation for quality old-school content. This partnership demonstrated how OSR could support sustainable commercial publishing beyond hobbyist production.

Finch also worked on Spire RPG, demonstrating that his design sensibilities extended beyond retroclones to original systems. While maintaining old-school principles, he explored how those principles could inform games mechanically distinct from D&D. This showed OSR philosophy transcending specific mechanics to inform broader design approaches.

Currently, Finch remains active in OSR community through design work, theoretical writing, and community engagement. His Kickstarter campaigns for Swords & Wizardry products consistently succeed, demonstrating sustained interest in his design approach. His theoretical writings continue influencing new designers discovering OSR principles.

Finch's career demonstrates how clear theoretical articulation can shape entire movements. While OSRIC and Swords & Wizardry proved retroclones' commercial viability, his theoretical writings provided intellectual foundation explaining why old-school gaming mattered beyond nostalgia. This combination—practical design work and philosophical articulation—established Finch as OSR's most important theorist and one of its most influential designers.

## Design Philosophy

Matt Finch's design philosophy centers on principles articulated in the Quick Primer for Old School Gaming, emphasizing player agency, referee judgment, and mechanical simplicity enabling creativity.

**Rulings Over Rules**: Comprehensive rules cannot cover all situations. Referees should adjudicate based on situation, logic, and genre expectations rather than searching for specific rules. This requires trusting referees to make fair, reasonable decisions. Finch believes extensive rules constrain creativity by attempting to systematize the inherently improvisational nature of roleplaying. Simple core mechanics allow referees to focus on situation and story rather than rules adjudication.

**Player Skill Over Character Abilities**: Success should depend more on player ingenuity than character statistics. When players describe clever approaches to problems, referees should reward creativity regardless of whether specific rules or abilities cover the action. A player who describes using iron spikes to jam a door shut shouldn't need a specific door-jamming skill—the idea should work if it makes sense. This emphasis on player problem-solving creates different play dynamic than build-optimization focus of modern games.

**Simplicity Enables Creativity**: Mechanical simplicity isn't limitation—it's liberation. With fewer rules constraining actions, players and referees have freedom to attempt anything reasonable. Complex systems with comprehensive skill lists and specific maneuver rules actually restrict creativity by implying that unlisted actions are impossible or require special mechanics. Simple systems trust participants to determine what's possible through common sense and genre understanding.

**Heroic Not Superheroic**: Characters should be capable but vulnerable. Old-school gaming maintains real danger—characters can die from bad luck, poor decisions, or underestimating threats. This vulnerability creates tension and makes survival meaningful. Unlike modern heroic fantasy where protagonists inevitably triumph, old-school gaming embraces possibility of failure, making successes more satisfying.

**Ascending Armor Class**: Finch pioneered adopting ascending AC in OSR games. Traditional D&D's descending AC (where lower numbers are better, requiring THAC0 calculations or matrix lookups) creates unnecessary arithmetic. Ascending AC (higher is better, simple modifier addition) maintains exact same mechanical outcomes while being far more intuitive. This demonstrates how old-school principles can accept modern conveniences that improve usability without changing fundamental gameplay.

**Modularity and Options**: Different groups want different complexity levels. Finch designs modular systems allowing referees to adopt or ignore optional subsystems. Swords & Wizardry's multiple versions serve different preferences—White Box for maximum simplicity, Complete Rules for more options—while maintaining compatibility. This respects diverse play preferences within old-school framework.

**Exploration and Discovery**: Gameplay should emphasize exploring unknown, discovering secrets, and unraveling mysteries. This differs from modern quest-based structures providing clear objectives and paths. Old-school gaming values emergent narratives arising from exploration rather than following predetermined story arcs. Dungeons are mysterious locations to explore, not merely combat encounter chains.

**Compatibility and Accessibility**: Retroclones should maximize compatibility with classic D&D adventures and supplements while remaining accessible to new players. Finch designs games usable with vast catalog of classic adventures while teaching rules quickly. This bridges old-school gaming's history with contemporary accessibility standards.

**Referee Empowerment**: Games should empower referees rather than restricting them with comprehensive rules. Finch's designs provide frameworks and guidelines but trust referees to adjudicate fairly. This requires different social contract than rules-heavy games—players must trust referee judgment, and referees must exercise that trust responsibly.

**Death Is Possible and Meaningful**: Character death shouldn't be avoided through extensive safety mechanics. Possibility of death creates genuine stakes and tension. However, death should result from meaningful choices and circumstances, not random arbitrariness. This balance makes mortality matter without feeling unfair.

**System Matters Less Than Principles**: Old-school gaming is philosophical approach, not specific ruleset. Finch's writings emphasize that OSR principles can inform various systems. While retroclones provide mechanical frameworks, the philosophy—player ingenuity, referee judgment, exploration focus—transcends specific dice mechanics.

## Notable Works

### Swords & Wizardry (2008)

Swords & Wizardry became one of the OSR's most successful and influential retroclones by streamlining original D&D to essential elegance while modernizing usability.

**Design Goals**:

Finch aimed to capture original D&D's spirit—simple core mechanics, three core classes (Fighter, Magic-User, Cleric), straightforward advancement, and emphasis on exploration and problem-solving—while improving usability through ascending AC, clear rules presentation, and modern layout design.

The game eliminates original D&D's idiosyncrasies that created confusion without adding meaningful depth. Ascending AC replaces descending AC's THAC0 confusion. Single saving throw mechanic replaces five separate saving throw categories. Streamlined combat procedures eliminate unnecessarily complicated initiative and movement rules.

**Multiple Versions**:

Swords & Wizardry exists in several versions serving different preferences:

**White Box**: Emulates 1974 original D&D closely—three classes, race as class for demihumans, four levels of spells, simplest possible mechanics. For players wanting most streamlined old-school experience.

**Core Rules**: Adds race and class separation, thief class, more spell levels, and additional options while maintaining simplicity. Balance between minimalism and options.

**Complete Rules**: Comprehensive version including ascending and descending AC options, additional classes, extensive optional rules, and maximum compatibility with classic adventures. For groups wanting more mechanical detail while keeping old-school philosophy.

This modular approach lets different groups find appropriate complexity level while maintaining compatibility—adventures work across versions, characters convert easily, and groups can gradually adopt more complex options if desired.

**Ascending AC Innovation**:

Finch's adoption of ascending AC was initially controversial among OSR purists but became widely accepted for improving usability dramatically. Rather than "roll d20, add THAC0, compare to descending AC" or matrix lookups, combat becomes "roll d20, add attack bonus, compare to AC." Same mechanical outcomes, vastly simpler arithmetic.

**Compatibility**:

Swords & Wizardry maintains broad compatibility with classic D&D adventures. Monsters, spells, treasures, and challenges from old modules work with minimal conversion. This allows using decades of published adventures while playing streamlined modern rules.

**Commercial Success**:

The game spawned extensive third-party support. Publishers released adventures, settings, and supplements for Swords & Wizardry, creating ecosystem similar to classic D&D. Annual Swords & Wizardry Appreciation Day events built community and promoted the game.

Kickstarter campaigns for Swords & Wizardry products consistently succeeded, demonstrating sustained commercial viability. The game proved retroclones could support serious publishing businesses, not just hobbyist productions.

**Influence**:

Swords & Wizardry influenced numerous subsequent OSR games. Its streamlined approach, ascending AC adoption, and modular design philosophy appeared in many later retroclones and OSR-inspired games. It demonstrated that old-school gaming could be accessible without sacrificing its essential character.

### Quick Primer for Old School Gaming (2008)

The Primer represents Finch's most influential theoretical contribution, articulating OSR philosophy with clarity that defined the movement.

**Core Principles**:

The document identifies key differences between old-school and modern gaming:

**Rulings, Not Rules**: Referees adjudicate based on situation rather than searching for specific rules. Simple core mechanics allow focus on narrative and logic rather than mechanical complexity.

**Player Skill, Not Character Abilities**: Success depends on player ingenuity in describing actions and solving problems, not character builds or skill ranks. Clever approaches succeed regardless of whether specific mechanics cover them.

**Heroic, Not Superheroic**: Characters are capable but vulnerable. Death is possible and meaningful, creating genuine stakes rather than guaranteed success.

**Forget Game Balance**: Adventures need not be balanced for party level. Dangerous situations require caution, intelligence, and sometimes retreat. Players learn to assess threats and choose engagements wisely rather than assuming everything is level-appropriate.

**These principles transformed OSR from mechanical replication of old D&D into coherent design philosophy applicable beyond specific systems.

**Impact**:

The Primer became essential OSR reading, distributed widely and influencing countless designers and referees. It provided vocabulary for discussing what made old-school gaming distinctive, helping community articulate why they preferred certain approaches.

The document helped newcomers understand OSR philosophy, converting players accustomed to modern gaming to old-school approaches. It also gave referees framework for running old-school games effectively, explaining how to adjudicate fairly while maintaining player agency focus.

**Design Influence**:

The Primer influenced how OSR games were designed. Designers consciously incorporated its principles—providing simple core mechanics allowing referee judgment, emphasizing player problem-solving over character capabilities, accepting lethality and genuine danger, and avoiding balance assumptions that constrain adventure design.

### OSRIC (2006)

Co-created with Stuart Marshall, OSRIC established retrocloning's viability and provided framework for AD&D First Edition emulation.

**Legal Innovation**:

OSRIC demonstrated that OGL could support games recreating pre-3E D&D mechanics. This required careful legal analysis determining which elements constituted OGL-licensed game mechanics versus copyrighted expression. The project established templates for subsequent retroclones.

**AD&D Emulation**:

OSRIC recreated AD&D First Edition's mechanics comprehensively—classes, races, spells, magic items, combat procedures, treasure, and monsters. The game maintained AD&D's complexity while organizing information more clearly than Gygax's original presentation.

**Publishing Framework**:

Beyond providing AD&D rules, OSRIC created legal framework for publishers to release AD&D-compatible adventures and supplements. This opened classic D&D market to new publishers without licensing restrictions, enabling commercial OSR publishing.

**Community Impact**:

OSRIC proved that old-school gaming had sufficient audience for commercial products. It demonstrated that players wanted simpler alternatives to d20 System's complexity. And it showed that clear rules presentation could improve classic games' usability without changing underlying mechanics.

### Mythmere's Adventure Design Deskbook (2010)

The Deskbook provides practical guidance for designing old-school adventures, complementing the Primer's philosophical principles with concrete methodology.

**Systematic Approach**:

Finch presents structured process for adventure creation:
- Establishing core concept and theme
- Designing maps emphasizing exploration and multiple paths
- Stocking encounters balancing combat, puzzles, mysteries, and negotiation
- Creating meaningful choices with consequences
- Calibrating difficulty rewarding clever play over brute force
- Providing hooks and context for referee improvisation

**Dungeon Design**:

Extensive guidance on dungeon creation covering room layouts, encounter distribution, treasure placement, faction dynamics, environmental hazards, and creating atmosphere through description. This helps referees design dungeons that reward exploration and problem-solving.

**Wilderness Adventures**:

The book addresses wilderness adventure design—often neglected compared to dungeon design—covering hex-crawl structure, encounter design for open environments, creating points of interest, and managing exploration pacing.

**Practical Wisdom**:

Throughout, Finch provides insights from decades of refereeing—common pitfalls, how to handle unexpected player actions, balancing preparation with improvisation, and creating adventures supporting player agency.

## Impact and Legacy

Matt Finch's influence on the Old School Renaissance operates through both practical design work and theoretical articulation of OSR principles.

**OSR Philosophy**: The Quick Primer crystallized OSR thinking, providing vocabulary and framework for discussing old-school gaming. It transformed OSR from nostalgia into coherent design philosophy, influencing countless designers and referees.

**Retroclone Success**: OSRIC and Swords & Wizardry demonstrated retroclones' commercial viability, opening doors for OSR publishing explosion. Their success proved that old-school gaming could sustain serious commercial operations.

**Ascending AC Adoption**: Finch's advocacy for ascending AC helped OSR accept modernizations improving usability without compromising philosophy. This demonstrated that old-school gaming could evolve pragmatically.

**Modular Design**: Swords & Wizardry's multiple versions showed how games could serve diverse preferences within single framework. This influenced how designers think about providing options without forcing complexity.

**Accessibility Focus**: Finch's emphasis on making old-school gaming accessible to newcomers helped OSR grow beyond aging grognards to include new generations. His clear writing and streamlined designs welcomed players discovering old-school principles.

**Theoretical Foundations**: Beyond specific games, Finch's writings provided intellectual foundation for OSR as movement. His articulation of principles helped OSR become recognized approach to gaming rather than mere retrocomputing exercise.

**Community Building**: Finch's active community engagement, support for third-party publishers, and promotion of OSR events helped build cohesive community around old-school principles.
