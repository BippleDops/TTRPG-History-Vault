# Emily Care Boss

**Active Years**: 2002-present  
**Birth Year**: ~1970
**Nationality**: United States

Emily Care Boss stands as indie RPG's pioneering designer of romance mechanics and relationship-focused gaming, creating the Romance Trilogy (Breaking the Ice, Shooting the Moon, Under My Skin) demonstrating how games could explore courtship, attraction, and emotional connection through elegant mechanical frameworks rather than awkward roleplay, influencing how designers think about non-combat conflict, collaborative storytelling, and creating space for diverse relationship narratives beyond typical adventure gaming. Her work validates romance as legitimate compelling genre for mechanically sophisticated play.

## Early Career and The Forge

Boss emerged from The Forge community in early 2000s, pursuing design space largely unexplored: romantic relationships as primary gameplay focus. While most designers tackled heroism, horror, or adventure, Boss asked "what if the interesting conflicts were about attraction, courtship, and connection?"

Her design philosophy emerged from observations:
- Romance in traditional RPGs was often awkward roleplay without mechanical support
- Relationship conflicts could be as compelling as combat
- Collaborative rather than competitive play enabled different story types
- Games could explore emotional territory beyond violence
- Diverse relationship narratives deserved mechanical sophistication

## Major Works

### Breaking the Ice (2005)

Boss's breakthrough design, Breaking the Ice casts two players as potential romantic partners on first three dates, collaboratively creating characters, rolling dice to overcome attraction barriers, and determining whether romance succeeds through mechanical and narrative development.

**Revolutionary Elements**:

**Two-Player Romance**: Just two players required—no GM, perfect for couples or friends exploring romance narratives.

**Three Date Structure**: Each date is scene with specific mechanical framework:
- Date One: Initial attraction and awkwardness
- Date Two: Deepening connection and revealing complications  
- Date Three: Decision point—relationship continues or ends?

**Attraction Mechanics**: Characters have Traits (personality elements) and Attractions (what draws them to partner). Rolling uses these to overcome Conflicts (barriers to connection).

**Collaborative Opposition**: Players don't oppose each other—they collaboratively create interesting obstacles while rooting for romance to succeed.

**Bonus Dice from Real Conversation**: Players discussing their characters' situations earn bonus dice, mechanically rewarding actual emotional engagement.

**Endgame Resolution**: After three dates, dice pool sizes determine relationship outcome—success means ongoing romance, failure means parting ways.

**Impact**: Demonstrated romance could be mechanically interesting, not just awkward roleplay. Proved two-player games viable. Showed collaborative rather than competitive frameworks enabled different play styles.

**Reception**: Critical acclaim within indie community. Players report genuine emotional investment in fictional romance. Some found it therapeutic for exploring relationship dynamics. Couples used it as relationship-building exercise.

### Shooting the Moon (2008)

Second Romance Trilogy entry, Shooting the Moon features three players: two Suitors competing for Beloved's affection while Beloved player adjudicates romantic gestures and ultimately chooses (or neither).

**Three-Player Dynamics**: 
- Two Suitors pursuing Beloved
- Beloved responds to romantic gestures
- Creates romantic triangle drama

**Romantic Gesture Mechanics**: Suitors describe romantic actions, roll dice modified by previous gestures' success/failure.

**Beloved's Choice**: Throughout game, Beloved indicates which gestures succeed, building toward final decision.

**Competitive Romance**: Unlike Breaking the Ice's collaboration, Suitors compete while respecting Beloved's agency.

**Multiple Ending Types**: Beloved chooses Suitor A, Suitor B, neither, or both (polyamory option).

**Innovation**: Demonstrated romantic competition could create engaging gameplay. Gave Beloved agency matching their narrative position.

### Under My Skin (2012)

Third Romance Trilogy entry, Under My Skin explores established relationship under stress, with players portraying couple facing challenges threatening their connection.

**Established Relationship**: Characters are already together, fighting to preserve what they have.

**External Pressures**: Game introduces stressors (family disapproval, financial hardship, illness, distance) testing relationship.

**Resolution Mechanics**: Similar to Breaking the Ice but focused on overcoming relationship threats rather than initial attraction.

**Relationship Preservation**: Success means relationship survives challenges; failure means breaking up despite love.

**Emotional Depth**: Often most emotionally intense Romance Trilogy game due to high stakes (losing what you have vs. gaining something new).

**Impact**: Completed trilogy covering relationship arc—beginning (Breaking the Ice), competition (Shooting the Moon), preservation (Under My Skin).

## Design Philosophy

Boss's approach emphasizes:

**Romance as Legitimate Genre**: Treating romantic relationships as worthy of mechanical sophistication as combat or mystery

**Collaborative Not Competitive**: Many games work better through cooperation than opposition

**Emotional Engagement Through Mechanics**: Systems creating genuine emotional investment rather than relying solely on roleplay

**Diverse Relationship Narratives**: Making space for varied relationship types beyond heteronormative defaults

**Player Agency in Emotions**: Mechanics supporting emotional exploration without forcing specific feelings

**Accessibility**: Simple rules enabling focus on relationship dynamics

**Real Conversation Rewards**: Bonus dice for genuine emotional discussion between players

**Multiple Outcomes as Valid**: Relationships ending can be satisfying resolution, not failure

**Safety and Consent**: Thoughtful about potentially sensitive relationship content

**Inclusion**: Creating welcoming games for players traditionally marginalized in hobby
