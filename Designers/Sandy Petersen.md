# Sandy Petersen

**Active Years**: 1980-present
**Birth Year**: 1955

## Biography

Carl Sanford Joslyn "Sandy" Petersen (born September 16, 1955) is best known as the creator of Call of Cthulhu, the most successful horror RPG in history. Born in St. Louis, Missouri, Petersen discovered H.P. Lovecraft's works as a teenager and became fascinated with the Cthulhu Mythos. This early passion would later shape not only his most famous creation but influence how horror gaming evolved as a genre.

Petersen's journey into professional game design began in the late 1970s when he started contributing to RuneQuest at Chaosium. Working on RuneQuest provided valuable experience with Chaosium's Basic Roleplaying System, which would become the foundation for his own revolutionary design. However, Petersen recognized a significant gap in the gaming market: no RPG adequately captured the existential dread and cosmic horror that defined Lovecraft's fiction.

Traditional RPGs of the era focused on heroic characters growing stronger through experience, directly opposing Lovecraft's themes of human insignificance and the psychologically destructive nature of forbidden knowledge. Petersen set out to create something different—a game where investigation mattered more than combat, where learning the truth came at psychological cost, and where characters were ordinary people rather than heroes.

Call of Cthulhu launched in 1981 to immediate critical acclaim, winning the Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules that year. The game's Sanity mechanic—representing mental health as a degrading resource damaged by exposure to cosmic horrors—proved revolutionary. For the first time, an RPG mechanically represented the psychological costs of horror, not just physical danger.

Throughout the 1980s, Petersen served as lead developer on Call of Cthulhu, authoring numerous supplements, adventures, and sourcebooks that established the game's tone and expanded the Mythos for interactive play. His most famous adventure, Masks of Nyarlathotep (1984), became legendary as one of the greatest RPG campaigns ever written—an epic globe-spanning investigation combining research, exploration, and desperate confrontation with cultists serving the Crawling Chaos.

Petersen's Call of Cthulhu work balanced faithfulness to Lovecraft's vision with necessary adaptations for gaming. While Lovecraft's protagonists typically died or went insane, game characters needed some chance of survival to make play viable. Petersen threaded this needle by making survival possible but never guaranteed, maintaining horror's tension while enabling sustained campaigns.

In 1987, Petersen made a surprising career transition to video game design. Joining id Software, he designed levels for Doom (1993) and Doom II: Hell on Earth (1994), helping create two of the most influential first-person shooters ever made. His horror sensibility and understanding of pacing, atmosphere, and environmental design translated effectively to 3D spaces. Doom's hellish environments, oppressive atmosphere, and carefully calibrated difficulty reflected Petersen's tabletop design philosophy adapted to digital medium.

Petersen later worked at Ensemble Studios, contributing to Age of Empires (1997) and Age of Empires II (1999), demonstrating versatility beyond horror. These strategy games showcased his ability to design complex systems and balance competing game elements—skills developed through years of tabletop design.

Despite his video game career success, Petersen never fully left tabletop gaming. He consulted on various projects, continued design work sporadically, and participated in the gaming community. His dual expertise in tabletop and digital gaming provided unique perspective on game design across media.

In 2015, Petersen became actively involved in a Kickstarter campaign that restored Chaosium to its original leadership after years of financial difficulties. Returning to active involvement with Call of Cthulhu, he contributed to the game's seventh edition (2014), helping modernize the system while preserving its essential character.

Recent work includes Cthulhu Wars (2015), a massive board game funded through one of the most successful Kickstarter campaigns in board game history. The game combines strategic conquest gameplay with Lovecraftian horror, featuring enormous miniature figures of Great Old Ones. The success of Cthulhu Wars led to Petersen Games, his board game publishing company, and additional projects including Glorantha: The Gods War.

Now in his late sixties, Petersen continues contributing to tabletop gaming through design, consultation, and active participation in the gaming community. His influence extends across tabletop RPGs, video games, and board games—a testament to his design versatility and the lasting impact of his innovations.

## Design Philosophy

Petersen's design philosophy centers on creating horror experiences through game mechanics:

**Sanity as Resource**: Mental health should be a game statistic that degrades through exposure to horrors. This mechanically represents cosmic horror's psychologically destructive nature.

**Investigation Focus**: Horror games should emphasize discovery, research, and deduction over combat. Knowledge-seeking drives the narrative.

**Deadly Combat**: Fighting should be dangerous and best avoided. Characters are fragile investigators, not powerful heroes.

**Knowledge is Dangerous**: Learning the truth should come at cost. The more you understand about cosmic horror, the less sane you become.

**Ordinary Heroes**: Characters should be normal people thrust into extraordinary circumstances, grounding horror in relatability.

**Atmospheric Preparation**: Rules should support creating dread, tension, and horror atmosphere. Mechanics serve mood.

**Faithful Adaptation**: When adapting literary sources, respect the original while making necessary changes for interactivity.

**Player Agency with Consequences**: Players make meaningful choices, but those choices have severe, often tragic consequences.

## Major Works

### Call of Cthulhu (1981)

Call of Cthulhu represents Petersen's masterwork and one of the most influential RPG designs ever created. The game adapted H.P. Lovecraft's cosmic horror fiction to interactive format, creating the template for horror gaming that persists four decades later.

The game's central innovation was the Sanity system. Characters possess a Sanity statistic that decreases when witnessing horrific events, encountering unnatural creatures, or learning forbidden knowledge. Sanity loss can cause temporary madness, lasting phobias, or permanent insanity. This mechanic brilliantly captures Lovecraftian themes—the more you learn about cosmic horrors, the less capable you become of processing that knowledge without psychological damage.

Character creation emphasized ordinary people with mundane occupations: professors, journalists, private investigators, doctors, or even drifters and criminals. Each occupation provided skill templates reflecting professional training, grounding characters in recognizable reality. This contrasted sharply with fantasy RPGs where characters were warriors, wizards, or adventurers from the start.

Combat in Call of Cthulhu is deliberately dangerous. Characters have relatively few hit points, weapons deal substantial damage, and monsters often outclass investigators. Fighting is generally a poor choice—fleeing, hiding, or clever planning are usually wiser. This mechanical reality reinforces the game's themes about human vulnerability and the futility of violence against cosmic forces.

The investigation structure became Call of Cthulhu's hallmark gameplay pattern: characters discover hints of something wrong, research leads through libraries and archives, interview witnesses and experts, gradually uncover the horrifying truth, and must decide whether to confront the threat despite danger. This investigation-driven gameplay influenced countless subsequent games.

Call of Cthulhu won the Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules in 1981 and has remained continuously in print for over 40 years—an extraordinary achievement. The game's longevity demonstrates how well Petersen's design captures both Lovecraftian horror and compelling gameplay.

### Masks of Nyarlathotep (1984)

Masks of Nyarlathotep is widely considered one of the greatest RPG campaigns ever published. This epic adventure spans the globe and several years of game time, taking investigators from New York to London, Cairo, Kenya, Shanghai, and Australia as they unravel a worldwide conspiracy serving Nyarlathotep, the Crawling Chaos.

The campaign's structure allows groups to tackle locations in various orders, supporting player agency while maintaining narrative coherence. Each location presents distinct challenges, cultures, and horror themes, showcasing the Cthulhu Mythos's global reach. The research and investigation in one location provides clues pointing to others, creating satisfying interconnected mystery.

Masks features extensive handouts including letters, newspaper clippings, photographs, and diary entries that players can physically examine, enhancing immersion. The campaign balances investigation, social interaction, and deadly confrontations, with success requiring careful planning rather than straightforward combat.

The campaign has been reprinted multiple times, adapted for different Call of Cthulhu editions, and remains highly regarded by players who consider completing it a badge of honor. Its structure influenced how designers approach campaign-length adventures.

### Video Game Level Design

Petersen's Doom and Doom II level design work bridged tabletop and video game design in innovative ways. His understanding of pacing, spatial design, and atmosphere from tabletop gaming informed his digital level construction.

Doom levels designed by Petersen often featured:
- Oppressive, claustrophobic spaces building tension
- Careful monster placement creating surprise and challenge
- Environmental storytelling through level layout
- Pacing varying between action and exploration
- Difficulty curves testing player skill progressively

His most famous Doom II level, "Downtown," presented urban environment with vertical complexity and non-linear exploration. The level design principles he developed in tabletop gaming—multiple approaches, interconnected spaces, meaningful choices—translated effectively to 3D environments.

### Cthulhu Wars (2015)

Cthulhu Wars represents Petersen's return to Lovecraftian gaming through board game medium. This massive strategy game has players controlling different Great Old One factions competing for world domination, combining area control mechanics with asymmetric faction powers.

The game features enormous, beautifully sculpted miniatures of Great Old Ones including Cthulhu, Nyarlathotep, Shub-Niggurath, and others. The Kickstarter campaign raised over $1.4 million, one of the most successful board game campaigns at the time.

Gameplay involves recruiting cultists, summoning monsters, awakening Great Old Ones, and competing for control of Earth. Each faction plays distinctively, with unique abilities reflecting their mythological characteristics. The game balances competitive strategy with thematic implementation of Lovecraftian horror.

Cthulhu Wars' success launched Petersen Games as board game publisher and led to numerous expansions adding factions, maps, and gameplay variants.

## Innovations and Contributions

### Mechanical Innovations

**Sanity System**: The most influential horror game mechanic ever designed. Sanity as degrading resource mechanically represents cosmic horror's mental toll. Witnessing horrors causes sanity loss, leading to temporary or permanent mental illness. This innovation has been copied by virtually every subsequent horror RPG.

**Investigation Structure**: Created mechanical framework supporting clue-gathering, research, and deduction as primary activities. The game's structure encourages detective work over combat.

**Skill-Based Advancement**: Characters improve through use, with successful skill rolls enabling advancement. This organic development felt more natural than experience point accumulation.

**Occupation-Based Creation**: Character creation centered on mundane professions (professor, journalist, private investigator) providing skill templates and grounding characters in reality.

**Percentile Resolution**: While adopted from Basic Roleplaying, Petersen demonstrated percentile systems could support horror gaming, with intuitive probability assessment enhancing tension.

**Deadly Damage**: Designed damage and hit point systems making combat genuinely dangerous, mechanically reinforcing horror vulnerability themes.

### Genre Contributions

**Horror Gaming Legitimacy**: Proved horror could work as RPG genre, establishing that games didn't need power progression to engage players.

**Lovecraft Popularization**: Introduced millions to Lovecraft's Cthulhu Mythos, significantly expanding weird fiction's cultural reach.

**Literary Adaptation Model**: Demonstrated how to adapt literary properties faithfully while making necessary gaming compromises.

**Failure as Content**: Showed that character setbacks, madness, and death could create compelling play experiences.

## Influence on Later Designers

Petersen's sanity mechanic influenced virtually every horror game designer since 1981. Games from Trail of Cthulhu to Dread to Bluebeard's Bride incorporate sanity or mental health mechanics. His investigation-focused design influenced detective and mystery games across genres.

The deadly combat philosophy appears in OSR designs, horror games, and realistic systems prioritizing caution over heroic action.

His video game work influenced horror game atmosphere and level design, particularly in Doom's hellish environments and pacing.

## Quotes and Interviews

"The key to Call of Cthulhu is that the investigators are not heroes. They're ordinary people faced with the extraordinary. And that's terrifying."

"Lovecraft's horror is about confronting the incomprehensible. The sanity system mechanically represents that confrontation—you literally cannot fully understand cosmic horror without losing your mind."

"In a good horror game, combat means something has already gone wrong. You've failed to avoid the monster, or you're making a desperate last stand. Fighting should feel like desperation, not empowerment."

"The best Call of Cthulhu scenarios make players want to investigate even though they know investigation will destroy their characters. That tension between player curiosity and character survival creates real horror."

## Legacy

Sandy Petersen's creation fundamentally shaped horror gaming. Call of Cthulhu remains in print over 40 years after publication, an extraordinary achievement. The sanity mechanic appears in games across all platforms—tabletop, video games, board games.

His work demonstrated that RPGs could successfully explore genres beyond fantasy, encouraging industry diversification. The investigation-focused gameplay influenced mystery games, procedurals, and narrative-driven designs.

Petersen's dual careers in tabletop and video game design showcase the cross-pollination between gaming media, with his horror sensibility enhancing both forms.
