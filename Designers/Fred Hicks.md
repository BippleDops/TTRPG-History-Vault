# Fred Hicks

**Active Years**: 2001-present
**Birth Year**: 1971 (approximate)
**Nationality**: United States

Fred Hicks is co-founder of Evil Hat Productions, co-designer of the Fate roleplaying system, and one of indie RPG publishing's most influential figures. Through Fate's evolution from *Fudge* variant to comprehensive story gaming system, Hicks has championed accessible narrative mechanics, transparent game design, and ethical business practices. Evil Hat's success bridging indie and mainstream markets while maintaining community values demonstrates that principled publishing can be commercially sustainable. Hicks's influence extends beyond game design to publishing philosophy, industry advocacy, and community building.

## Early Life and Career

Fred Hicks came to game design through extensive roleplaying experience and involvement in online gaming communities during the internet's early explosion. Unlike designers formed in isolation, Hicks developed within connected networks of gamers discussing theory, sharing designs, and experimenting with narrative mechanics. This collaborative environment shaped his design philosophy and publishing approach.

Hicks's professional background in technology and project management informed his systematic approach to game development and publishing. He understood production workflows, team coordination, and sustainable business models—knowledge that would prove crucial to Evil Hat's success.

In the early 2000s, Hicks became involved with *Fudge*, Steffan O'Sullivan's free universal gaming system using descriptive dice (rated from Terrible to Superb rather than numerical). Fudge's flexibility and Open Game License availability made it ideal foundation for development. Hicks saw potential to evolve Fudge's framework into more focused system emphasizing narrative authority and collaborative storytelling.

## Major Works

### Evil Hat Productions Foundation (2001)

In 2001, Hicks co-founded Evil Hat Productions with Rob Donoghue to publish *Fate*, their Fudge-based fantasy RPG. The company name referenced Donoghue's tendency to "think evil thoughts" during game design sessions. This small press origin—two designers self-publishing through Print-on-Demand—represented typical indie RPG starting point.

Early Evil Hat operated on minimal budget, reinvesting revenue into better production values and new projects. This bootstrap approach, combined with community engagement and quality focus, gradually built reputation and audience. The company's growth from hobby project to professional publisher demonstrates indie gaming's maturation as industry segment.

Crucially, Hicks brought business acumen, project management, and long-term strategic thinking. While many indie publishers collapsed under financial or organizational pressure, Evil Hat developed sustainable practices enabling consistent output and longevity.

### Spirit of the Century (2006)

*Spirit of the Century* represented Fate's breakthrough success and Evil Hat's arrival as major publisher. The game combined pulp adventure setting (1920s "Century Club" of superhuman heroes fighting evil) with refined Fate mechanics emphasizing cinematic action and player narrative control.

**The Setting**: Drawing from pulp magazines, movie serials, and adventure literature, SotC presented optimistic, two-fisted adventure. Characters were larger-than-life heroes with superhuman abilities, facing villainous conspiracies and exotic threats. The tone emphasized fun, competence, and heroism rather than grim realism.

**Character Creation**: The game's revolutionary phase trio character creation connected player characters through shared backstory. Players created characters sequentially, with each character appearing in others' "novels" representing previous adventures. This ensured party cohesion and established relationships before play began, solving the "why are we together?" problem plaguing many campaigns.

Aspects—short phrases describing character elements—became central to Fate's mechanics. A character might have aspects like "Inventor of Fantastical Devices," "Enemy: The Gorilla Khan," or "I Never Leave a Man Behind." These aspects provided mechanical benefits when invoked (spending Fate points) and created complications when compelled (receiving Fate points).

**Mechanical Refinements**: SotC refined earlier Fate versions, creating elegant frameworks for skills, stunts, and conflicts. The stress track system modeled escalating tension without hit points' granular damage tracking. Consequences represented lasting injuries or complications characters took to avoid being taken out.

The game featured transparent, fiction-first resolution. Players declared actions, rolled Fudge dice (marked +, -, and blank), added skill ratings, and compared to opposition. Success degrees determined outcome quality. This simple core supported tactical depth through aspects, stunts, and fictional positioning.

**Success and Legacy**: *Spirit of the Century* won multiple ENnie Awards and achieved significant commercial success. More importantly, it introduced thousands of players to Fate and narrative gaming, demonstrating story-focused mechanics could support satisfying tactical play. The game influenced pulp adventure gaming and showed licensed-setting-quality production values were achievable for indie publishers.

### The Dresden Files RPG (2010)

Landing the Dresden Files license—Jim Butcher's bestselling urban fantasy series—represented major coup for Evil Hat. The project elevated Fate to licensed property adaptation while bringing mainstream fantasy audience to indie system.

Hicks, alongside Rob Donoghue and Leonard Balsera, adapted Fate to urban fantasy, creating city creation systems, magic mechanics, and supernatural templates reflecting Butcher's mythology. The game's innovations included:

**City Creation**: Collaborative worldbuilding where players designed their setting (or adapted existing city) through aspects representing locations, themes, faces (important NPCs), and places. This distributed narrative authority, making the setting everyone's creation rather than GM's exclusive domain.

**The Supernatural Ladder**: Scaling Fate to accommodate everything from mortal detectives to godlike entities required mechanical stretching. The game used expanded skill ladders and refresh (starting Fate points) to differentiate power levels while maintaining system coherence.

**Magic System**: Translating Butcher's detailed magic to game mechanics required balancing flexibility with thematic constraints. The evocation (fast, destructive magic) and thaumaturgy (slow, ritual magic) split reflected series distinctions while creating interesting tactical choices.

**Compels and Aspects**: Dresden Files leaned heavily on compels—GM (or players) offering Fate points to complicate situations through aspects. This mechanized the series' constant personal drama and moral complications, making Harry Dresden-style conflicted protagonists emerge naturally from mechanics.

The two-volume set won multiple awards and introduced mainstream fantasy readers to Fate. The game demonstrated indie systems could handle major licenses professionally, opening doors for future licensed projects.

### Fate Core (2013)

By 2013, Fate existed in multiple versions—Spirit of the Century, Dresden Files, Diaspora, Starblazer Adventures—each modifying core system differently. Hicks and team recognized need for unified, comprehensive Fate edition serving as foundation for all future projects.

*Fate Core* launched via Kickstarter, raising $433,365 (target: $3,000), becoming one of roleplaying's most successful crowdfunding campaigns. The overwhelming support demonstrated Fate's mainstream acceptance and Evil Hat's community goodwill.

**System Refinement**: Core consolidated and refined previous Fate iterations, creating definitive version. The four actions (Overcome, Create Advantage, Attack, Defend) unified resolution, replacing earlier versions' varied frameworks. Skills became customizable toolkit rather than fixed list, enabling genre-specific adaptation.

**Aspects and Fate Points**: The aspect economy received careful tuning. Fate points flowed through invocations (spending for bonuses), compels (accepting complications for points), and concessions (surrendering conflicts for narrative control and points). This economy created dynamic resource management where accepting complications funded future successes.

**Bronze Rule**: "In Fate, you can treat anything like a character." This design principle enabled characters, organizations, vehicles, cities, or abstract concepts to use same mechanical framework. A detective agency, a spaceship, or a brewing war could have aspects, skills, and stress tracks, creating fractal design elegance.

**Modes and Toolkits**: Rather than one-size-fits-all rules, Core provided modular options. The back half offered alternative subsystems, optional rules, and adaptation guidance, explicitly teaching game design principles. This made Fate both complete game and game design toolkit.

**Open Licensing**: Fate Core released under Open Game License and Creative Commons, encouraging derivative works and community development. This openness spawned Fate Accelerated, Fate Condensed, and hundreds of community hacks and settings.

**Impact**: Fate Core became definitive narrative system, competing with Powered by the Apocalypse for story gaming dominance. The Kickstarter's success demonstrated crowdfunding could fund major RPG development. The comprehensive rules and extensive guidance made Fate accessible to broader audience while serving experienced players.

### Fate Accelerated (2013)

Alongside Core, Evil Hat released *Fate Accelerated Edition* (FAE)—streamlined Fate focusing on approaches (Careful, Clever, Flashy, Forceful, Quick, Sneaky) rather than skills. This simplified version reduced barrier to entry while maintaining Fate's core aspects and narrative focus.

FAE's 50-page rulebook contrasted with Core's 300+ pages, demonstrating system's scalability. The approaches framework inspired numerous hacks and introduced players intimidated by extensive skill lists. FAE proved particularly successful introducing younger players and newcomers to roleplaying.

### Other Notable Works

**Don't Rest Your Head** (2006): Hicks's solo design, a surreal horror game about insomniacs with reality-warping powers fighting nightmarish Nightmares in the Mad City. The game used competing dice pools (discipline, exhaustion, madness) creating escalating tension and risk. Its dark psychological horror contrasted with Fate's heroic adventure, showing Hicks's range.

**Do: Pilgrims of the Flying Temple** (2011): Family-friendly storytelling game using letter-answering structure, designed with Daniel Solis. Players portray young monks helping troubled worlds by solving problems (often creating new ones). The accessible, gentle design reached younger audiences and demonstrated Evil Hat's diversity.

**Range of licenses**: Evil Hat published numerous licensed Fate settings: *Atomic Robo*, *The Venture City*, *Uprising*, demonstrating system's flexibility.

## Design Philosophy

Hicks's design philosophy emphasizes accessibility, transparency, and player empowerment:

**Fiction First, Always**: Mechanics emerge from and serve fiction. Players describe actions fictionally; mechanics adjudicate based on narrative context. Rules don't dictate fiction—they support it.

**Transparent Mechanics**: Players should understand how mechanics work and why. No hidden math or opaque subsystems. Clear cause-effect relationships enable informed decision-making.

**Player Narrative Authority**: Players contribute to fiction beyond their characters. Aspects, declarations, and concessions distribute storytelling authority, making games collaborative rather than GM-dominated.

**Aspects as Universal Language**: Descriptive phrases (aspects) model characters, situations, objects, and conflicts uniformly. This creates consistent framework while remaining flexible and fiction-forward.

**Failure as Opportunity**: Failure shouldn't mean nothing happens. Failed rolls create complications, costs, or partial successes moving fiction forward. This maintains pacing and stakes.

**Resource Economy Creates Drama**: Fate point flow creates tactical depth and pacing rhythm. Accepting compels funds future heroics; running low forces accepting consequences. Economy models dramatic arc mechanically.

**Modularity and Customization**: Provide frameworks and toolkits, not rigid prescriptions. Games should adapt to table preferences through conscious mechanical choices.

**Accessibility Through Clarity**: Game texts should be readable, well-organized, and beautiful. Clear writing, helpful examples, and strong visual design lower barriers to entry.

**Ethical Publishing**: Fair payment to creators, inclusive representation, respect for community, sustainable business practices. Publishing should be ethical, not purely profit-driven.

**Open Development and Licensing**: Share rules, encourage hacks, support community creation. Gaming thrives through sharing and iteration, not proprietary hoarding.

## Innovations and Contributions

### Mechanical Innovations

**Aspect-Based Resolution**: Short descriptive phrases mechanically representing story elements, creating unified framework applicable across all game elements. This influenced countless narrative games.

**Fate Point Economy**: Dynamic resource flow where accepting complications funds future success, creating mechanical incentive for dramatic adversity and pacing rhythm.

**Phase Trio Character Creation**: Collaborative backstory generation ensuring character connections and shared investment before play begins.

**Four Actions Framework**: Standardizing resolution through Overcome, Create Advantage, Attack, and Defend unified previously fragmented subsystems.

**Bronze Rule**: Treating anything as character (with aspects, skills, stress) created fractal design elegance enabling consistent mechanical handling of diverse elements.

**Success at Cost**: Formalizing partial success and failure with complications, ensuring failed rolls advance fiction rather than stalling it.

**Stress and Consequences**: Abstract damage modeling where characters take consequences (lasting complications) to remain in conflicts, creating dramatic injury without hit point accounting.

### Publishing Innovations

**Ethical Indie Publishing**: Evil Hat pioneered fair creator payment, transparent business practices, and sustainable indie publishing, proving ethical approaches could be profitable.

**Pay What You Want Models**: Evil Hat experimented with PWYW pricing, accessibility initiatives, and community-supported development.

**Kickstarter Best Practices**: Evil Hat's successful Kickstarters established standards for realistic budgeting, clear communication, and fulfillment practices, raising crowdfunding's professional standards.

**Open Licensing Leadership**: Aggressive use of Creative Commons and OGL to encourage derivative works and community development.

**Diverse Representation**: Conscious inclusion in art, writing, and setting development, addressing RPG industry's historical homogeneity.

**Fate Patreon and Ongoing Support**: Sustained product development through varied revenue streams, including subscription models.

### Industry Impact

**Bridging Indie and Mainstream**: Evil Hat successfully operates in both indie and mainstream markets, distributing through hobby channels while maintaining indie ethos.

**Licensed Property Success**: Demonstrating indie publishers could professionally handle major licenses opened doors industry-wide.

**Community Publishing Platform**: The Fate Core license enabled community publishing programs, where creators sell Fate-compatible products through Evil Hat's support.

**Professional Standards**: Raising expectations for production values, layout, art, and editing across indie publishing.

**Industry Advocacy**: Hicks's public discussion of business practices, ethical issues, and industry problems contributed to broader conversations about gaming culture and commerce.

## Legacy and Influence

Fred Hicks's influence extends across design, publishing, and community:

**Fate Ecosystem**: Hundreds of Fate-powered games explore diverse genres and settings. The system rivals Powered by the Apocalypse as narrative gaming's dominant framework. Fate Condensed (2020) continues refining the system for new audiences.

**Aspect Adoption**: Aspect-based mechanics appear in games across design traditions, from D&D 5E's inspiration/bonds/flaws to indie narrative games. The concept of story-first mechanical tags became widespread.

**Publishing Model**: Evil Hat's ethical practices, open licensing, and sustainable business model influenced indie publishers industry-wide. The company demonstrates values-driven publishing can succeed commercially.

**Kickstarter Impact**: Fate Core's success inspired RPG crowdfunding boom, showing established games could raise significant funding for new editions.

**Educational Resource**: Fate Core's extensive guidance on game design, adaptation, and GMing makes it valuable educational text studied by aspiring designers.

**Industry Leadership**: Hicks's advocacy on creator rights, ethical publishing, and industry issues positions him as thought leader beyond game design.

**Mainstream Accessibility**: Fate brought narrative gaming to broader audiences, making story game concepts accessible to traditional RPG players.

Contemporary designers citing Fate's influence include numerous indie creators, mainstream designers incorporating aspects or player narrative authority, and the entire Fate community creating derivative works. The system's ubiquity in indie publishing demonstrates lasting impact.

## Collaborations

**Rob Donoghue**: Evil Hat co-founder and Fate co-designer. Their partnership balances Hicks's business and development management with Donoghue's design innovation.

**Leonard Balsera**: Fate Core lead designer, developed four actions framework and numerous mechanical refinements. Balsera's design acumen elevated Fate to new sophistication.

**Amanda Valentine**: Evil Hat's other co-founder, handling operations and community engagement. Valentine's work enables company's smooth functioning.

**Sean Nittner, Mark Diaz Truman, Sophie Lagacé**: Frequent collaborators on various Evil Hat projects, contributing design, development, and writing.

**Licensed Property Authors**: Working with Jim Butcher (Dresden Files), Brian Clevinger (Atomic Robo), and other license holders, balancing creator vision with game design needs.

Evil Hat operates collaboratively, with Hicks as managing partner rather than sole auteur. This team approach produces stronger products and sustainable company culture.

## Quotes and Interviews

"Fate is about telling stories where characters' aspects—their beliefs, relationships, and traits—matter mechanically. Your 'Trouble' aspect isn't just flavor text; it actively creates complications that drive story forward."

"The best thing about Fate points is they make 'bad things happen to my character' into something you want. Compels give you resources to be awesome later, so you're incentivized to accept dramatic complications."

"Fiction first means you describe what your character does, then figure out which mechanics apply. You don't say 'I use my Rapport skill'—you say 'I convince the guard to let us pass' and we determine that's Rapport."

On Evil Hat's ethics: "We pay our creators fairly, represent diverse voices, and operate transparently. It's more work, but it's the right way to do business. And it's sustainable—our community supports us because they trust us."

"Open licensing Fate was scary financially, but it's been incredibly rewarding. Seeing hundreds of games using Fate, knowing we enabled that creativity, means more than maximizing profit."

"The Dresden Files RPG taught us that indie companies can handle major licenses if they do the work professionally and respect the source material. That opened doors across the industry."

On game design: "Every mechanic should answer 'Why is this here?' If it doesn't serve the game's goals or create interesting decisions, cut it."
