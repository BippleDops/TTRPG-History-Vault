# Flying Buffalo

**Founded**: 1970
**Active Era**: Golden Age through Modern Era (1970-Present)
**Historical Significance**: ⭐ 5/5
**Headquarters**: Scottsdale, Arizona
**Status**: Active

## Company History

Flying Buffalo holds distinction as gaming's oldest continuously operating independent game publisher, predating even TSR. Founded in 1970 by Rick Loomis and Steve MacGregor, the company pioneered play-by-mail gaming, created the second commercially published fantasy roleplaying game (Tunnels & Trolls), developed innovative solo adventures, and maintained independent operations through five decades of industry upheaval. The company's journey from college startup through gaming industry's transformation demonstrates remarkable perseverance, innovation, and commitment to accessible gaming.

Rick Loomis founded Flying Buffalo while attending University of Arizona, initially creating play-by-mail (PBM) games as college project. The company name originated from Loomis's appreciation for the ridiculous—"Flying Buffalo" being deliberately absurd and memorable. This playful irreverence characterized much of the company's future output.

The play-by-mail concept proved innovative for early 1970s. Players submitted turn orders by mail; Loomis processed them manually, calculating results and mailing responses. These moderated games required no real-time coordination, enabling people across distances to compete. Games like Nuclear Destruction (later Nuclear War) created strategic challenges resolved through postal system.

The manual processing proved laboriously time-consuming. Loomis recognized computers could automate calculation, dramatically increasing capacity. Securing computer access (expensive and rare in early 1970s), he programmed games processing turns automatically. This computerized play-by-mail became Flying Buffalo's signature innovation.

Games like Starweb, Rimworld, and others built devoted followings. These multi-player strategic games involved empire building, diplomacy, warfare, and economic competition. The asynchronous play suited adults with limited gaming time, creating market distinct from face-to-face gaming. Flying Buffalo became PBM industry leader, eventually processing thousands of turns weekly.

The PBM business provided steady revenue funding other publishing ventures. While not making anyone wealthy, the subscription-based income enabled Flying Buffalo's survival when other game companies collapsed. This diversified revenue stream proved essential for long-term viability.

Ken St. Andre's Tunnels & Trolls (1975) became Flying Buffalo's most significant RPG publication. St. Andre, a librarian and D&D player frustrated by original game's complexity and cost, designed simpler fantasy RPG playable without extensive rules mastery or expensive polyhedral dice—using only d6s (six-sided dice, readily available everywhere).

The game simplified D&D's concepts: straightforward character creation, streamlined combat resolution, accessible magic system, and focus on playability over simulation. Rather than competing directly with D&D, Tunnels & Trolls served audiences intimidated by original game's complexity or lacking access to expensive components.

Flying Buffalo published Tunnels & Trolls beginning in 1975, making it the second commercial fantasy RPG after D&D. The game achieved moderate commercial success—never threatening D&D's dominance but building devoted following and remaining continuously available across nearly five decades.

The solo adventures became particularly important innovation. Traditional RPGs required groups; solo adventures enabled individual play through programmed branching narratives. Players read scenarios, made choices, and turned to indicated paragraphs based on decisions and dice rolls. This created accessible RPG experiences for people lacking gaming groups.

The Buffalo Castle (1976) by Rick Loomis became first commercially published solo adventure. The dungeon-crawl scenario walked players through branching narrative with combat, exploration, and puzzle-solving. The success spawned extensive Tunnels & Trolls solo adventure line—eventually over 30 modules exploring diverse scenarios and difficulties.

The solo adventures served multiple purposes: introduced new players to RPG concepts individually before requiring group coordination, provided entertainment for players lacking groups, and generated steady supplement sales. The innovation influenced subsequent solo gamebook publishing—Fighting Fantasy, Lone Wolf, and others followed Flying Buffalo's pioneering efforts.

The Catalyst series represented ambitious PBM superhero game. Players designed superheroes submitted to Flying Buffalo; moderators adjudicated battles, adventures, and interactions. The game combined PBM's strategic elements with RPG character development and comic book sensibilities. While never achieving Starweb's scale, Catalyst demonstrated Flying Buffalo's willingness to experiment beyond proven formulas.

Citybook series (1982-1993) provided universal fantasy city resources usable with any RPG system. Each book detailed city locations with maps, NPCs, adventure hooks, and atmospheric descriptions—functioning as plug-and-play resources for GMs needing urban settings. The system-neutral approach maximized market while demonstrating Flying Buffalo's service to broader gaming community beyond just proprietary products.

Grimtooth's Traps series (1981) by Paul O'Connor became beloved sourcebook line. These illustrated trap compilations presented deadly, clever, and often humorous dungeon hazards. Grimtooth the Troll narrated with sadistic glee, creating distinctive voice. The books served practical GM resources while entertaining through absurdity and creativity. Multiple volumes explored different trap categories—magical traps, technological traps, environmental hazards—creating comprehensive resource.

Nuclear War card game (originally Nuclear Destruction, 1965) predated Flying Buffalo but became company signature. The darkly humorous game had players launching nuclear weapons eliminating each other's populations. The combination of serious subject matter with absurdist humor created memorable gameplay. The game spawned multiple expansions and variants, achieving cult classic status and remaining in print for decades.

Throughout 1980s and 1990s, Flying Buffalo maintained steady operations through diversified products: PBM games provided subscription revenue, Tunnels & Trolls generated core sales and supplements, solo adventures created ongoing releases, card games like Nuclear War achieved cult following, and universal resources like Citybooks served broader market.

The company avoided chasing trends or attempting explosive growth. While other publishers bet heavily on particular games or product lines, Flying Buffalo maintained conservative approach—modest releases, sustainable operations, and focus on proven successes. This unglamorous strategy enabled survival through industry downturns killing more ambitious competitors.

Rick Loomis's death in 2019 created existential crisis. Loomis had led company for nearly 50 years, and his loss raised questions about Flying Buffalo's future. However, the company continued operations under new management, demonstrating institutional strength beyond single leader.

The transition involved Ken St. Andre and others stepping into management roles, maintaining operations while honoring Loomis's legacy. New Tunnels & Trolls editions, reissues of classic materials, and continued product support demonstrated commitment to continuity.

The COVID-19 pandemic challenged small publisher dependent on conventions and physical sales. However, Flying Buffalo's digital presence, print-on-demand capabilities, and devoted fanbase enabled adaptation. The company's long history of survival through challenges proved assets during unprecedented disruption.

Today, Flying Buffalo operates as living historical artifact—oldest continuous independent game publisher still releasing products. While never achieving TSR's dominance or Evil Hat's modern success, the company's five-decade persistence demonstrates remarkable achievement. The catalog spanning PBM games, RPGs, card games, and supplements represents substantial contribution to gaming history.

## Business Model and Philosophy

Flying Buffalo developed distinctive approach enabling unprecedented longevity:

**Diversified Revenue Streams**: Rather than depending on single product, Flying Buffalo maintained multiple income sources—PBM game subscriptions, RPG sales, card games, universal resources, and solo adventures. This diversification reduced vulnerability to any single product's failure.

**Play-by-Mail Foundation**: The PBM games provided steady subscription revenue less vulnerable to market fluctuations than retail sales. This reliable income funded other ventures and enabled survival through industry downturns.

**Conservative Operations**: Flying Buffalo avoided overextension, maintained modest overhead, and operated within means. The unglamorous but sustainable approach prevented spectacular failures that destroyed more ambitious competitors.

**Accessible Design Philosophy**: Tunnels & Trolls, solo adventures, and other products emphasized accessibility over complexity. This served audiences intimidated by or lacking access to more complex games.

**Long-Term Product Support**: Rather than abandoning products for new releases, Flying Buffalo maintained long-term support—Tunnels & Trolls remained available across nearly five decades, solo adventures accumulated extensive catalog, and classic products stayed in print.

**System-Neutral Resources**: Citybooks and similar products served broad market by avoiding system specificity. This maximized potential audience while contributing to gaming community beyond proprietary interests.

**Independent Operations**: Maintaining independence from corporate ownership or major financing preserved creative control and operational freedom but limited resources available for ambitious projects.

**Niche Market Service**: Rather than competing directly with major publishers, Flying Buffalo served specific niches—PBM gamers, solo adventurers, accessibility-focused players—building devoted followings in underserved markets.

**Humor and Personality**: Products like Grimtooth's Traps and Nuclear War embraced humor and personality distinguishing them from serious competitors. This created memorable brand identity and emotional connection with audiences.

**Adaptation and Survival**: The company repeatedly adapted to changing circumstances—computerizing PBM, embracing solo adventures, adding new product lines, transitioning leadership—demonstrating resilience enabling unprecedented longevity.

**Community Connection**: Despite small size, Flying Buffalo maintained strong community relationships through convention presence, responsive customer service, and genuine engagement with fans.

This multifaceted approach created sustainable business model enabling five-decade operations—unique achievement in volatile gaming industry.

## Major Releases

### Tunnels & Trolls (1975)

Tunnels & Trolls, designed by Ken St. Andre, became the second commercially published fantasy RPG, following D&D by roughly one year. St. Andre created the game from frustration with original D&D's complexity, expense, and rules ambiguity. His alternative emphasized accessibility, simplicity, and playability.

The game's accessibility proved defining characteristic. Character creation involved rolling stats, choosing kindred (species—humans, elves, dwarves, hobbits, etc.), selecting class, and determining equipment—taking minutes rather than hours. This streamlined approach enabled quick starts without extensive rules study.

The combat system dramatically simplified D&D's approach. Rather than individual initiative and separate attack/damage rolls, T&T used saving rolls and combat adds. All combatants on each side combined combat values, rolled dice, compared totals, and applied difference as damage to losing side. This resolved entire combat rounds quickly without tracking individual actions.

The saving rolls handled non-combat challenges. Characters rolled dice based on relevant stat, attempting to exceed difficulty. Higher stats improved success chances through higher die rolls. This unified resolution system simplified gameplay while maintaining meaningful stat differentiation.

The magic system streamlined spellcasting. Wizards and specialists learned spells costing Strength to cast rather than using Vancian memorization. This resource management approach allowed casters to repeatedly use known spells while preventing unlimited spellcasting. The system proved more intuitive for players unfamiliar with D&D's memorization mechanics.

The kindreds system provided character variety beyond humans. Elves, dwarves, hobbits, fairies, leprechauns, and various species offered mechanical differentiation and roleplaying variety. The system avoided race-as-class restrictions, enabling dwarf wizards or elf rogues freely.

The solo adventure innovation became T&T's signature contribution. The programmed paragraph format enabled individual play through branching narratives. Players read scenarios, made choices, fought enemies, and turned to indicated paragraphs creating personalized adventures. This accessibility served players lacking groups while introducing RPG concepts individually.

The d6-only dice requirement reduced barrier to entry. While D&D required expensive polyhedral dice (especially hard to acquire in 1970s), T&T used standard six-sided dice available everywhere. This practical consideration substantially reduced entry costs for potential players.

The lighter tone distinguished T&T from D&D's sometimes ponderous seriousness. The rules embraced humor, the game encouraged creative problem-solving over tactical optimization, and the overall atmosphere proved more welcoming to casual players. This tonal difference appealed to audiences finding D&D intimidating or overly serious.

Multiple editions refined original concepts. The 5th Edition (1979) became definitive version for decades. Subsequent editions—7th, 7.5, 8th, and deluxe editions—updated rules while maintaining core accessibility. These revisions demonstrated ongoing development rather than abandonment after initial release.

The solo adventure line grew extensive—eventually over 30 modules exploring diverse scenarios. Buffalo Castle, Arena of Khazan, City of Terrors, and others provided ready-to-play content for solo gamers. This comprehensive support created self-contained gaming experience without requiring group coordination.

The game never challenged D&D commercially but built devoted following spanning nearly five decades. T&T served as introduction to RPGs for players intimidated by D&D, provided alternative for those preferring streamlined systems, and created accessible solo gaming before Fighting Fantasy popularized gamebooks.

The cultural significance extends beyond sales. T&T demonstrated that alternative approaches to fantasy gaming could succeed commercially, that simplicity needn't mean lack of depth, and that accessible design served genuine market need. The game influenced subsequent accessible RPG design while proving independent publishers could create lasting properties.

### Grimtooth's Traps Series (1981)

Grimtooth's Traps, written and illustrated by Paul O'Connor, became beloved sourcebook series presenting deadly dungeon hazards with sadistic humor. Grimtooth the Troll—evil dungeon designer delighting in adventurer suffering—narrated books with gleeful malice creating distinctive personality.

The traps themselves ranged from clever to absurd. Practical hazards like pit traps with innovative triggers sat alongside ridiculous deathtraps involving elaborate Rube Goldberg mechanisms. The variety served both functional GM resources and entertainment through creativity and humor.

The illustrations proved essential to presentation. O'Connor's detailed cross-section diagrams showed trap mechanisms, trigger points, and consequences. These visual explanations made complex traps comprehensible while adding dark humor through depicted adventurer suffering.

The sadistic humor created memorable tone. Grimtooth's commentary celebrated adventurer deaths, offered tips for maximizing suffering, and reveled in creative cruelty. This villainous perspective inverted typical heroic gaming narratives, creating entertainment through perspective shift.

The practical utility served GMs seeking trap ideas. Rather than requiring original invention for every dungeon hazard, GMs could consult Grimtooth for tested concepts. The system-neutral presentation worked across fantasy RPGs, maximizing utility beyond just Flying Buffalo's games.

Multiple volumes explored different trap categories. Grimtooth's Traps (1981) presented general hazards. Grimtooth's Traps Fore (1985) continued with more mechanical mayhem. Grimtooth's Traps Bazaar (1986) featured merchant and urban traps. Grimtooth's Dungeon of Doom (1989) presented complete trapped dungeon. This specialization created comprehensive trap resource.

The series achieved cult classic status, fondly remembered by generation of gamers. References to Grimtooth became shorthand for sadistic GM tendencies, and the character entered gaming folklore alongside Tomb of Horrors and similar notorious challenges.

The books demonstrated Flying Buffalo's personality-driven approach. Rather than dry mechanical resources, Grimtooth created memorable character and humorous reading experience. This entertainment value beyond pure utility distinguished Flying Buffalo products from utilitarian alternatives.

### Play-by-Mail Games (1970s-2000s)

Flying Buffalo's play-by-mail games pioneered remote asynchronous gaming decades before internet enabled online play. These moderated strategic games created unique gaming experiences impossible in face-to-face settings.

Starweb became signature PBM game. Players controlled empires expanding through space, managing resources, conducting diplomacy, waging war, and competing for dominance. The simultaneous turn processing meant all players submitted orders processed together, creating dynamic evolving situations.

The diplomacy element distinguished Starweb from pure strategy games. Players could negotiate alliances, betray partners, deceive enemies, and conduct complex political maneuvering. The postal communication enabled elaborate diplomatic exchanges impossible in real-time gaming.

The turn processing involved sophisticated computer systems (advanced for 1970s-1980s). Players submitted order sheets; computers processed moves calculating combat results, economic changes, exploration discoveries, and diplomatic consequences. Flying Buffalo mailed results back to players who then submitted next turns. This cycle continued over months or years creating epic campaigns.

Rimworld presented post-apocalyptic empire building. Players developed territories, managed resources, researched technology, and competed for dominance in devastated world. The thematic variation demonstrated PBM versatility beyond just space opera.

Other PBM games explored different genres and mechanics. Heroic Fantasy offered RPG-style character development through postal turns. Mad Scientist pitted inventors against each other in absurdist competition. Nuclear Destruction brought Nuclear War to PBM format. This diversity prevented pigeonholing while serving varied interests.

The asynchronous play suited adults with limited gaming time. Submitting turns weekly or monthly fit busy schedules better than coordinating regular gaming sessions. This accessibility expanded gaming audiences beyond students and hobbyists to working professionals.

The social connections created community beyond gameplay. Players developed correspondence relationships, formed lasting friendships, and built communities around games. Some players maintained PBM relationships for decades, creating social networks predating online communities.

The subscription model provided steady revenue. Players paid per turn or through ongoing subscriptions, creating reliable income stream less vulnerable to market fluctuations than retail sales. This financial stability funded Flying Buffalo's broader operations.

The computer infrastructure required significant investment but enabled scalability impossible with manual processing. The same systems could process thousands of turns with effort only marginally greater than hundreds. This created economy of scale supporting business model.

The PBM industry's decline with internet's rise challenged Flying Buffalo. Online gaming offered real-time interaction without postal delays, superior graphics, and social features PBM couldn't match. However, devoted PBM community maintained support for years, with some games continuing into 2000s.

The historical significance extends beyond commercial success. Flying Buffalo pioneered remote gaming, demonstrated asynchronous play's viability, created computer-moderated games before internet existed, and built communities spanning continents through postal system. These innovations influenced subsequent online gaming development.

### Solo Adventures (1976-present)

Flying Buffalo's solo adventures, beginning with Buffalo Castle (1976), pioneered commercially published programmed solo RPG experiences. These choose-your-own-adventure-style modules enabled individual play before Fighting Fantasy popularized gamebooks.

Buffalo Castle by Rick Loomis established format. Players created Tunnels & Trolls characters, read scenario introductions, made choices, and turned to indicated paragraphs. Combat used T&T rules, creating mechanical gameplay rather than just branching narrative. The dungeon-crawl scenario taught basic concepts while providing complete adventure.

The mechanical integration distinguished T&T solo adventures from later gamebooks. Rather than simplified combat abstractions, players used full T&T rules—creating characters, managing equipment, gaining experience, and advancing levels. This made solo adventures legitimate RPG experiences rather than just interactive fiction.

The branching structures created replayability. Different choices led to different encounters, discoveries, and outcomes. Players could attempt adventures multiple times exploring alternatives, creating value beyond single playthrough.

The difficulty range served different player experience levels. Early solos like Buffalo Castle suited beginners, while advanced solos like Naked Doom challenged experienced players. This progression enabled skill development through solo play before group participation.

The diverse scenarios prevented monotony. Dungeon crawls, wilderness adventures, urban investigations, and exotic locations provided variety. This thematic diversity maintained interest across extensive catalog.

The mechanical innovations enhanced gameplay. Some solos featured special rules, unique magic items, or scenario-specific mechanics. These innovations kept experiences fresh while demonstrating RPG possibility space.

The educational purpose served new players. Solos taught rules through practical application, building confidence before group play. This lowered barrier to RPG entry by removing social performance anxiety from initial learning.

The commercial success spawned extensive line—eventually over 30 solo adventures for Tunnels & Trolls plus solos for other systems. This comprehensive catalog created substantial product line requiring minimal inventory investment compared to physical components.

The historical significance established programmed solo adventure format later popularized by Fighting Fantasy. Flying Buffalo pioneered mechanical integration distinguishing RPG solo adventures from simple branching narratives.

## Impact on Industry

Flying Buffalo's influence on gaming proved substantial despite modest commercial scale:

**Second Fantasy RPG**: Tunnels & Trolls demonstrated D&D alternatives could succeed commercially, establishing precedent for diverse fantasy RPG market.

**Play-by-Mail Pioneer**: Flying Buffalo created and led PBM gaming industry, pioneering remote asynchronous gaming decades before internet.

**Computer-Moderated Gaming**: Early adoption of computer processing for game moderation anticipated online gaming's eventual dominance.

**Solo Adventure Innovation**: Pioneering programmed solo adventures established format later popularized by Fighting Fantasy and other gamebooks.

**Accessible Design Philosophy**: T&T's emphasis on accessibility, simplified rules, and low barriers to entry influenced subsequent accessible RPG design.

**Independent Publisher Model**: Five decades of continuous independent operations demonstrated viability of focused, conservatively managed small publisher.

**System-Neutral Resources**: Citybooks and similar products established model for universal gaming resources serving broad market.

**Diversified Business Model**: Combining PBM subscriptions, RPG sales, card games, and supplements created template for sustainable multi-stream revenue.

**Humor in Gaming**: Grimtooth's Traps, Nuclear War, and other products demonstrated humor's commercial and cultural viability in gaming products.

**Longevity Through Adaptation**: Successfully navigating five decades of industry transformation demonstrated resilience and adaptation capabilities.

## Current Status and Future

Flying Buffalo continues operating as independent publisher:

**Post-Loomis Operations**: Successfully transitioning leadership after founder's death demonstrated institutional strength beyond single leader.

**Financial Stability**: Modest but sustainable operations maintain viability through conservative management and devoted customer base.

**Product Availability**: Tunnels & Trolls, Nuclear War, and other signature products remain available through print-on-demand and traditional distribution.

**Digital Adaptation**: PDF sales and online distribution supplement traditional retail channels.

**Community Support**: Devoted fanbase maintains enthusiasm for classic products and supports continued operations.

**Historical Significance**: Recognition as gaming's oldest continuous independent publisher creates cultural capital and market interest.

**Challenges**: Limited resources, changing market conditions, and competition from larger publishers present ongoing challenges.

**Future Prospects**: Continued operation likely through focused catalog, print-on-demand efficiency, and devoted niche audience, though major growth unlikely given market position and resource limitations.
