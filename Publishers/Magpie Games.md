# Magpie Games

**Founded**: 2011
**Active Era**: Modern Era (2011-Present)
**Historical Significance**: ⭐ 4/5
**Headquarters**: St. Louis, Missouri
**Status**: Active

## Company History

Magpie Games emerged as transformative force in independent roleplaying game publishing, demonstrating that queer-positive, inclusive design combined with professional production values could achieve mainstream commercial success. Founded by Mark Diaz Truman and Marissa Kelly, Magpie built reputation for Powered by the Apocalypse games featuring diverse representation, thoughtful mechanics, and premium presentation. The company's journey from publishing small experimental games to producing major licensed properties like Root while maintaining commitment to progressive values and indie design sensibilities represents remarkable achievement in modern gaming landscape.

Mark Diaz Truman and Marissa Kelly met through indie gaming communities during story games movement's expansion. Both shared frustration with traditional gaming's representation limitations and mechanical conservatism. They envisioned publisher championing innovative design, diverse creators, and explicit inclusion while maintaining professional quality matching major publishers. This vision—progressive values combined with commercial viability—distinguished Magpie from inception.

The company name "Magpie" referenced the bird's reputation for collecting shiny objects—appropriate metaphor for publisher gathering and showcasing brilliant designers and innovative games. From beginning, Magpie positioned itself as curator of indie talent rather than just proprietary product factory.

Magpie's early releases established company identity. Transantiago (2011), their first publication, presented economic game about Santiago's public transportation system—unconventional subject matter signaling Magpie's willingness to explore topics beyond traditional fantasy adventure. Though modest in commercial terms, Transantiago demonstrated design ambition and thematic range.

The company's breakthrough came with Urban Shadows (2015), written by Andrew Medeiros and Mark Diaz Truman. This Powered by the Apocalypse game explored urban fantasy political drama, featuring vampires, werewolves, wizards, and other supernatural factions vying for power in dark cities. The game's focus on political maneuvering, debt economy, and corruption distinguished it from combat-focused urban fantasy games.

Urban Shadows demonstrated Magpie's design philosophy crystallizing. The game featured explicit queer-positive content, diverse representation in art and text, and mechanics supporting political intrigue over tactical combat. Debt mechanics tracked favors and obligations between characters, creating web of relationships driving drama. Faction mechanics encouraged political thinking and coalition-building. The corruption system showed characters changing through exposure to supernatural world's darkness.

The game achieved critical and commercial success, winning multiple awards and establishing Magpie as significant PbtA publisher. Urban Shadows proved that progressive representation and mechanical innovation could achieve mainstream acceptance and commercial viability. The game's Kickstarter success validated Magpie's approach and provided resources for ambitious future projects.

Masks: A New Generation (2016), designed by Brendan Conway, became Magpie's signature success. This PbtA game focused on teenage superheroes navigating identity, relationships, and expectations while developing powers and fighting villains. The game's genius lay in focusing on emotional conflicts and identity formation rather than just superhero combat.

Masks' mechanical innovations perfectly supported thematic goals. Instead of fixed attributes, characters had Labels—Danger, Freak, Savior, Superior, Mundane—representing how they saw themselves. Labels shifted based on others' influence, mechanizing how external opinions affected self-perception. This fluid identity system captured teenage experience and superhero coming-of-age narratives brilliantly.

The game explicitly centered queer experiences and diverse identities. Playbooks supported various gender identities, sexual orientations, and cultural backgrounds naturally integrated into mechanics and text. This representation felt organic rather than tokenistic, setting new standards for inclusive design.

Masks achieved remarkable commercial success and cultural impact. The game won multiple ENnie awards, achieved extensive actual play coverage, and introduced many players to both PbtA mechanics and indie gaming broadly. Masks demonstrated that games centering teenage emotional drama and identity exploration could achieve commercial success rivaling traditional adventure games.

The success provided Magpie with resources and reputation enabling ambitious expansion. The company became sought-after publisher for innovative designers and licensed properties. Magpie's track record of successful Kickstarters, professional production, and fair creator treatment attracted top indie talent.

Bluebeard's Bride (2017), designed by Whitney "Strix" Beltrán, Marissa Kelly, and Sarah Richardson, showcased Magpie's range. This horror game explored abusive relationships through fairy tale lens, creating intensely disturbing experience examining domestic violence and psychological abuse. The game featured single protagonist—the Bride—portrayed by multiple players representing different psychological aspects.

Bluebeard's Bride pushed boundaries of what RPGs could address. The game's unflinching examination of abuse, all-women design team, and explicit content warnings demonstrated maturity about difficult subject matter. While commercially smaller than Masks, Bluebeard's Bride earned critical acclaim and demonstrated Magpie's commitment to supporting diverse voices and challenging content.

Root: The Roleplaying Game (2020) represented major milestone—licensed property based on Cole Wehrle's acclaimed board game. Root combined PbtA mechanics with woodland warfare setting featuring animal factions struggling for forest control. The license demonstrated major properties trusted Magpie with prestigious brands while maintaining innovative design approaches.

Root's success validated Magpie's evolution from pure indie publisher to company bridging indie sensibilities with licensed properties. The game achieved strong sales, positive reception, and demonstrated that licensed games could maintain mechanical innovation and thematic depth rather than just exploiting existing IP.

Throughout expansion, Magpie maintained commitment to progressive values. The company consistently hired diverse creators, featured inclusive representation, supported marginalized voices, and addressed social issues through thoughtful game design. This consistency demonstrated that progressive values represented core identity rather than marketing strategy.

Magpie's business approach balanced indie flexibility with professional standards. Kickstarter campaigns funded projects while building community investment. Professional production matched major publishers in quality. Fair creator contracts and revenue sharing attracted talented designers. Transparent communication built trust with backers and customers.

The company became influential voice in industry conversations about representation, creator treatment, and business ethics. Magpie demonstrated that publishers could prioritize ethics, inclusion, and innovation while achieving commercial success—proof of concept influencing broader industry discussions.

Today, Magpie Games operates as respected publisher spanning original properties and licenses while maintaining commitment to diverse creators, innovative design, and progressive representation. The company exemplifies how indie publishers can achieve professional success without compromising values or experimental design sensibilities.

## Company History Expansion

Magpie's development reflected broader indie gaming evolution. The company emerged during PbtA's expansion beyond Apocalypse World, when designers explored how the engine could support diverse genres and themes. Magpie recognized PbtA's potential for commercial indie publishing while many viewed it as experimental fringe system.

The founders' backgrounds shaped company direction. Mark Diaz Truman brought game design expertise, theoretical knowledge from indie gaming discourse, and publishing business understanding. Marissa Kelly contributed design skills, editorial vision, and commitment to inclusive representation. Together they created partnership balancing creative ambition with business sustainability.

Early financial challenges tested commitment. Indie publishing required significant capital investment for professional production while sales remained uncertain. Kickstarter provided crucial funding mechanism, but successful campaigns required existing reputation and marketing expertise. Magpie's early projects built gradually toward breakthrough success.

The company's geographic location in St. Louis, outside traditional gaming industry hubs, presented challenges and opportunities. Distance from coastal gaming centers required stronger online presence and convention engagement to build visibility. However, Midwestern base provided lower operating costs and distinctive perspective outside insular coastal gaming scenes.

Magpie's commitment to queer representation reflected personal investment and strategic market recognition. Founders recognized that LGBTQ+ gamers represented underserved market hungry for authentic representation. By centering rather than tokenizing queer characters and creators, Magpie built loyal following while influencing broader industry representation standards.

The company's editorial standards distinguished it from some indie publishers. Magpie invested in professional editing, sensitivity reading, and production quality matching traditional publishers. This quality commitment required higher budgets but created products competing effectively in mainstream market rather than just indie niche.

Magpie's designer relationships emphasized collaboration over hierarchy. The company viewed designers as creative partners rather than work-for-hire contractors. Fair contracts, revenue sharing, and creative control made Magpie desirable publisher for talented designers choosing where to publish projects.

Convention presence proved crucial for building community and visibility. Magpie maintained strong presence at Gen Con, PAX, and gaming conventions, running games, meeting fans, and networking with industry. These face-to-face connections built relationships translating to sales and reputation.

The company's social media strategy leveraged progressive positioning. Magpie's frank discussions of representation, inclusive design, and social issues attracted like-minded creators and players while occasionally generating controversy. However, commitment to values proved commercially successful despite some pushback from conservative gaming corners.

Actual play content significantly boosted visibility. Masks particularly benefited from actual play coverage, with shows demonstrating the game's emotional depth and inclusive design. These actual plays functioned as extended demonstrations converting viewers to customers more effectively than traditional marketing.

Magpie's expansion into licensed properties represented calculated evolution. Rather than abandoning indie roots, licenses like Root demonstrated that indie design sensibilities could enhance rather than compromise licensed games. This approach attracted licenses from progressive properties valuing innovative design over generic system application.

## Publishing Philosophy

Magpie Games' publishing philosophy centers on several core principles:

**Queer-Positive Centering**: Magpie doesn't just include queer representation—games center queer experiences as default rather than exception. LGBTQ+ characters, relationships, and identities receive mechanical support and narrative prominence. This centering creates games where queer players see themselves reflected authentically rather than relegated to sidebars or special cases.

**Diverse Creator Support**: The company actively seeks designers, artists, and contributors from marginalized backgrounds. Magpie recognizes that authentic representation requires diverse creative teams with lived experiences informing content. Fair compensation and collaborative relationships make company attractive to talented diverse creators.

**Powered by the Apocalypse Focus**: While not exclusively PbtA, Magpie recognized the engine's potential for commercial indie publishing. The company became premier PbtA publisher, demonstrating system's versatility across genres while maintaining consistent quality and innovation.

**Professional Production Standards**: Magpie matches major publishers in production quality—professional editing, premium art, solid layout, quality printing. This commitment ensures indie games compete effectively with traditional publishers rather than appearing amateur by comparison.

**Emotional Engagement**: Magpie's games prioritize emotional engagement and character development over tactical combat or simulation. Mechanics support exploring relationships, identity, psychological states, and dramatic choices. This focus attracts players seeking meaningful dramatic experiences.

**Thematic Coherence**: Each Magpie game pursues focused thematic agenda with mechanics supporting specific creative goals. Rather than generic systems, games target precise experiences with every mechanical element reinforcing thematic objectives.

**Safety and Consent**: Magpie emphasizes safety tools, content warnings, and consent mechanics. Games addressing difficult content include guidance for safe exploration. This maturity about potentially triggering material demonstrates responsibility toward players.

**Community Building**: The company invests in building communities around games through organized play, online engagement, and convention support. These communities provide ongoing play groups, content creation, and word-of-mouth marketing.

**Kickstarter Expertise**: Magpie mastered Kickstarter publishing, running campaigns that fund projects while building community investment. Professional campaign presentation, clear communication, and reliable delivery built reputation for trustworthy campaigns.

**Values Consistency**: Magpie maintains progressive values across all operations—creator treatment, representation, community engagement, and business practices. This consistency demonstrates genuine commitment rather than opportunistic marketing.

## Major Releases

### Masks: A New Generation (2016)

Masks: A New Generation transformed teenage superhero gaming by focusing on identity formation, emotional conflicts, and relationships rather than just superpowered combat. Designed by Brendan Conway, the game centered on young heroes navigating expectations from adults, peers, media, and themselves while developing powers and fighting villains.

The game's mechanical innovations brilliantly captured adolescent experience. Labels—Danger, Freak, Savior, Superior, Mundane—replaced traditional attributes, representing how characters viewed themselves. These Labels shifted constantly based on others' influence, mechanizing how external opinions affected self-perception. When mentors praised hero's power, Superior increased while other Labels decreased. When civilians feared hero's abilities, Freak increased. This fluid identity system captured teenage experience where self-perception constantly shifted based on others' judgment.

Playbooks represented superhero archetypes through adolescent psychology lens. The Janus struggled with dual identity and maintaining normal life. The Beacon lacked powers but compensated through determination and skill. The Nova wielded overwhelming power threatening to consume them. Each playbook explored specific identity conflicts and coming-of-age challenges.

Influence mechanics structured relationships between characters. When someone had Influence over character, their opinions shifted that character's Labels. Characters could reject Influence by explaining why that person's opinion didn't matter, creating moments of self-definition and growing independence. This mechanized how teenagers gradually developed autonomous identity separate from others' expectations.

Adult NPCs functioned differently from traditional wise mentors. Adults imposed expectations, offered unwanted advice, and demonstrated flawed approaches heroes might emulate or reject. The game explicitly addressed navigating relationships with authority figures whose power and experience didn't necessarily translate to wisdom or good judgment.

Team mechanics emphasized found family and peer support. The team provided space where young heroes could be themselves, support each other, and develop independent from adult supervision. This captured how adolescent peer groups functioned as primary identity formation context.

The game explicitly supported diverse identities, orientations, and backgrounds. Playbooks accommodated various gender identities and sexual orientations naturally. Cultural backgrounds informed character details without reducing characters to stereotypes. This inclusive design made Masks welcoming to players traditionally marginalized in superhero media.

Masks achieved remarkable commercial success and cultural impact. The game won multiple ENnie awards, achieved extensive actual play coverage, and introduced many players to PbtA gaming. The game demonstrated that focusing on emotional drama and identity rather than tactical combat could achieve mainstream commercial success.

The game's influence extended beyond direct imitators. Masks' approach to fluid identity mechanics, influence systems, and adolescent themes influenced numerous later games exploring similar concepts. The game proved teenage dramatic focus could support full campaigns as effectively as traditional adventure structures.

### Urban Shadows (2015)

Urban Shadows brought political urban fantasy to PbtA, focusing on supernatural factions vying for power in dark cities. Designed by Andrew Medeiros and Mark Diaz Truman, the game centered on vampires, werewolves, wizards, hunters, and other supernatural beings navigating complex political landscape where alliances shifted and debts accumulated.

The game's faction system structured political landscape. Four factions—Night (vampires, ghosts), Power (wizards, oracles), Mortality (humans, hunters), Wild (werewolves, fae)—represented different approaches to supernatural existence. Characters allied with factions but maintained individual agency, creating tension between factional loyalty and personal goals.

Debt mechanics drove political drama. Characters accumulated and spent Debts—favors, obligations, blackmail—representing political currency. Calling in Debt provided mechanical advantages while owing Debt created vulnerability. The web of Debts between characters generated political complexity where everyone owed and was owed, creating intricate relationship networks.

Corruption tracked characters' transformation through exposure to supernatural world's darkness. As Corruption increased, characters gained power but moved toward Dramatic moment where they fundamentally changed—becoming full vampire, embracing monstrous nature, or achieving transformation. This tension between power and corruption created compelling progression arcs.

Political moves emphasized negotiation, manipulation, and factional maneuvering over direct combat. Characters advanced by pursuing political goals, building alliances, and navigating faction politics rather than just fighting enemies. This political focus distinguished Urban Shadows from combat-focused urban fantasy games.

The game featured explicit queer-positive representation with diverse characters, relationships, and identities throughout text and art. Supernatural society's social structures provided space for exploring identity and relationships outside mainstream norms. This representation felt organic to setting rather than forced inclusion.

Urban Shadows demonstrated PbtA's versatility for political drama and factional play. The game influenced later political games and showed how debt mechanics could drive dramatic conflicts. Commercial success validated Magpie's approach and established company reputation.

### Bluebeard's Bride (2017)

Bluebeard's Bride presented horror game exploring abusive relationships through fairy tale lens, creating intensely disturbing experience examining domestic violence, psychological abuse, and gendered horror. Designed by all-women team including Whitney "Strix" Beltrán, Marissa Kelly, and Sarah Richardson, the game demonstrated maturity about difficult subject matter while pushing boundaries of RPG content.

The game's structure featured single protagonist—the Bride—portrayed by multiple players each representing different psychological aspect: Animus (aggression), Bloodlust (base desires), Fatale (seduction), Maiden (innocence), Mother (nurturing), Witch (intuition). This fragmented protagonist approach created unique play experience where Bride's internal conflicts played out through different players' interpretations.

Players explored Bluebeard's mansion discovering rooms revealing husband's horrifying secrets. Each room presented disturbing revelations forcing Bride to confront marriage's reality. The structure guaranteed progression toward awful truth while players chose how Bride responded to revelations.

Investigation mechanics emphasized horror and psychological trauma over puzzle-solving. As Bride explored and learned terrible truths, she accumulated trauma and faced impossible choice between accepting horror or rejecting marriage. The mechanical systems ensured psychologically damaging experience reflecting abusive relationship dynamics.

The game included extensive content warnings, safety tools discussion, and guidance for handling difficult material. This mature approach to potentially triggering content demonstrated responsibility while enabling safe exploration of serious themes. The game showed how RPGs could address difficult subjects respectfully with appropriate support structures.

Bluebeard's Bride earned critical acclaim, won awards for horror design, and demonstrated games could explore abusive relationships and gendered violence thoughtfully. While commercially smaller than Masks, the game proved significant culturally and artistically. The all-women design team brought authentic perspective to gendered horror, creating work resonating with players' lived experiences.

### Root: The Roleplaying Game (2020)

Root: The Roleplaying Game adapted Cole Wehrle's acclaimed board game into PbtA roleplaying game, demonstrating Magpie's successful expansion into licensed properties while maintaining innovative design sensibilities. The game explored woodland creatures' factional conflicts as Marquisate occupation, Eyrie dynasties, forest's Woodland Alliance, and wandering vagabonds struggled for control.

The game combined PbtA mechanics with Root's asymmetric faction play. Different factions followed different rules reflecting their nature—Marquisate's bureaucratic control, Eyrie's decree-bound traditions, Alliance's guerrilla resistance. This asymmetry created varied play experiences while maintaining mechanical coherence.

Clearing-based structure divided forest into locations with their own politics, resources, and sympathies. Characters moved between clearings, influenced local situations, and participated in larger factional conflicts. This geographic structure grounded political conflicts in specific locations with tangible stakes.

Reputation mechanics tracked standing with various factions. Characters built reputation through actions, spent it for factional support, and managed complex web of relationships across forest's political landscape. This created situations where characters navigated conflicting factional loyalties and shifting alliances.

The game balanced cute woodland aesthetic with serious political themes—occupation, resistance, collaboration, revolution. This contrast between adorable animal characters and weighty political conflicts created distinctive tone. The setting allowed exploring serious themes through fantastical lens providing emotional distance.

Root demonstrated licensed games could maintain mechanical innovation and thematic depth rather than just applying generic mechanics to existing IP. The game succeeded commercially, validated Magpie's evolution toward licensed properties, and showed indie design sensibilities enhanced rather than compromised licensed games.
