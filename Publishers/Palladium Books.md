# Palladium Books

**Founded**: 1981
**Active Era**: Golden Age through Modern Era (1981-Present)
**Historical Significance**: ⭐ 4/5
**Headquarters**: Taylor, Michigan
**Status**: Active

## Company History

Palladium Books emerged in 1981 when [[Kevin Siembieda]] founded independent publisher that would become synonymous with high-powered, kitchen-sink gaming emphasizing vast interconnected "Megaverse" of compatible game worlds. For over four decades, Palladium maintained fiercely independent operation, surviving industry upheavals that destroyed many contemporaries while cultivating intensely loyal following. The company represents both independent publishing's possibilities and challenges—demonstrating how distinctive vision and devoted community can sustain operations across decades while also illustrating difficulties maintaining relevance when refusing to adapt to changing industry standards and audience expectations.

Kevin Siembieda's background as artist, writer, and gamer shaped Palladium from inception. Rather than imitating D&D or other established games, Siembieda developed distinctive approach emphasizing detailed character creation, power progression, cross-genre compatibility, and simulationist detail. This independent vision created games feeling fundamentally different from contemporaries, attracting audiences wanting alternatives to mainstream gaming while potentially limiting mass market appeal.

The Palladium Fantasy Role-Playing Game (1983) established company identity. The game presented traditional fantasy gaming but with distinctive Palladium sensibilities: detailed character classes (O.C.C.s—Occupational Character Classes), skills-based advancement, combat system emphasizing tactical positioning and armor types, and extensive equipment catalogs. The setting combined standard fantasy elements with Siembieda's world-building, creating distinctive if somewhat generic fantasy world.

The game achieved moderate success, establishing Palladium as legitimate publisher. However, early releases demonstrated patterns that would characterize Palladium throughout its history: dense text-heavy layouts, extensive mechanical detail, baroque rules systems, and emphasis on character power and capability.

Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles & Other Strangeness (1985) represented major commercial success and demonstrated Palladium's licensed property capabilities. Based on Eastman and Laird's then-independent comic, the game let players create mutant animal characters with human intelligence navigating modern world's absurdities and dangers.

The game coincided fortuitously with TMNT's explosion into mainstream consciousness through cartoon adaptation and merchandising. Palladium's RPG benefited enormously from this cultural moment, achieving sales far exceeding typical RPG market. The game introduced many to roleplaying through beloved property, though Palladium's mechanical complexity challenged newcomers.

The animal creation system—spending points on animal forms, mutations, and abilities—provided entertaining character creation where group could include mutant raccoon ninja, intelligent dog investigator, and anthropomorphic bird scientist. This flexibility and power fantasy appealed broadly.

Robotech: The Shadow Chronicles (1986) secured another significant license—Harmony Gold's adaptation of Japanese anime Macross. The mecha-focused science fiction setting provided military space opera adventures with transforming fighters, epic battles, and romantic drama.

The game adapted Palladium's system to mecha combat, creating rules for piloting, transforming, and fighting in giant robots. The detailed vehicle statistics, weapon systems, and combat rules satisfied simulationist preferences while supporting anime-style action.

Robotech achieved strong sales particularly among anime fans discovering tabletop gaming. The game demonstrated Palladium's ability to secure and execute licensed properties while adapting system to diverse genres.

However, Rifts (1990) became Palladium's most successful and defining product. This post-apocalyptic science fantasy setting presented Earth transformed by dimensional rifts releasing magic, summoning aliens and monsters, and creating wildly diverse regions. The premise enabled literally anything—cyborgs fought dragons, alien invaders battled techno-wizards, and transdimensional traders navigated between parallel dimensions.

The "kitchen sink" approach—including every conceivable character type, setting element, and power level—created maximalist gaming experience. Players could portray Glitter Boys (iconic powered armor pilots), dragon hatchlings, ley line walkers (magic users), Juicers (augmented warriors), and hundreds of other options.

The Megaverse concept connected Rifts with other Palladium games. Characters could theoretically cross between Rifts, Palladium Fantasy, Heroes Unlimited, and other settings through dimensional travel. This interconnectedness appealed to audiences wanting vast, unlimited campaign possibilities.

Rifts achieved remarkable commercial success, becoming Palladium's flagship and best-selling product. The game spawned extensive supplement line exploring Coalition States (techno-fascist human supremacists), various kingdoms and regions, alien dimensions, and countless character options. The sheer volume of Rifts material eventually became both selling point (endless content) and barrier (overwhelming newcomers).

The setting's gonzo maximalism polarized gaming community. Supporters loved unlimited possibilities, high power levels, and imaginative wildness. Critics found mechanical inconsistency, balance problems, and baroque rules frustrating. Regardless, Rifts cultivated devoted following sustaining Palladium for decades.

Throughout 1990s and 2000s, Palladium maintained steady publication across multiple lines. Beyond Darkness, Nightbane, Splicers, Dead Reign, and other games demonstrated continuing creativity though none achieved Rifts' success. The company operated conservatively, avoiding debt-fueled expansion but also limiting growth.

Palladium's business model remained distinctly old-school. The company maintained traditional three-tier distribution (publisher→distributor→retailer), avoided digital distribution for years, resisted crowdfunding, and maintained small operation centered on Siembieda's personal control. This conservatism provided stability but potentially limited adaptation to changing industry conditions.

The 2000s brought significant challenges. The d20 boom and bust disrupted markets, digital distribution transformed industry economics, and audience expectations evolved. Palladium maintained existing approaches largely unchanged, creating growing disconnect between company practices and contemporary standards.

Financial difficulties emerged periodically. Production delays, cash flow problems, and occasional crises threatened operations. Siembieda's public appeals to fans during difficulties demonstrated both community loyalty and company's ongoing struggles. The devoted fanbase repeatedly supported company through purchases and patience during difficult periods.

Controversies also marked Palladium's history. The Robotech RPG Tactics Kickstarter (2013) became notorious disaster—massive delays, production problems, communication failures, and ultimate partial fulfillment created one of gaming's most infamous crowdfunding failures. The debacle damaged reputation and demonstrated challenges when Palladium attempted contemporary business models without appropriate expertise or infrastructure.

Legal issues with former freelancers, work-for-hire disputes, and licensing complications created additional difficulties. These problems reflected small company's challenges managing complex legal, financial, and organizational demands.

Despite challenges, Palladium survived through devoted community, Rifts' sustained sales, conservative operations, and Siembieda's commitment. The company avoided acquisition, bankruptcy, or closure that eliminated many contemporaries. This survival itself represents significant achievement regardless of criticism regarding business practices or design evolution.

Today, Palladium operates as independent publisher maintaining existing product lines with devoted but niche audience. The company represents both independent publishing's romantic ideal—single creator maintaining vision across decades—and its limitations—difficulty adapting to changing markets and audience expectations.

## Business Model and Philosophy

Palladium developed distinctive approach reflecting founder's priorities and independence:

**Auteur Publishing**: Kevin Siembieda maintained direct personal control over nearly all aspects—creative direction, writing, art direction, business decisions. This centralization enabled consistent vision but created bottlenecks and limited organizational growth.

**System Consistency**: The Palladium system (Megaversal system) provided unified mechanics across diverse games. This consistency enabled Megaverse connections and reduced learning curves between games. However, system refinement remained limited despite decades of feedback.

**Supplement-Heavy Model**: Extensive supplement lines for flagship properties created ongoing revenue while serving dedicated fans wanting comprehensive coverage. However, volume potentially overwhelmed newcomers and created barrier to entry.

**Conservative Operations**: Palladium avoided debt, maintained small staff, operated sustainably within means, and avoided speculative expansion. This conservatism enabled survival but potentially limited growth opportunities.

**Traditional Distribution**: Maintaining three-tier distribution model, resisting direct sales emphasis, and slow digital adoption reflected traditional approach. This maintained retailer relationships but potentially missed opportunities.

**Independent Resistance**: Fierce independence from corporate acquisition, licensing to larger publishers, or external investment maintained creative control but limited resources and scaling opportunities.

**Fan Community Dependence**: Direct relationship with devoted fanbase provided support during crises but also created echo chamber potentially limiting adaptation to broader market feedback.

**Licensed Property Pursuit**: Securing licenses like TMNT and Robotech provided commercial boosts though licensing challenges and renewals created complications.

**Creative Maximalism**: "More is better" philosophy produced vast content volumes appealing to dedicated fans but potentially overwhelming casual audiences.

**Mechanical Complexity**: Detailed, simulationist mechanics satisfied specific preferences but created accessibility barriers compared to streamlined contemporary designs.

This approach created sustainable if limited business serving devoted niche audience while maintaining independence and creative vision across four decades.

## Major Releases

### Rifts (1990)

Rifts became Palladium's flagship product and defining creation—post-apocalyptic science fantasy setting where dimensional rifts transformed Earth into wildly diverse, high-powered adventure world. The premise enabled unlimited possibilities: any character concept, any setting element, any power level could exist somewhere in Rifts Earth.

The apocalyptic history saw ley lines (magical energy conduits) awakening catastrophically, nuclear war triggering magical cascade, and dimensional rifts opening across planet. Centuries later, Earth featured incredible diversity: Coalition States' techno-fascist human supremacy, Free Quebec's independence struggle, Tolkeen's magical kingdom, Atlantis controlled by alien Splugorth, South American vampire kingdoms, and countless other factions and regions.

Character options proved staggering. Players could portray Glitter Boy pilots (iconic powered armor), Juicers (augmented warriors with limited lifespans), Crazies (psychotically enhanced combatants), Dragon Hatchlings, Ley Line Walkers (mages), Cyber-Knights, Coalition soldiers, Techno-Wizards, alien species, and hundreds more. The sheer variety satisfied power fantasy and concept diversity desires.

The Megaverse concept connected Rifts with other Palladium games. Rifts enabling dimensional travel meant characters could visit Palladium Fantasy's world, Heroes Unlimited's Earth, or Phase World's intergalactic civilization. This interconnectedness appealed to audiences wanting unlimited campaign scope.

Combat system emphasized armor (Mega-Damage vs. S.D.C.), weapon types, positioning, and tactical choices. The detailed approach satisfied simulationist preferences though baroque complexity challenged some players.

The extensive supplement line explored regions, factions, character options, and storylines comprehensively. World Books covered Africa, Japan, England, Atlantis, and many others. Sourcebooks detailed Coalition States, magic, technology, and countless topics. The volume provided enormous content for dedicated fans while potentially overwhelming newcomers.

Rifts achieved remarkable commercial success and cultivated devoted following. The game's imaginative scope, unlimited possibilities, and power fantasy appealed strongly despite mechanical complexity and balance issues. For many players, Rifts represented ultimate kitchen-sink gaming experience.

### Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles & Other Strangeness (1985)

TMNT RPG adapted Eastman and Laird's comic into roleplaying format before property's mainstream explosion. The game's fortuitous timing—releasing as cartoon adaptation began—generated significant commercial success and introduced many to tabletop gaming.

The animal creation system provided entertaining character building. Players selected animal base (mammals, birds, reptiles, etc.), added mutations (human intelligence, bipedal form, special abilities), and created character histories. Groups could include mutant raccoon ninjas, intelligent dog detectives, anthropomorphic cats, or bizarre combinations.

The setting combined comic's gritty urban adventures with absurd humor. Adventures involved street crime, scientific conspiracies, alien invasions, and bizarre encounters. The tone balanced action with comedy, supporting serious adventure or lighthearted fun.

The system used standard Palladium mechanics—attributes, skills, hit points, S.D.C.—adapted for modern setting. Combat provided detailed resolution while non-combat skills supported investigation and social interaction.

The game's success extended Palladium's reach significantly. TMNT's popularity brought audiences beyond typical RPG markets. While mechanical complexity challenged newcomers, the appealing premise and beloved property overcame barriers for many.

Supplements explored mutations, equipment, adventures, and campaign options. After the Bomb spin-off expanded post-apocalyptic mutant animal gaming. The extensive support demonstrated TMNT's importance to Palladium's commercial success.

### Robotech RPG (1986)

Robotech RPG adapted Harmony Gold's anime series into tabletop format, providing mecha combat, military space opera, and romantic drama. The game appealed particularly to anime fans discovering tabletop gaming through beloved property.

The setting covered Robotech's various eras: Macross Saga (transforming Veritech fighters vs. alien Zentraedi), Robotech Masters (second invasion), and New Generation (resistance against Invid). Each era offered distinctive aesthetics and campaign frameworks.

Mecha combat rules provided detailed vehicle statistics, weapon systems, transformation mechanics, and tactical options. The simulationist approach satisfied audiences wanting comprehensive technical specifications and combat resolution.

Character creation included military roles, skill specializations, and relationship dynamics. The system supported both mecha pilot action and personal drama between missions.

The game achieved strong sales among anime fans and demonstrated Palladium's licensed property capabilities. Extensive supplements covered ships, mecha, aliens, and campaigns throughout Robotech's continuity.

### Heroes Unlimited (1984)

Heroes Unlimited provided superhero gaming in Palladium system. The game enabled creating vast range of hero types: mutants, experiments, magic users, aliens, technological heroes, and ordinary humans with training.

The point-buy character creation allocated budget for powers, skills, and attributes. The flexibility supported diverse concepts from street-level vigilantes to cosmic powerhouses, though system struggled balancing such range.

The setting presented modern Earth with emerging superhuman population. The ambiguous framework allowed campaigns from four-color heroics to gritty realism.

While never achieving commercial success matching Rifts or TMNT, Heroes Unlimited maintained devoted following and received ongoing support. The game demonstrated Palladium's genre versatility and Megaverse interconnection potential.

### Palladium Fantasy Role-Playing Game (1983)

Palladium Fantasy established company's first major original property. The game provided traditional fantasy adventures but with distinctive Palladium mechanics and setting details.

The O.C.C. system (Occupational Character Classes) provided character frameworks similar to D&D classes but with Palladium's detailed skill-based approach. Warriors, wizards, priests, and others gained abilities through class and skill selections.

The setting combined standard fantasy elements—humans, elves, dwarves, orcs—with original creations. The world, while less distinctive than Rifts, provided campaign framework and connected to Megaverse.

Combat emphasized armor types, weapon choices, and tactical positioning. The detailed approach satisfied simulationist preferences though complexity exceeded many competitors.

While overshadowed by Rifts, Palladium Fantasy maintained devoted following and ongoing support. The game demonstrated company's fantasy gaming capabilities and provided Megaverse connection point.

## Impact on Industry

Palladium Books influenced gaming significantly despite niche positioning:

**Megaverse Concept**: The interconnected multiverse enabling cross-game play influenced later shared universe approaches. The concept demonstrated appeal of unlimited setting scope.

**Kitchen Sink Gaming**: Rifts' anything-goes maximalism influenced games embracing unlimited options and genre-mixing. The approach showed demand existed for high-power, unrestricted gaming.

**Independent Publishing Survival**: Palladium's four-decade independent operation demonstrated possibility of surviving without corporate backing. The company's longevity encouraged other independent publishers despite challenges.

**Licensed Property Success**: TMNT and Robotech's commercial success demonstrated licensed properties' potential for smaller publishers, though subsequent complications illustrated difficulties.

**Power Fantasy Gaming**: Rifts' high-powered characters and epic scope influenced games emphasizing empowerment and capability over limitation and challenge.

**Cautionary Tales**: Business challenges, crowdfunding disasters, and adaptation difficulties provided cautionary examples for industry about importance of professional operations, communication, and evolution.

**Cult Following Model**: Demonstrated that intensely devoted niche audiences could sustain publishers even without broad market appeal or industry trends adoption.

## Current Status and Future

Palladium Books continues operating with devoted but limited audience:

**Financial Health**: Sustainable operations maintaining modest profitability through dedicated fanbase though limited compared to larger publishers.

**Product Lines**: Ongoing support for Rifts, Palladium Fantasy, and other properties maintains community engagement though release frequency reduced from earlier periods.

**Leadership**: Kevin Siembieda continues directing company with unchanged personal control and vision.

**Industry Position**: Niche publisher serving devoted following largely separate from mainstream gaming discussions and markets.

**Future Challenges**: Aging fanbase without significant new player acquisition, resistance to contemporary design and business practices, competition from more accessible alternatives, and succession planning questions.

**Legacy Recognition**: Despite current limitations, historical significance and influence remain acknowledged by industry and gaming historians.
