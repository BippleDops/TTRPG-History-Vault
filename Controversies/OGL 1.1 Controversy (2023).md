# OGL 1.1 Controversy (2023)

**Year**: 2023
**Key Dates**: January 5 - January 27, 2023
**Parties Involved**: Wizards of the Coast, D&D community, third-party publishers, Paizo, Kobold Press, VTT platforms
**Impact Areas**: Open gaming ecosystem, third-party publishing, community trust
**Resolution**: WotC withdrew OGL 1.1, released SRD 5.1 under Creative Commons
**Significance**: 5/5 - Industry-transforming event

## Overview

The OGL 1.1 Controversy was a major crisis in the tabletop RPG industry that occurred in January 2023, when Wizards of the Coast attempted to update the Open Game License (OGL) - the foundation of D&D third-party publishing for over 20 years - with a new version (OGL 1.1) that dramatically restricted content creators' rights. The leak of the draft OGL 1.1 terms on January 5, 2023, sparked immediate backlash from the TTRPG community, leading to D&D Beyond subscription cancellations, publisher organizing, and ultimately forcing WotC to completely reverse course and release D&D's core rules under Creative Commons instead.

The controversy highlighted tensions between corporate ownership and community values, raised questions about the trustworthiness of "open" licenses, and catalyzed the creation of alternative licensing frameworks. It resulted in the largest unified mobilization of the TTRPG community in history and permanently altered the relationship between D&D's corporate owners and its community of players and creators.

The resolution - WotC's decision to place the D&D 5.1 SRD into Creative Commons - was a victory for the community but came at the cost of lasting trust damage and accelerated development of D&D alternatives.

## Background: The Original OGL

### Creation of OGL 1.0a (2000)

In 2000, Wizards of the Coast released Dungeons & Dragons Third Edition alongside the Open Game License (OGL) 1.0a. This revolutionary move allowed third-party publishers to create and sell compatible content legally, opening D&D to unprecedented third-party support.

**Key Terms of OGL 1.0a**:
- Perpetual and irrevocable license
- Anyone could publish OGL content
- Must distinguish Open Game Content from Product Identity
- No royalties required
- "Use of Contributor Work" clause allowing others to build on OGL content

**Impact**:
- Explosion of third-party d20 System content
- Small publishers and independent creators flourished
- Retroclones (like [[OSRIC (2006)]]) preserved earlier editions
- Pathfinder became viable D&D alternative
- Industry standard for 20+ years

### D&D 5E and the OGL (2014-2022)

When D&D 5th Edition launched in 2014, Wizards of the Coast maintained the OGL 1.0a, creating the 5E SRD (System Reference Document) as Open Game Content. This decision:

- Enabled third-party 5E publishing
- Supported Roll20, Fantasy Grounds, and other VTT platforms
- Allowed independent creators to build 5E content
- Contributed to 5E's massive success

By 2022, D&D 5E was the most successful edition in history, with the OGL enabling an ecosystem of third-party content estimated to generate hundreds of millions of dollars annually.

### Tensions Growing (2021-2022)

Signs of WotC reconsidering OGL:

**August 2021**: Wizards of the Coast acquired D&D Beyond for $146.3 million, signaling digital strategy focus

**September 2022**: WotC announced D&D "One D&D" (later called D&D 2024) with hints of "updated OGL"

**December 2022**: Rumors began circulating that OGL 1.1 would be restrictive

**Community Concerns**:
- Would WotC try to "de-authorize" OGL 1.0a despite "perpetual" language?
- Would new terms include royalties?
- Would VTT platforms be affected?
- Could retroclones and Pathfinder be targeted?

## Timeline of Events

### Week 1: The Leak (January 5-8, 2023)

**January 5, 2023**: Gizmodo and io9 published leaked draft of OGL 1.1

**Draft Terms Revealed**:
- OGL 1.0a would be "de-authorized" (declared no longer valid)
- 25% royalty on revenue over $750,000
- WotC could use any OGL 1.1 content royalty-free for any purpose
- WotC could revoke license at will
- Restrictions on "harmful or illegal" content (WotC as sole arbiter)
- VTT platforms severely limited
- Kickstarter and crowdfunding restrictions

**Immediate Reaction**:
- Twitter, Reddit, and forums erupted in outrage
- "#OpenDnD" and "#DnDBegone" hashtags trended
- YouTubers made emergency videos
- Legal experts weighed in on validity of "de-authorizing" OGL 1.0a

### Week 2: Community Mobilization (January 9-15)

**D&D Beyond Cancellations**:
- Tens of thousands cancelled subscriptions
- #CancelDnDBeyond trending
- Financial pressure on WotC mounting

**Publisher Response**:
- Paizo announced investigation into alternative licensing
- Kobold Press pledged to continue supporting OGL 1.0a
- Critical Role remained silent (later addressed)
- Hundreds of small publishers voiced concerns

**Roll20 Statement** (January 11):
- Would not accept OGL 1.1
- Committed to supporting OGL 1.0a content
- Significant given Roll20's market position

**Organized Campaigns**:
- OpenDnD.games website launched tracking signatories
- Over 60,000 signatures pledging OGL 1.0a support
- Publishers coordinating response
- Community-driven educational content about licensing

### Week 3: WotC's First Response (January 13)

**January 13**: Wizards of the Coast published statement on D&D Beyond

**Key Claims**:
- OGL 1.1 was "draft shared with select partners"
- Not intended to affect VTT platforms or individual creators
- Royalties only for commercial publishers over threshold
- "Protecting D&D" from harmful content and AI-generated works

**Community Reaction**:
- Statement dismissed as insufficient
- No commitment to keep OGL 1.0a
- "We heard you" without meaningful change
- Cancellations continued

### Week 4: The ORC License (January 13)

**January 13**: Paizo announced **ORC License** (Open RPG Creative License)

**Key Points**:
- System-agnostic open license
- Managed by independent nonprofit
- No corporate control
- Perpetual and irrevocable by design
- Support from Kobold Press, Chaosium, Legendary Games, Rogue Genius Games, others

**Significance**:
- Alternative to OGL emerging
- Publishers preparing for post-OGL ecosystem
- Pressure on WotC increased

### Week 5: WotC's Second Response (January 18)

**January 18**: WotC published second statement titled "Onward"

**Content**:
- Acknowledged missteps
- OGL 1.1 wouldn't de-authorize OGL 1.0a
- Royalties and content ownership clauses removed
- Still wanted creators to adopt OGL 1.1
- Draft would be released for community feedback

**Community Reaction**:
- Cautiously optimistic but skeptical
- Wanted to see actual draft
- Concern about "revocable" vs. "irrevocable"
- Trust severely damaged

### Week 6: Draft OGL 1.2 Released (January 20)

**January 20**: WotC released draft OGL 1.2 via Creative Commons survey

**Terms**:
- No royalties
- No content ownership by WotC
- No ability to use OGL content in hateful/discriminatory ways
- Still revocable at WotC's discretion
- OGL 1.0a would remain available

**Community Survey**:
- Over 90% opposed OGL 1.2
- Demanded truly irrevocable license
- Wanted Creative Commons instead

### Week 7: The Final Reversal (January 27)

**January 27**: WotC announced complete reversal

**Major Changes**:
- OGL 1.1/1.2 abandoned entirely
- D&D 5.1 SRD released under **Creative Commons Attribution 4.0**
- Creative Commons license is perpetual, irrevocable, and lawyer-tested
- No registration required
- OGL 1.0a remains available for existing content

**Community Victory**:
- Creative Commons better than any OGL version
- Cannot be revoked or changed
- Internationally recognized license
- Complete community win

## Key Issues and Arguments

### Could WotC "De-Authorize" OGL 1.0a?

**WotC's Position**: Section 9 of OGL 1.0a allows updating authorized versions

**Legal Counter-Arguments**:
- OGL 1.0a states license is "perpetual"
- Once granted, cannot be revoked from existing works
- "Authorized" refers to future versions, not retroactive revocation
- Attempting to de-authorize likely breach of contract

**Expert Opinion**: Most legal experts believed WotC couldn't retroactively revoke OGL 1.0a, but litigation would be expensive and uncertain

**Resolution**: WotC eventually stated OGL 1.0a would remain available

### Content Ownership and Royalty-Free Use

**Original OGL 1.1 Term**: WotC could use any OGL 1.1 content for any purpose without compensation

**Community Concern**:
- Publishers' work could be incorporated into official D&D products
- No compensation or attribution required
- Derivative works could compete with original creators
- "Open license" benefiting only WotC

**Comparison**: This clause was opposite of open licensing philosophy

**Resolution**: Removed from subsequent drafts after backlash

### VTT Restrictions

**Original OGL 1.1 Terms**: Virtual tabletops could not use "animated effects" or simulate tabletop experience beyond static representations

**Impact**:
- Would cripple Roll20, Foundry VTT, Fantasy Grounds
- Eliminate dynamic lighting, automation, animations
- Essentially require VTTs to be glorified image viewers
- Obvious attempt to restrict competition with WotC's planned VTT

**Interpretation**: Seen as anticompetitive move to advantage WotC's D&D Beyond VTT

**Resolution**: VTT restrictions removed from later drafts

### "Harmful Content" Provisions

**OGL 1.1 Terms**: WotC could revoke license for content deemed "harmful, discriminatory, or illegal"

**Concerns**:
- WotC as sole arbiter of what's "harmful"
- Could be used to silence criticism
- "Harmful" undefined and subjective
- Political or social content could be targeted
- LGBTQ+ content potentially vulnerable

**Counterpoint**: Preventing actual hateful content is legitimate goal

**Balance**: Creative Commons has no such provisions; relies on existing law

## Community Response

### D&D Beyond Subscription Cancellations

**Scale**: Estimated 40,000-60,000 cancellations in January 2023

**Impact**:
- Direct financial pressure on WotC
- Visible metric of community anger
- Affected WotC's digital strategy
- Demonstrated organized community action

**Long-term**: Some users returned after resolution; others permanently left

### Publisher Coordination

**Major Publishers**:
- **Paizo**: Led ORC License development
- **Kobold Press**: Prepared "Black Flag" OGL-independent system
- **MCDM**: Matt Colville advocated for community rights
- **Chaosium, Legendary Games, others**: Joined ORC consortium

**Small Publishers**:
- Hundreds signed OpenDnD pledge
- Coordinated messaging on social media
- Shared legal resources and information

**Industry Unity**: Rare unified front across competitors

### Content Creator Response

**YouTubers and Streamers**:
- Large channels dedicated videos to controversy
- "OGL Explained" content educated community
- Some (Critical Role) criticized for slow response
- Others (Matt Colville, XP to Level 3) vocal early

**Actual Play Shows**:
- Some announced plans to diversify systems
- Explored non-D&D content
- Community questioned loyalty to WotC vs. audience

**Individual Creators**:
- Many pledged to support OGL 1.0a
- Explored alternative systems
- Built contingency plans

### Social Media Campaigns

**#OpenDnD**: Coordination hub for community response

**#CancelDnDBeyond**: Subscription cancellation campaign

**#DnDBegone**: More aggressive anti-WotC sentiment

**Impact**:
- Kept pressure on WotC visible
- Educated broader community
- Coordinated response
- Made news beyond TTRPG sphere

## Industry Impact

### Immediate Effects

**D&D Alternatives Surge**:
- **Pathfinder 2E**: Paizo reported 500% sales increase
- **13th Age, Shadowdark, OSR games**: Increased visibility
- **Level Up: Advanced 5E**: Gained attention as 5E alternative
- **Indie games**: Broader awareness of non-D&D options

**Publisher Strategies**:
- Accelerated work on OGL-independent systems
- Kobold Press announced Tales of the Valiant (formerly Project Black Flag)
- Others moved to ORC License
- Diversification away from single-system dependence

**VTT Impact**:
- Roll20, Foundry VTT emphasized system-agnostic capabilities
- Increased support for non-D&D systems
- Some developers explored other markets

### Long-term Consequences

**Trust Damage**:
- Permanent wariness of WotC's intentions
- Community skeptical of corporate promises
- Increased interest in community-owned systems
- Lasting reputation damage to WotC brand

**Market Diversification**:
- Players more willing to try non-D&D systems
- D&D's market dominance slightly reduced
- Healthier ecosystem with more viable alternatives
- OSR and indie scenes strengthened

**Licensing Innovation**:
- ORC License establishment as OGL alternative
- Creative Commons adoption for TTRPGs
- Greater attention to license terms community-wide
- Publishers more careful about dependencies

**Corporate Accountability**:
- Demonstrated community can force corporate reversals
- Established precedent for organized response
- Showed importance of open licensing to TTRPG ecosystem
- Cautionary tale for other publishers

## The ORC License

### Development and Structure

**Announced**: January 13, 2023 by Paizo

**Management**: Azora Law (independent nonprofit)

**Design Goals**:
- Truly open and irrevocable
- No corporate control over license terms
- System-agnostic (not just D&D)
- International compatibility
- Protection from bad actors without censorship concerns

**Launch**: June 2023 (finalized after community feedback)

### Support and Adoption

**Founding Publishers**:
- Paizo (Pathfinder, Starfinder)
- Kobold Press (Tales of the Valiant)
- Chaosium (Call of Cthulhu, RuneQuest)
- Legendary Games
- Rogue Genius Games
- Green Ronin
- Many others

**Impact**:
- Viable alternative to OGL
- No single company controls terms
- Can't be revoked or altered by corporation
- Broader than D&D ecosystem

## Creative Commons Solution

### WotC's Decision (January 27, 2023)

**Announcement**: D&D 5.1 SRD released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0

**Why Creative Commons**:
- Internationally recognized license
- Lawyer-tested for decades
- Truly irrevocable
- No corporate control
- Community demanded it

**What Was Released**:
- Core 5E rules mechanics
- Basic classes, races, spells
- Monster stat blocks (SRD creatures)
- DMG basic rules

**What Wasn't Released** (remains Product Identity):
- Specific settings (Forgotten Realms, etc.)
- Product Identity monsters (beholders, mind flayers, etc.)
- Published adventures
- Setting-specific content
- Artwork and trade dress

### Community Reception

**Positive**:
- Complete victory for community
- Better than any OGL version
- Truly irrevocable protection
- International standard

**Lingering Concerns**:
- Smaller SRD than OGL 1.0a provided
- Some creatures and content excluded
- Doesn't address concerns about future D&D editions
- Trust in WotC still damaged

**Practical Impact**:
- Third-party 5E publishing secure
- Retroclones unnecessary for 5E
- VTT platforms unaffected
- Content creators protected

## Lessons and Implications

### For the TTRPG Community

**Community Power**: Organized response can force corporate accountability

**Trust Fragility**: Years of goodwill can be destroyed quickly

**Diversification Value**: Dependence on single system/company is risky

**Open Licensing Importance**: Community values openness and accessibility

**Vigilance Required**: Must watch corporate actions around community resources

### For Publishers

**License Risk**: Dependence on corporate-controlled licenses dangerous

**Community Value**: Respecting community builds loyalty and sales

**Alternative Systems**: Viable to create OGL-independent games

**Transparency Importance**: Secrecy and surprise changes breed backlash

### For Corporations

**Community as Stakeholder**: TTRPG communities aren't just customers

**Open Source Dynamics**: Can't easily close what's been opened

**Social Media Power**: Bad decisions amplified instantly

**Long-term Damage**: Short-term gains not worth trust loss

**Recoverability Limits**: Some reputation damage is permanent

## Conclusion

The OGL 1.1 Controversy was a watershed moment in TTRPG history, demonstrating both the power of organized community action and the fragility of trust between corporations and fan communities. While the immediate crisis was resolved with D&D 5.1's Creative Commons release - a complete community victory - the controversy's effects continue to shape the industry.

The lasting impact includes:
- Permanent diversification of the TTRPG market away from D&D hegemony
- Establishment of ORC License as viable alternative to OGL
- Heightened community awareness of licensing and corporate control issues
- Damaged trust between WotC and significant portion of community
- Cautionary tale for other publishers about respecting open ecosystems

The controversy ultimately strengthened the TTRPG industry by reducing single-point-of-failure dependence on one company's licensing decisions, even as it weakened D&D's cultural dominance. It stands as a reminder that TTRPG communities are not passive consumers but active stakeholders with both power and vigilance.
