# The Oberoni Fallacy

**Time Period**: 2002-present (ongoing)
**Origin**: ENWorld forums, early 2000s
**Key Figures**: Oberoni (forum user), Monte Cook, WotC designers
**Central Question**: Does "you can house rule it" excuse design flaws?
**Resolution Status**: Ongoing philosophical debate
**Historical Significance**: 4/5 - Fundamental design philosophy question affecting industry discourse

## Overview

The Oberoni Fallacy is a term coined on ENWorld forums in the early 2000s to describe a specific pattern of argument used to defend perceived flaws in game design: "If you don't like the rule, change it." The fallacy represents the claim that the ability to house-rule a problem makes criticism of that problem invalid or irrelevant. It crystallizes a fundamental tension in tabletop RPG culture between two competing philosophies: designer responsibility to create functional, balanced rules versus player/DM agency to customize and modify games to suit their tables.

The controversy emerged during D&D Third Edition's heyday when the system's complexity, coupled with its "Ivory Tower" design philosophy (intentionally including trap options), led to vigorous online debates about broken mechanics, overpowered options, and design flaws. Defenders would often respond to criticism with variations of "just house rule it" or "the DM can fix it," prompting pushback from those who believed designers should be held accountable for the rules they published.

The Oberoni Fallacy debate touches on consumer expectations, product quality standards, designer-player social contracts, the purpose of published rules, accessibility for new players, and the fundamental question of what role-playing game books are supposed to provide. It remains relevant today as a framework for evaluating design criticism and understanding different expectations about what published RPG products should deliver.

While named after a single forum user's argument, the fallacy represents a widespread rhetorical pattern that predates its naming and continues to appear in RPG discourse across all systems and editions.

## Background and Context

### The House Rule Tradition

**D&D's Customization Culture**:
- Gary Gygax explicitly encouraged house rules and customization from Original D&D onward
- "The secret we should never let the gamemasters know is that they don't need any rules" - Gary Gygax
- AD&D Dungeon Master's Guide emphasized DM authority and customization
- House ruling seen as integral to RPG hobby
- No two tables play exactly the same way

**Rule Zero**:
- Informal principle: DM has final authority over rules
- Can override, modify, or ignore any published rule
- Predates term "Rule Zero" but always present
- Seen as fundamental to flexibility of tabletop RPGs
- Distinguishes TTRPGs from rigid board games or video games

**Historical Context**:
- Early RPGs often incomplete or ambiguous
- House rules filled gaps in published materials
- Limited published content necessitated DM creativity
- Customization badge of honor and DM skill
- Community-sharing of house rules common

### D&D Third Edition and System Complexity

**3E Design Goals (2000)**:
- Comprehensive, unified rule system
- "A rule for everything"
- Transparency and consistency
- Character building options and tactical depth
- Mathematical precision in game balance

**Unintended Consequences**:
- Complexity created more opportunities for broken interactions
- Rules interactions not fully playtested
- Power disparities between classes and options
- "System mastery" rewarded but created barriers
- Optimization culture exposed imbalances

**Online Communities**:
- ENWorld, Wizards forums became hubs for 3E discussion
- Detailed system analysis and optimization
- Discovery and documentation of broken builds
- Public debate about design flaws
- Designer participation in forums

### "Ivory Tower" Design Philosophy

**Monte Cook's Articulation**:
- Lead 3E designer Monte Cook explained design included intentional "traps"
- Not all options meant to be equally viable
- Players expected to learn system and avoid bad choices
- System mastery curriculum built into design
- Designer acknowledged imbalances intentional

**Community Reaction**:
- Some appreciated transparency and accepted challenge
- Others felt betrayed by intentional trap options
- Debates about designer responsibility
- New player accessibility concerns
- Questions about consumer expectations

**Connection to Oberoni Fallacy**:
- If trap options intentional, is "house rule it" valid response?
- Does design philosophy excuse balance problems?
- Should consumers accept intentionally flawed product?
- Who responsible for player experience: designer or DM?

## The Controversy Erupts

### Origin of the Term

**The Original Argument (c. 2002-2004)**:
- ENWorld forum discussions about 3E/3.5E design flaws
- User "Oberoni" repeatedly responded to criticism with "if you don't like it, change it"
- Pattern recognized by other forum users
- Community began identifying argument as fallacious
- Term "Oberoni Fallacy" coined to describe this rhetorical move

**Why It's Called a Fallacy**:
- The ability to fix a problem doesn't mean the problem doesn't exist
- Shifts responsibility from designer to consumer
- Assumes all groups can/will identify and fix issues
- Devalues published product if extensive revision required
- Dodges legitimate criticism of design quality

**The Fallacy Defined**:
"The assertion that a flaw in a game's rules is not a flaw because the game's rules can be changed; or that identifying a flaw in a game's rules is pointless because the game's rules can be changed."

### Common Forms of the Argument

**Variations**:
- "Just house rule it"
- "The DM can fix it"
- "That's what Rule Zero is for"
- "If you don't like it, change it"
- "My group doesn't have that problem" (implying house rule solution)
- "That's why the DM has final authority"

**Why These Are Considered Fallacious**:
- Deflects from evaluating rule quality
- Assumes problem recognized and fix known
- Burdens DM with designer's job
- Unhelpful to new DMs/players
- Dismisses legitimate criticism
- Lowers standard for published product quality

### Examples in D&D 3E/3.5E Debates

**Linear Fighters, Quadratic Wizards**:
- **Problem**: Caster/martial power disparity increased at higher levels
- **Criticism**: Fighters became less effective relative to wizards
- **Oberoni Response**: "Just give fighters magic items / houserule fighter buffs"
- **Counter**: This admits the problem exists and requires fixing

**Broken Feats and Options**:
- **Problem**: Some feats/spells obviously superior or worthless
- **Criticism**: Trap options waste player choices
- **Oberoni Response**: "Don't allow broken options / ban weak feats"
- **Counter**: Why publish options that need banning?

**Encounter Balance Issues**:
- **Problem**: CR (Challenge Rating) system frequently inaccurate
- **Criticism**: DMs can't trust published encounter guidelines
- **Oberoni Response**: "DMs should adjust encounters for their party"
- **Counter**: This makes CR system useless as published

## Key Arguments

### Pro-House Rule Position

**RPGs Are Customizable By Nature**:
- Customization fundamental to tabletop RPGs
- No ruleset fits all tables perfectly
- House rules always expected and normal
- Distinguishes TTRPGs from other games
- Flexibility is feature, not bug

**DM Authority Is Central**:
- Rule Zero empowers DMs to create best experience
- Published rules are tools, not laws
- DM knows their table better than designer
- Authority to modify rules essential to DM role
- Player buy-in to DM authority expected

**Impossible to Please Everyone**:
- Different tables want different things
- No single ruleset perfect for all groups
- House rules let groups customize to preferences
- Designer can't anticipate every play style
- Modularity allows personalization

**Published Rules Are Framework**:
- Core books provide foundation, not complete solution
- Expected that DMs will modify and expand
- Rules serve as starting point for customization
- Expecting perfect-as-written unrealistic
- Community tradition supports this model

**Critical Engagement Is Good**:
- Identifying flaws and fixing them shows system engagement
- Customization part of creative DM role
- Problem-solving improves DM skills
- Community sharing of fixes benefits everyone
- Active participation better than passive consumption

### Anti-Oberoni Fallacy Position

**Consumer Expectations**:
- Paying customers deserve functional product
- Published rules should work as written
- House rules should be optional, not necessary
- Quality standards matter
- "You can fix it yourself" poor customer service

**Designer Responsibility**:
- Designers paid to create balanced, functional rules
- Professional obligation to deliver quality product
- Testing and refinement designer's job, not consumer's
- Expertise should produce better results than amateur fixes
- Accountability for design decisions important

**Accessibility and Barrier to Entry**:
- New DMs/players can't identify design flaws
- House ruling requires system mastery
- Assuming everyone can fix problems gatekeeping
- Published rules should be playable out of box
- Accessibility matters for hobby growth

**Devaluing the Product**:
- If extensive house rules needed, why buy the book?
- "Just house rule it" admits rules inadequate
- Undermines value proposition of published product
- Fan labor shouldn't be required to make product work
- Sets low standard for publisher accountability

**Deflection of Legitimate Criticism**:
- Valid critiques dismissed without engagement
- Prevents community from pressuring better design
- Shuts down useful discussion
- Protects poor design choices from consequences
- Enables publishers to avoid accountability

**System Matters**:
- Different rulesets create different play experiences
- House rules don't always preserve designer intent
- Extensive modifications create effective homebrew system
- Published system design choices have consequences
- "Just house rule it" ignores that system design matters

**Recognition and Naming**:
- Community quickly adopted "Oberoni Fallacy" terminology
- Used to identify and call out fallacious arguments
- Became shorthand in design discussions
- Spread beyond ENWorld to other forums
- Part of community discourse vocabulary

**Debate Intensification**:
- Naming the fallacy made debates more structured
- Both sides argued whether it truly was fallacious
- Meta-debates about validity of calling it fallacy
- Increased awareness of argument pattern
- More sophisticated discourse about design responsibility

**Designer Participation**:
- WotC designers (Sean K Reynolds, Skip Williams, Andy Collins) participated
- Some defended house rule flexibility
- Others acknowledged design responsibility
- Designers' positions varied
- Public designer-community dialogue on expectations

**Spread to Other Systems**:
- Pattern recognized in non-D&D games
- Pathfinder, 4E, 5E discussions used terminology
- Indie RPG design communities engaged with concept
- Universal applicability across systems

## Industry Response

### Monte Cook's Position

**Design Philosophy Defense**:
- Monte Cook defended Ivory Tower design in essays and interviews
- Argued system mastery valuable and worth pursuing
- Positioned house rules as expected part of engagement
- Maintained designers can't anticipate every table
- Emphasized DM authority as feature of TTRPGs

**Transparency Argument**:
- Acknowledged imbalances in design
- Argued transparency about design philosophy important
- Distinguished between bugs and intentional choices
- Encouraged informed customization
- Positioned house rules as engagement, not failure

**Later Evolution**:
- Monte Cook's later designs (Numenera, Cypher System) emphasized simpler, more flexible frameworks
- Moved away from complex, trap-laden systems
- "GM intrusions" and similar mechanics gave structured DM authority
- Evolution suggested learning from 3E experience
- More explicit about design goals and customization

### WotC and D&D 4E Response

**4E Design Philosophy (2008)**:
- Explicit rejection of Ivory Tower design
- "All options should be viable"
- Tighter mathematical balance
- Clearer guidelines for DMs
- Less reliance on house rules for functionality

**Addressing Oberoni Concerns**:
- 4E positioned as working well out-of-box
- Reduced need for extensive house rules
- More playtesting and mathematical rigor
- Encounter design tools more reliable
- DM guidance clearer and more practical

**Community Reaction**:
- Some appreciated reduced need for fixes
- Others felt over-corrected, too rigid
- "Video gamey" criticism
- Edition Wars complicated reception
- Design philosophy shift acknowledged even by critics

### D&D 5E Approach

**Middle Ground Philosophy**:
- "Rulings, not rules" approach
- Explicit DM empowerment but functional baseline
- Advantage/disadvantage simplifies DM adjudication
- Core rules work as written
- House rules optional enhancement, not necessity

**Addressing Both Sides**:
- Published rules functional and balanced (anti-Oberoni)
- DM authority and customization emphasized (pro-customization)
- "Rule of Cool" and flexibility encouraged
- Bounded accuracy reduces need for complex fixes
- Compromise approach more widely accepted

## Conclusion

The Oberoni Fallacy represents a fundamental and enduring tension in tabletop RPG culture: the balance between designer responsibility and player/DM agency. While customization and house rules are integral to the hobby's DNA, the fallacy identifies when appeals to that tradition become deflections from legitimate criticism of design quality.

The controversy has pushed the industry toward higher standards for published products while maintaining the flexibility and customization that make tabletop RPGs unique. Modern design increasingly tries to satisfy both imperatives: creating functional, balanced rules that work as written while still empowering DMs to customize and adapt to their tables' needs.

The Oberoni Fallacy remains relevant because it articulates consumer expectations in a niche hobby market. Players purchasing RPG products deserve rules that work, explanations that make sense, and designs that deliver what they promise. Acknowledging this doesn't eliminate house rules or DM authority - it simply establishes that "you can fix it yourself" isn't a valid excuse for poor design.

As the hobby has matured, so has understanding of this balance. Different games position themselves differently on the spectrum from "toolkit requiring customization" to "tightly designed experience meant to be played as written." The key is transparency about expectations and delivering on promises. The Oberoni Fallacy serves as a reminder that while house rules are wonderful options, they shouldn't be necessary obligations.

The debate ultimately made the RPG community and industry better: designers more accountable, consumers more articulate in their expectations, and everyone more thoughtful about the relationship between published rules and table practice.
