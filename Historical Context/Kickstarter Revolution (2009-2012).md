# Kickstarter Revolution (2009-2012)

**Date**: April 28, 2009 (Kickstarter launch); 2009-2012 (TTRPG adoption period)
**Historical Significance**: 5/5

## Overview

The launch of Kickstarter in April 2009 and its subsequent adoption by tabletop roleplaying game publishers between 2009-2012 fundamentally transformed the TTRPG industry's economic model, power structure, and creative possibilities. Crowdfunding eliminated traditional barriers to publication, allowed creators to gauge demand before production, enabled ambitious projects impossible under traditional publishing models, and shifted power from distributors and retailers to creators and their direct audiences. The "Kickstarter Revolution" democratized TTRPG publishing, enabling a renaissance of creativity, diversity, and innovation that reshaped the industry's landscape.

The transformation was profound and multifaceted: small publishers could compete with established companies, creators could connect directly with audiences, niche projects could find their audiences, production quality could be determined by community support rather than publisher budgets, and the relationship between creators and consumers became collaborative rather than transactional. Early successful campaigns by Evil Hat Productions (Fate), Monte Cook (Numenera), and Kobold Press (among others) established templates and proved that crowdfunding could support professional-quality TTRPG products. By 2012, Kickstarter had become an essential part of the TTRPG industry's infrastructure, with lasting implications for how games are designed, funded, produced, and distributed.

## Background and Context

### Traditional TTRPG Publishing Model (Pre-2009)

Before crowdfunding, TTRPG publishing operated under a three-tier system with significant barriers:

**Three-Tier Distribution**:
1. Publisher creates product
2. Distributor purchases and warehouses inventory
3. Retailer purchases from distributor and sells to consumers

**Capital Requirements**: Publishers needed substantial upfront capital for writing, art, editing, layout, and printing before seeing any revenue.

**Financial Risk**: Publishers bore complete financial risk—if products didn't sell, they lost their investment.

**Print Run Economics**: Needed to print enough copies to achieve reasonable per-unit costs, but not so many that unsold inventory created losses.

**Distributor Power**: Distributors effectively controlled market access. Without distribution deals, reaching customers was nearly impossible.

**Retail Barriers**: Limited shelf space meant retailers only stocked likely sellers—mostly established publishers and brands.

**Returns and Discounts**: Distributors demanded deep discounts (typically 60% off cover price) and sometimes return rights, squeezing publisher margins.

**Cash Flow Delays**: Publishers often waited months for payment while covering production costs upfront.

**Market Gatekeeping**: This system favored established publishers with capital, distribution relationships, and track records.

### Small Press Challenges

Independent and small publishers faced particular difficulties:

**Capital Access**: Limited access to investment capital or loans for game publishing.

**Distribution Barriers**: Difficulty securing distribution deals without track records.

**Retail Access**: Nearly impossible to get retail shelf space without distribution.

**Production Costs**: Professional-quality production required investments many couldn't afford.

**Marketing Limitations**: Limited marketing budgets meant competing against major publishers was difficult.

**Risk Aversion**: The financial risk discouraged ambitious or experimental projects.

**Market Testing**: No way to gauge demand before committing to production.

These barriers meant many great game concepts never reached publication, and talented designers couldn't enter the industry.

### Print-on-Demand Emergence

Print-on-demand (POD) technology provided some relief:

**Lulu, Lightning Source**: Services enabled printing individual books economically.

**Reduced Risk**: No large print run investment required.

**Limitations**: Higher per-unit costs, limited binding options, quality concerns, and still required upfront costs for development.

**Partial Solution**: POD helped but didn't solve capital, marketing, or distribution challenges.

**Digital Distribution**: DriveThruRPG and RPGNow enabled PDF sales, providing revenue streams but limited audience compared to physical retail.

### Crowdfunding Antecedents

Some pre-Kickstarter crowdfunding existed:

**Patronage/Pre-Orders**: A few publishers used pre-orders or subscription models to fund projects.

**Forum-Based Fundraising**: Small communities sometimes funded projects through forum coordination.

**Limited Success**: These approaches were small-scale, difficult to coordinate, and lacked infrastructure.

**IndieGoGo (2008)**: An earlier crowdfunding platform existed but hadn't gained traction in TTRPGs.

The infrastructure and cultural framework for crowdfunding were emerging but not yet realized.

### Kickstarter's Launch (April 2009)

Kickstarter launched on April 28, 2009, with specific features enabling TTRPG crowdfunding:

**All-or-Nothing Funding**: Projects only funded if reaching their goal—reducing risk for backers.

**Reward Tiers**: Multiple pledge levels with different rewards.

**Platform Infrastructure**: Handled payment processing, project pages, communication.

**Discovery**: Enabled browsing and discovering projects.

**Community Building**: Supported creator-backer interaction.

**Social Sharing**: Integration with social media for viral spreading.

**Categories**: Included "Games" category (later split into "Tabletop Games" and "Video Games").

Initially, Kickstarter focused on arts and creative projects broadly, not specifically gaming.

### Early Adoption (2009-2010)

Early TTRPG Kickstarter campaigns were small and experimental:

**Small Projects**: Initial campaigns had modest goals (few thousand dollars).

**Experimental Approach**: Creators testing whether crowdfunding could work for TTRPGs.

**Limited Awareness**: Most of the TTRPG community wasn't yet aware of Kickstarter.

**Learning Curve**: Creators learned what worked—reward structures, goal setting, communication.

**Building Trust**: Backers needed to trust creators would deliver.

**Few Successes**: Some projects succeeded, proving the model's viability.

## The Event Itself

### Breakthrough Campaigns (2011-2012)

Several campaigns demonstrated Kickstarter's transformative potential:

**Adventurer Conqueror King System (ACKS) by Autarch (2011)**:
- Goal: $4,000
- Raised: $11,648
- Significance: Early OSR success showing niche games could thrive

**Far West by Gareth-Michael Skarka (2011)**:
- Goal: $5,000
- Raised: $49,324
- Significance: One of first major successes (though later became cautionary tale about delayed delivery)

**Artesia: Adventures in the Known World (2011-2012)**:
- Multiple campaigns funding setting and adventures
- Demonstrated serial campaign potential

**Dwimmermount by Autarch/James Maliszewski (2012)**:
- Goal: $10,000
- Raised: $48,554
- Significance: Megadungeon campaign showing appetite for ambitious projects

### Evil Hat's Fate Core (2013 campaign, late 2012 development)

Fred Hicks and Evil Hat Productions became early Kickstarter pioneers:

**Previous Experience**: Evil Hat had run successful campaigns for Dresden Files RPG and other products.

**Fate Core Campaign (2013)**:
- Goal: $3,000
- Raised: $433,365 from 10,103 backers
- Revolutionary success demonstrating the platform's potential

**Innovations**:
- Extensive stretch goals
- Pay What You Want PDF commitment
- Open licensing (OGL and Creative Commons)
- Community involvement in development
- Transparent communication

**Impact**: Established templates other campaigns would follow.

**Fred Hicks' Leadership**: Hicks became vocal advocate for ethical crowdfunding practices, sharing knowledge freely.

### Monte Cook's Numenera (2012)

Monte Cook's Numenera campaign was a watershed moment:

**Context**: Monte Cook was a legendary designer (D&D 3E co-designer, Ptolus, Arcana Evolved) who'd recently left Wizards of the Coast.

**Campaign (August 2012)**:
- Goal: $20,000
- Raised: $517,255 from 4,658 backers
- At the time, the highest-funded TTRPG Kickstarter

**Significance**:
- Proved star designers could self-publish successfully
- Demonstrated crowdfunding could fund ambitious, professional-quality products
- Showed creators didn't need traditional publishers
- Enabled Monte Cook Games as independent publisher

**Production Quality**: Numenera's exceptional quality validated crowdfunding's ability to produce professional products.

**Industry Impact**: Inspired other established designers to consider independent publishing.

### Wolfgang Baur and Kobold Press

Wolfgang Baur (former TSR and WotC designer) pioneered subscription/crowdfunding hybrid approaches:

**Kobold Quarterly**: Had used subscription models successfully.

**Open Design**: Pioneered collaborative design with patron subscribers.

**Kickstarter Adoption**: Brought subscription model experience to Kickstarter.

**Multiple Successful Campaigns**:
- Journeys to the West (2010)
- Midgard Campaign Setting (2011-2012)
- Numerous adventure and supplement campaigns

**Template Creation**: Established models for setting books, adventure compilations, and ongoing support.

### 13th Age by Rob Heinsoo and Jonathan Tweet (2012)

**Designer Pedigree**: Rob Heinsoo (D&D 4E lead) and Jonathan Tweet (D&D 3E co-lead) creating new system.

**Campaign (August 2012)**:
- Goal: $12,000
- Raised: $113,165

**Significance**: Two legendary designers creating independent game proved you didn't need D&D or major publisher backing.

**Pelgrane Press**: Established publisher using Kickstarter for market testing and capital.

**Design Innovation**: The campaign funded genuinely innovative design rather than iteration on existing games.

### Patterns Emerging (2012)

By 2012, clear patterns emerged:

**Goal Setting**: Modest initial goals with extensive stretch goals proved effective.

**Stretch Goals**: Encouraged increased backing and generated excitement.

**Transparency**: Regular updates and open communication built trust.

**Community Involvement**: Backer input and collaboration enhanced projects and loyalty.

**Reward Tiers**: Carefully structured rewards at multiple price points maximized backing.

**Production Quality**: Professional art, layout, and production were expected and achievable.

**Delivery Commitments**: Clear timelines and delivery commitments (though many would struggle with realistic scheduling).

**PDF Early Access**: Providing PDF early while physical production continued.

## Immediate Reactions

### Industry Disruption

Traditional publishers faced new competition:

**Power Shift**: Creators could bypass traditional publishing entirely.

**Distribution Disruption**: Direct-to-consumer reduced distributor/retailer power.

**Capital Democratization**: Crowdfunding provided capital previously requiring publishers.

**Market Testing**: Built-in market validation through funding success.

**Risk Reduction**: Creators received funding before production, shifting risk to shared model.

### Creator Empowerment

Designers and writers gained new opportunities:

**Independence**: Could publish independently without publishers.

**Creative Control**: Retained complete creative control.

**Ownership**: Owned their intellectual property.

**Direct Relationships**: Built direct relationships with audiences.

**Viable Careers**: Crowdfunding enabled full-time independent creative careers.

### Consumer Response

Backers embraced the model enthusiastically:

**Early Access**: Getting products before retail release.

**Exclusive Content**: Kickstarter-exclusive rewards and versions.

**Participation**: Feeling part of creating something, not just buying it.

**Discovery**: Finding niche products matching their interests.

**Community**: Becoming part of communities around projects.

**Support**: Supporting creators and projects they believed in.

### Established Publisher Response

Major publishers had mixed reactions:

**Threat Recognition**: Some saw crowdfunding as threatening traditional models.

**Adoption**: Others embraced it as fundraising and marketing tool.

**Resistance**: Some resisted, believing their brands and distribution were sufficient.

**Hybrid Approaches**: Many eventually adopted hybrid models using both crowdfunding and traditional publishing.

**Onyx Path Example**: White Wolf's successor Onyx Path built entire business model on Kickstarter.

## Short-Term Impact (2012-2014)

### Campaign Size Escalation

Funding amounts grew rapidly:

**2012**: First $500,000+ campaigns (Numenera, Fate Core)

**2013**: Reaper Miniatures' Bones campaign raised $3.4 million—showing tabletop gaming's crowdfunding potential

**2014**: Kingdom Death: Monster raised $2 million (would later raise $12.4 million in 2016 expansion)

**Professionalization**: Campaigns became increasingly sophisticated and professional.

**Expectations Rise**: Both creator and backer expectations increased.

### Small Publisher Renaissance

Independent publishers proliferated:

**New Companies**: Monte Cook Games, Pelgrane Press expansion, numerous small presses.

**Niche Products**: Games serving specific niches found audiences.

**Diversity**: Increased diversity in game styles, settings, and mechanics.

**Innovation**: Experimental and innovative designs found funding.

**OSR Boom**: Old School Renaissance benefited enormously from crowdfunding.

### Production Quality Improvements

Average production quality increased:

**Professional Art**: Crowdfunding enabled hiring professional artists.

**Full-Color Production**: Full-color interiors became achievable for small publishers.

**Premium Components**: Deluxe editions, custom dice, accessories, and premium materials.

**Competitive Quality**: Small publishers could match or exceed major publisher quality.

### Delivery Challenges Emerge

Success brought challenges:

**Underestimated Timelines**: Many creators underestimated production time.

**Scope Creep**: Stretch goals sometimes made projects unmanageable.

**Fulfillment Complexity**: International shipping and reward tier management proved complex.

**Communication**: Keeping backers informed during delays required effort and transparency.

**Cautionary Tales**: Some high-profile project delays damaged crowdfunding's reputation.

### Platform Evolution

Kickstarter evolved to serve tabletop gaming:

**Tabletop Games Category**: Split from general "Games" category.

**Feature Additions**: Tools specifically for tabletop campaigns.

**Discovery Improvements**: Better browsing and discovery for backers.

**Analytics**: Better data for creators.

**Community Features**: Enhanced backer-creator communication.

## Long-Term Consequences

### Permanent Industry Transformation

Crowdfunding permanently changed TTRPG publishing:

**Standard Practice**: Kickstarter became standard for many publishers, not exception.

**Primary Revenue Source**: Some publishers use crowdfunding as primary business model.

**Market Testing**: Standard tool for gauging interest before full production.

**Capital Access**: Democratized access to production capital.

**Risk Management**: Shared risk between creators and backers.

### Direct-to-Consumer Shift

Distribution models shifted:

**Reduced Distributor Dependence**: Creators could reach customers directly.

**Retail Relationship Changes**: Hobby stores had to adapt to Kickstarter-first releases.

**Hybrid Models**: Many products went Kickstarter-first, then retail after backer fulfillment.

**Margin Improvement**: Direct sales improved creator margins dramatically.

**Audience Building**: Crowdfunding built audiences and mailing lists.

### Creative Renaissance

Crowdfunding enabled unprecedented creativity:

**Niche Games**: Games for small but passionate audiences could thrive.

**Experimental Design**: Risky innovative designs could find funding.

**Diverse Voices**: Creators from diverse backgrounds could publish.

**Passionate Projects**: Labors of love found audiences.

**Innovation Acceleration**: Rapid iteration and experimentation became possible.

### Professional Opportunities

New career paths emerged:

**Independent Publishers**: Viable career as independent publisher.

**Freelance Sustainability**: Freelancers could run their own campaigns.

**Full-Time Creators**: More people could work full-time in TTRPGs.

**Specialist Roles**: Artists, cartographers, editors could crowdfund directly.

**Community Managers**: Managing backer communities became a skill/role.

### Power Structure Shift

Industry power dynamics changed:

**Creator Power**: Creators gained power relative to publishers and distributors.

**Community Power**: Backer communities influenced projects and priorities.

**Reduced Gatekeeping**: Traditional gatekeepers lost monopoly on market access.

**Transparency**: Financial transparency became more common.

**Accountability**: Direct creator-backer relationships created accountability.

### Quality and Ethics Questions

Long-term questions emerged:

**Delivery Obligations**: What obligations do creators have to backers?

**Project Management**: Need for better project management and realistic planning.

**Communication Standards**: Expectations for update frequency and transparency.

**Equity**: Is crowdfunding accessible to all or does it favor privileged creators?

**Kickstarter Fatigue**: Market saturation concerns and backer fatigue.

## Key Figures Involved

### Platform Founders

**Perry Chen, Yancey Strickler, Charles Adler**: Kickstarter co-founders who created the platform that transformed creative funding.

### TTRPG Pioneers

**Fred Hicks and Rob Donoghue** (Evil Hat Productions): Early adopters who established best practices and shared knowledge freely. Advocates for ethical crowdfunding.

**Monte Cook**: Proved star designers could self-publish successfully. Numenera's success was watershed moment.

**Wolfgang Baur** (Kobold Press): Brought subscription model experience to Kickstarter. Established templates for settings and adventure campaigns.

**Rob Heinsoo and Jonathan Tweet**: 13th Age demonstrated legendary designers creating independent games.

**James Raggi** (Lamentations of the Flame Princess): OSR publisher who used crowdfunding extensively, showing its utility for niche genres.

**Autarch (Alexander Macris, Tavis Allison)**: Early successful campaigns for ACKS and Dwimmermount.

### Established Designers Going Independent

**Gareth-Michael Skarka**: Far West was early major success (though later controversial for delays).

**Ken Hite, Robin D. Laws** (Pelgrane Press): Established designers embracing crowdfunding.

**Multiple Others**: Dozens of established designers leveraging Kickstarter for independent projects.

## Related Developments

### Reaper Miniatures Bones (2012)

**Campaign**: Goal $30,000; Raised $3.4 million from 17,744 backers

**Significance**: Demonstrated tabletop gaming's crowdfunding potential beyond RPG books.

**Industry Impact**: Showed miniatures and accessories could be crowdfunded successfully.

**Production Scale**: Proved crowdfunding could fund manufacturing-scale production.

### Onyx Path Publishing Business Model

**White Wolf Successor**: Onyx Path acquired White Wolf properties.

**Kickstarter-Based**: Built entire business model on Kickstarter.

**Vampire: The Masquerade 20th Anniversary (2011)**: Early success funding White Wolf legacy product.

**Model Template**: Established model for funding new editions and supplements.

**Ongoing Success**: Continues using Kickstarter as primary funding mechanism.

### PDF Revolution Integration

**Digital Rewards**: PDFs became standard reward tier.

**Early Access**: PDF delivery while physical production continued.

**Print-on-Demand Integration**: DriveThruRPG POD fulfilled some Kickstarter physical rewards.

**Searchable Databases**: Digital versions became standard expectation.

### International Expansion

**Global Backers**: Kickstarter enabled global backer participation.

**Shipping Challenges**: International shipping became major logistics challenge.

**Regional Solutions**: Fulfillment centers and regional partners emerged.

**Translation Funding**: Some campaigns funded translations through stretch goals.

**International Creators**: Enabled creators worldwide to reach global audiences.

### Stretch Goal Evolution

**Mechanics Refined**: Stretch goals evolved from simple to sophisticated.

**Types Developed**:
- Additional content (adventures, chapters, setting material)
- Production upgrades (full color, better paper, hardcover)
- Additional products (maps, accessories, companion books)
- Charity donations
- Unlocked free content for all backers

**Strategic Tool**: Became sophisticated strategic tool for maximizing funding.

## Historical Significance

### Democratization of Publishing

Kickstarter democratized TTRPG publishing more than any previous development:

**Barrier Removal**: Eliminated capital and distribution barriers.

**Access Equality**: Anyone with good ideas and presentation could potentially succeed.

**Diversity**: Enabled diverse creators and perspectives.

**Innovation**: Removed financial barriers to innovation.

**Market Determination**: Communities, not gatekeepers, determined what succeeded.

### Economic Model Transformation

Fundamentally changed TTRPG economics:

**Risk Sharing**: Distributed risk between creators and backers.

**Capital Access**: Provided capital without debt or equity surrender.

**Direct Revenue**: Enabled direct creator-to-consumer transactions.

**Margin Improvement**: Eliminated or reduced distributor/retailer cuts.

**Sustainable Careers**: Made independent creative careers more viable.

### Community-Creator Relationship

Transformed creator-consumer relationships:

**Collaboration**: Backers became collaborators, not just consumers.

**Transparency**: Created expectations for financial and creative transparency.

**Communication**: Established ongoing communication standards.

**Investment**: Backers invested in creators, not just products.

**Community Building**: Projects built communities, not just customer bases.

### Industry Power Restructuring

Permanently altered industry power structures:

**Creator Empowerment**: Creators gained unprecedented power and independence.

**Publisher Disintermediation**: Traditional publishers became optional, not necessary.

**Distributor Decline**: Traditional distribution became less essential.

**Retail Adaptation**: Forced hobby stores to adapt business models.

**Community Influence**: Consumer communities gained influence over what got made.

## Legacy and Modern Relevance

### Industry Standard

Crowdfunding is now standard industry practice:

**Expected Tool**: Most publishers use crowdfunding for some projects.

**Primary Model**: Some publishers use it as primary business model.

**Product Launches**: Major product launches often go through Kickstarter first.

**Market Testing**: Standard tool for testing market interest.

**Capital Source**: Primary capital source for many publishers.

### Ongoing Evolution

The model continues evolving:

**Sophistication**: Campaigns become increasingly sophisticated.

**Professionalization**: Professional campaign management emerges as specialty.

**Platform Alternatives**: BackerKit, Gamefound, and others provide alternatives.

**Late Pledges**: Post-campaign pledge management becomes standard.

**Ongoing Funding**: Some use "always-on" crowdfunding models.

### Challenges and Concerns

Ongoing challenges exist:

**Market Saturation**: Hundreds of campaigns compete for attention.

**Backer Fatigue**: Some backers experience fatigue from constant campaigns.

**Delivery Expectations**: Managing realistic delivery timelines remains challenging.

**Quality Variance**: Quality varies widely between campaigns.

**Accessibility**: Questions about whether crowdfunding favors privileged creators with existing audiences.

**Economic Barriers**: Some creators lack resources for professional campaign presentation.

### Best Practices Established

The industry developed best practices:

**Realistic Timelines**: Learning to set achievable delivery dates.

**Communication Standards**: Regular, transparent updates.

**Stretch Goal Management**: Careful planning to avoid scope creep.

**Fulfillment Planning**: Professional logistics and fulfillment planning.

**Project Management**: Proper project management training and tools.

**Ethical Standards**: Community-developed ethical standards.

### Alternative Platforms

Competition emerged:

**BackerKit**: Late pledge management that became crowdfunding platform.

**Gamefound**: Tabletop-specific platform from Awaken Realms.

**IndieGoGo**: Alternative general crowdfunding platform.

**Patreon**: Subscription-based ongoing funding for creators.

**Platform Diversity**: Multiple platforms serve different needs.

### Integration with Traditional Publishing

Hybrid models emerged:

**Kickstarter First, Then Retail**: Common model for major releases.

**Publisher-Backed Campaigns**: Traditional publishers running campaigns.

**Pre-Order Campaigns**: Using crowdfunding as pre-order system.

**Distribution Partnerships**: Coordinating crowdfunding with traditional distribution.

**Complementary Models**: Crowdfunding complementing rather than replacing traditional publishing.

### Cultural Impact

Beyond economics, crowdfunding affected culture:

**Participatory Culture**: Backers participate in creation process.

**Community Identity**: Backer communities form around projects.

**Transparency Norms**: Expectations for creator transparency.

**Shared Investment**: Communities invest in bringing ideas to life.

**Democratic Creation**: Communities influence what gets made.
