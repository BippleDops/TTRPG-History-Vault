# The Indie Explosion (2008-2015)

## Overview

The Indie Explosion transformed what roleplaying games could be and who could make them. Between 2008 and 2015, independent designers created games that challenged fundamental assumptions about RPG structure, play culture, and creative possibilities. What began as an experimental fringe movement became influential enough to reshape mainstream design thinking and expand gaming's creative boundaries dramatically.

The movement's watershed moment was [[Vincent Baker]]'s [[Apocalypse World]] (2010). This post-apocalyptic game introduced "[[Powered by the Apocalypse]]" (PbtA) mechanics that would influence hundreds of subsequent games. Characters used playbooks—character sheets with thematic moves defining their archetype. Actions triggered moves with structured outcomes: on a 10+, full success; on a 7-9, success with complications; on a 6 or less, GM makes a move (something bad happens). This created "fail forward" gameplay where every roll advanced the fiction dramatically.

The innovation wasn't just mechanical. Apocalypse World's GM section articulated principles and moves structuring how to run games: "play to find out what happens," "be a fan of the characters," "ask provocative questions." These principles codified narrative-focused refereeing that previous games had left implicit. The game's sex moves, focus on scarcity and violence, and mature themes demonstrated that indie games could engage adult subjects seriously.

[[Dungeon World]] (2012) by [[Sage LaTorra]] and [[Adam Koebel]] applied PbtA mechanics to D&D-style fantasy, proving the system could support traditional genres while maintaining narrative focus. Its success brought PbtA to wider audiences and spawned genre-spanning derivatives: [[Monster of the Week]] (horror investigation), [[Monsterhearts]] (teen supernatural romance), [[The Sprawl]] (cyberpunk), [[Urban Shadows]] (urban fantasy politics).

Simultaneously, other design philosophies flourished. [[Fate Core]] (2013) by [[Evil Hat Productions]] refined the [[Fate]] system into a comprehensive toolkit for cinematic narrative gaming. Its aspects, fate points, and collaborative world-building became widely influential. The massively successful Kickstarter ($433,365) demonstrated indie games could rival mainstream products commercially.

[[GM-less games]] proliferated. [[Fiasco]] (2009) by [[Jason Morningstar]] used no GM and minimal dice, creating Coen Brothers-style tales of ambition and poor planning through structured scenes and tables. [[Microscope]] (2011) by [[Ben Robbins]] collaboratively created history across eons without any single authority. These games proved that traditional GM/player structures weren't necessary for compelling play.

**Kickstarter** (launched 2009, mainstream by 2012) revolutionized funding. Creators could gauge interest, fund production, and build audiences before manufacturing. The platform democratized publishing—anyone with an idea could seek funding. Success stories like [[Fate Core]], [[Torment: Tides of Numenera]], and [[Kingdom Death: Monster]] demonstrated enormous potential. The platform also enabled niche products to find sufficient audiences globally.

**Safety tools** became prominent. [[John Stavropoulos]]' [[X-Card]] (2012) provided simple mechanisms for removing uncomfortable content. Later tools like [[Script Change]] and [[Lines and Veils]] followed. These reflected increased awareness of player comfort, consent, and emotional safety—a stark contrast to traditional gaming's "tough it out" culture.

**Emotional engagement** as explicit design goal characterized many indie games. [[Monsterhearts]] (2012) by [[Avery Alder]] explored teenage sexuality, identity, and messy relationships. [[The Quiet Year]] (2013) by [[Avery Alder]] used map-drawing to explore community tension and inevitable collapse. These games created genuine emotional experiences, not just tactical challenges or power fantasies.

The movement was explicitly political. Many designers were queer, feminist, or politically progressive, and their games reflected these perspectives. Representation, consent, emotional safety, and progressive themes weren't afterthoughts but central design concerns. This attracted new demographics while creating backlash from traditional gamers uncomfortable with politics in gaming.

By 2015, "indie games" were established category, not experimental fringe. [[Blades in the Dark]] (2015) by [[John Harper]] synthesized PbtA influences with unique mechanical innovations, becoming one of the era's most influential designs. The movement had proved that revolutionary game design could be commercially viable, critically acclaimed, and culturally significant.

## Cultural and Gaming Context

The Indie Explosion occurred within specific cultural and technological contexts enabling grassroots creative movements and challenging traditional industry power structures.

**Social Media's Rise** transformed communication. [[Twitter]] (2006), [[Facebook]]'s expansion beyond colleges (2006), [[Tumblr]] (2007), and [[Google+]] (2011-2019) created new discussion spaces. Gaming conversations migrated from forums to social media, creating faster-moving, more public discourse. Designers could build audiences directly, engage with fans, and promote work without institutional gatekeepers.

This democratization had complex effects. It enabled marginalized voices to reach audiences directly but also created call-out culture, pile-on dynamics, and visibility for harassment. The indie game community was particularly active on social media, creating both vibrant conversation and occasional toxicity.

**The Story Games Movement** provided intellectual and community infrastructure. The [[Story Games Forum]] (founded 2006) became central discussion hub for narrative-focused design. [[The Forge]] (Ron Edwards' earlier theory site) had established vocabulary and frameworks (GNS theory—Gamist/Narrativist/Simulationist) for discussing design goals. While GNS was eventually considered limiting, it shaped how designers thought about their work.

**Kickstarter Revolution** (launched 2009, mainstream 2012-2013) fundamentally changed funding models. Rather than seeking publishers or self-funding entirely, creators could crowdfund directly from future customers. This validated ideas before production, funded development, and built communities. The platform enabled ambitious projects impossible through traditional publishing.

Early gaming Kickstarters saw spectacular successes. [[Fate Core]] raised $433K. [[Torment: Tides of Numenera]] raised $4.2M. Success stories inspired thousands of campaigns, though many failed or delivered poorly. The platform became essential infrastructure for indie (and eventually mainstream) publishing.

**YouTube Gaming Content** was growing substantially. While actual play wouldn't peak until later, gaming channels were proliferating. [[TableTop]] with Wil Wheaton (2012-2017) brought board and RPG gaming to mainstream audiences, demonstrating how to present gaming engagingly for viewers rather than just players.

**Progressive Politics and Social Justice** movements influenced gaming culture significantly. Increased awareness of representation, diversity, inclusivity, and consent shaped design priorities. [[GamerGate]] (2014) brought these tensions into brutal visibility—while primarily focused on video games, the controversy affected tabletop communities, particularly indie designers who were often explicitly progressive.

**Indie Game Developers (Video Games)** provided parallel inspiration. Games like [[Braid]], [[Limbo]], [[Gone Home]], and [[Papers Please]] demonstrated that small teams could create artistically ambitious, personally expressive games outside AAA game studios. This validated indie tabletop designers' similar aspirations.

**The Rise of Twitch** (2011, gaming-focused 2012+) created livestreaming culture. While actual play streaming wouldn't peak until mid-2010s, the infrastructure for watching people play games as entertainment was being established.

**Web Series Culture** through YouTube created expectations for serialized narrative content. Shows like [[The Guild]] (2007-2013) about MMO players brought gaming culture to mainstream consciousness. This helped normalize geek culture generally.

**Smartphone Ubiquity** and mobile internet meant constant connectivity. Social media, communication, and information access were always available. This accelerated discourse, enabled rapid coordination, and made online communities more immediate.

**The Great Recession's Aftermath** (2008-2009 recession, slow recovery through mid-2010s) affected economics. Traditional employment was uncertain, driving some to creative entrepreneurship. Simultaneously, disposable income constraints made expensive traditional RPGs less accessible, while pay-what-you-want indie games fit tight budgets.

**Rising Awareness of Mental Health** influenced safety tools and emotional engagement. Increased cultural conversation about trauma, anxiety, and psychological wellbeing made content warnings, safety mechanics, and emotional care in gaming seem necessary rather than coddling.

**Feminist and Queer Theory** influenced design explicitly. Designers like [[Avery Alder]], [[Emily Care Boss]], [[Meguey Baker]] brought gender studies, queer theory, and feminist analysis directly into game design. This created games exploring identity, relationships, and power dynamics with theoretical sophistication.

**The Canon Wars in Video Games** about what games could be—whether they should be art, whether they could address serious themes, who they were for—paralleled tabletop indie games' similar assertions. Both mediums were expanding creative ambitions and confronting resistance from traditionalists.

**Reddit's Gaming Communities** provided discussion spaces separate from traditional forums. Subreddits like [[r/rpg]] created spaces for discovery and discussion, though quality and culture varied.

**PDF Markets Maturity** through [[DriveThruRPG]], [[itch.io]] (launched 2013), and others made digital distribution reliable and discoverable. Creators could reach global audiences with minimal barriers.

**Patreon** (launched 2013) enabled ongoing support for creators. Rather than one-time purchases, fans could fund creators monthly, enabling sustainable indie creative careers for some designers.

## Major Releases

**Apocalypse World (2010)** by [[Vincent Baker]] (Lumpley Games) revolutionized RPG design. The post-apocalyptic game introduced "Powered by the Apocalypse" mechanics that would influence hundreds of games. Characters chose playbooks—themed character sheets like The Hardholder, The Battlebabe, The Angel—each with distinctive moves. Actions triggered moves: roll 2d6+stat, with 10+ meaning success, 7-9 success with complications, and 6- meaning GM makes a move (something bad happens).

The game's MC (Master of Ceremonies) section was revolutionary, articulating principles ("play to find out what happens," "be a fan of the characters," "ask provocative questions") and providing structured moves for the MC. This codified narrative refereeing previously left implicit. The game's sex moves, focus on scarcity and violence, and mature themes demonstrated indie games could engage adult subjects seriously.

PbtA became one of indie gaming's most influential systems, spawning hundreds of derivatives across every genre.

**Dungeon World (2012)** by [[Sage LaTorra]] and [[Adam Koebel]] applied PbtA to D&D-style fantasy. Characters were familiar classes (Fighter, Wizard, Thief) but used moves and playbooks. The game maintained fantasy gaming's exploration and combat while adding narrative focus and fail-forward mechanics. Bonds between characters created relationships mechanically.

Dungeon World's accessibility brought PbtA to audiences who might not try experimental post-apocalyptic games. Its success proved PbtA could support traditional fantasy while maintaining narrative innovation. The game spawned numerous derivatives and became gateway to story games for traditional gamers.

**Fate Core (2013)** by [[Fred Hicks]], [[Rob Donoghue]], [[Leonard Balsera]] ([[Evil Hat Productions]]) refined the Fate system into comprehensive narrative toolkit. The game featured aspects (short phrases describing characters, places, or situations), fate points (meta-currency for invoking aspects or refusing compels), and collaborative setting creation. The "+/-/0" Fudge dice created bell curves around competence.

The massively successful Kickstarter ($433,365 from 10,103 backers) demonstrated indie games could rival mainstream products commercially. The game's explicit toolkit approach and open licensing enabled genre-spanning derivatives. [[Fate Accelerated]], the simplified version, became highly popular for pickup games.

**Fiasco (2009)** by [[Jason Morningstar]] ([[Bully Pulpit Games]]) brought GM-less narrative play to accessible form. The game created Coen Brothers-style tales of ambition and poor planning. Players used playsets—collections of tables for relationships, locations, objects, and needs—to establish situation, then played scenes in structured acts, eventually leading to inevitable disaster.

No GM, minimal rules, and scene-focused structure made Fiasco extremely accessible. The game demonstrated that tabletop RPGs could work without traditional authority structures. Its appearance on [[TableTop]] brought it mainstream visibility. Dozens of playsets expanded genres from heist films to corporate comedy.

**Microscope (2011)** by [[Ben Robbins]] ([[Lame Muse Games]]) enabled collaborative history creation across eons without GM. Players defined palette (what's allowed/forbidden), created periods, events, and scenes nonlinearly, exploring history fractal-like from cosmic to intimate scale. The game's structure prevented individual control while enabling coherent collaborative creation.

Microscope demonstrated worldbuilding could be gameplay itself, not just campaign preparation. Its influence appeared in collaborative setting creation mechanics across many subsequent games.

**Monsterhearts (2012)** by [[Avery Alder]] ([[Buried Without Ceremony]]) used PbtA to explore teenage supernatural romance—[[Buffy]], [[Twilight]], teen TV tropes. Playbooks like The Vampire, The Werewolf, The Chosen represented monster-as-metaphor for adolescent identity struggles. The game explicitly engaged sexuality, identity confusion, and messy relationships with mechanical support.

Strings (representing emotional leverage characters have over each other) made relationship dynamics mechanical. Sex moves created consequences for intimacy. The game's frank treatment of sexuality, queerness, and consent made it landmark for representation and emotional engagement in gaming.

**The Quiet Year (2013)** by [[Avery Alder]] created map-drawing game about communities under threat. Players collaboratively build a community knowing it will end when "The Frost Shepherds" arrive. Drawing locations, discussing projects, and resolving conflicts through card-drawn prompts created emergent narratives. Individual player ownership was minimal—the community was collective creation.

The game's melancholy tone, inevitable ending, and focus on small communities facing doom created distinctive emotional experiences. It demonstrated that RPGs could be contemplative and tragic rather than triumphant.

**Lady Blackbird (2009)** by [[John Harper]] was influential free game demonstrating elegant design. The steampunk game featured pre-generated characters with relationship-embedded backstories, key-based advancement, and simple but effective mechanics. Its tight scenario (escaping imprisonment, reaching The Remnants) provided clear initial situation while supporting player-driven continuation.

The game's generosity (completely free), production quality, and elegant simplicity made it widely played and referenced. It influenced how designers thought about scenario design and player-facing keys (goals).

**Blades in the Dark (2015)** by [[John Harper]] synthesized PbtA influences with unique innovations. The game focused on criminal crews in industrial fantasy city Doskvol. Playbooks represented criminal archetypes (The Cutter, The Spider, The Whisper). The game featured position-and-effect judgment for actions, flashback mechanics allowing retroactive planning, and crew development alongside character advancement.

The game's heist structure, elegant mechanical subsystems, and rich setting made it enormously influential. It spawned "Forged in the Dark" derivatives applying its mechanics to other genres. The successful Kickstarter demonstrated continued viability for ambitious indie projects.

**Dread (2005)** by [[Epidiah Ravachol]] gained prominence in this era. The horror game used a Jenga tower instead of dice—players pulled blocks for risky actions, with collapse meaning death. This created physical tension mirroring narrative tension. The game's elegant simplicity and distinctive mechanic made it popular for horror one-shots.

**Monster of the Week (2012)** by [[Michael Sands]] used PbtA for episodic monster-hunting (think [[Buffy]], [[Supernatural]], [[X-Files]]). Mystery structure, hunter playbooks, and Luck mechanic (depleting resource representing plot armor) created genre-appropriate play. The game demonstrated PbtA's flexibility for procedural episodic play.

## Design Trends

The Indie Explosion introduced mechanical innovations and design philosophies that reshaped how designers approached RPG creation.

**Powered by the Apocalypse** became dominant indie framework. Roll 2d6+stat with 10+/7-9/6- outcomes, playbooks, moves triggered by fiction, and MC principles created recognizable system supporting numerous genres. Derivatives included [[Dungeon World]], [[Monsterhearts]], [[Monster of the Week]], [[The Sprawl]], [[Urban Shadows]], and hundreds more. The system's modularity and genre flexibility made it ideal platform for experimentation.

**Playbook Design** from PbtA meant characters were archetypal templates with thematic moves. Rather than building characters from modular parts, players chose complete identities then customized within that framework. This supported genre emulation (playbooks ensured recognizable types appeared) while streamlining character creation.

**Moves and Triggers** formalized mechanics as fiction-triggered procedures. Rather than players declaring actions mechanically ("I attack"), they described fiction ("I charge the warlord with my sword"), which triggered moves if relevant. This "fiction first" approach made narrative primary and mechanics secondary.

**Fail Forward** through partial successes (7-9 results in PbtA) meant every roll advanced situations. Failure didn't mean nothing happened but meant complications, costs, or new problems. This maintained narrative momentum while creating tension.

**GM Principles and Moves** codified refereeing as structured practice. Rather than "rule however feels right," games provided principles ("be a fan of the characters") and moves (structured responses to player actions). This reduced GM burden while maintaining flexibility.

**Aspects and Tags** in Fate and elsewhere made narrative facts mechanically relevant. Descriptive phrases about characters or situations could be invoked for bonuses or compelled for complications. This encouraged vivid description with mechanical weight.

**Fate Points and Meta-Currencies** gave players narrative control. Spending points allowed refusing GM compels, invoking aspects, or asserting narrative facts. This shared authority between GM and players structurally.

**GM-less Play** structures appeared in [[Fiasco]], [[Microscope]], [[The Quiet Year]], and others. Scene framing, turn structures, and procedural prompts distributed traditional GM responsibilities. This proved authority didn't need centralization.

**Collaborative Worldbuilding** made setting creation shared activity. [[Fate]]'s setting creation, [[Microscope]]'s history-building, and many PbtA games' "first session" processes involved all participants defining world details. This created investment while reducing single-author burden.

**Safety Tools** became standard discussion. [[X-Card]], [[Lines and Veils]], and [[Script Change]] provided mechanisms for content negotiation and boundary-setting. This reflected increased awareness of consent, comfort, and emotional safety.

**Emotional Mechanics** in [[Monsterhearts]]' Strings, [[The Quiet Year]]'s Contempt, and similar systems made feelings mechanically relevant. Games explicitly supported emotional engagement rather than leaving it to player roleplay alone.

**Keys and Advancement Triggers** from [[Lady Blackbird]] and [[The Shadow of Yesterday]] tied character advancement to achieving specific goals or expressing values. This made progression about character expression rather than just accumulating power.

**Flashback Mechanics** from [[Blades in the Dark]] allowed retroactive planning. Rather than planning heists in detail, players declared "I prepared for this" and rolled to see if preparation succeeded. This maintained momentum while enabling clever planning.

**Position and Effect** from [[Blades in the Dark]] formalized risk/reward assessment. Rather than fixed difficulty, actions had position (how risky) and effect (potential impact), negotiated based on fiction. This created nuanced judgment framework.

**Clocks and Progress Tracks** appeared across games as visible countdown or progress mechanics. These made complex situations (enemy reinforcements arriving, ritual completion, building trust) mechanically trackable.

**Minimal Dice** characterized many games. [[Fiasco]] used minimal dice. [[Dread]] used Jenga. [[PbtA]] used only 2d6. This reduced barrier to entry while creating distinctive mechanical experiences.

**Structured Scenarios** like [[Fiasco]]'s playsets provided frameworks supporting specific stories. Rather than open-ended campaigns, these games created bounded experiences with clear structures.

**Session-Length Play** was common design goal. Many indie games were designed for single-session or short campaigns rather than years-long play. This fit busy adult schedules while enabling experimental play without long commitment.

## Industry Developments

The Indie Explosion transformed RPG industry structure, funding models, and creator relationships with audiences.

**Kickstarter as Primary Funding** revolutionized publishing. Rather than seeking publishers or self-funding, creators crowdfunded directly from customers. This validated demand before production, funded development, and built communities. Successful campaigns like [[Fate Core]] ($433K), [[Numenera]] ($400K), and [[Kingdom Death: Monster]] ($2M) demonstrated enormous potential.

The platform enabled projects impossible through traditional publishing. It also created new risks—overambitious projects, inexperienced creators, fulfillment challenges, and occasional fraud. Despite problems, Kickstarter became essential indie infrastructure.

**Social Media Marketing** replaced traditional advertising. Designers built audiences on [[Twitter]], [[Google+]], [[Tumblr]], and [[Facebook]]. Direct engagement, playtest sharing, and community building happened publicly. This democratized marketing but required constant social media presence.

**PDF-First Publishing** remained dominant for indie games. [[DriveThruRPG]], [[itch.io]], and direct sales meant global distribution without physical production. Print-on-demand enabled physical versions for successful products.

**Pay-What-You-Want** models appeared frequently. Free or PWYW versions built audiences while premium versions or donations funded creators. [[Lady Blackbird]] was completely free yet highly influential. This generosity built goodwill and accessibility.

**Open Licensing** characterized many successful games. [[Fate Core]]'s OGL, [[PbtA]]'s open licensing, and [[Creative Commons]] releases enabled derivative works. This created ecosystems where third-party creators expanded game lines, benefiting everyone through network effects.

**Patreon Support** (launched 2013) enabled ongoing creator funding. Rather than project-based Kickstarter, Patreon provided monthly income. Designers could fund continuous creation—adventures, supplements, zines—with predictable support.

**Convention Culture** featured indie game presence. [[Gen Con]], [[PAX]], and specialized conventions like [[Big Bad Con]], [[Metatopia]], and [[Indie Game Developer Network]] conferences provided community gathering, playtesting, and visibility.

**The Story Games Codex** and [[Story Games Forum]] provided community infrastructure. Design discussion, playtesting recruitment, and theory development happened in these spaces. They functioned as institutional support for otherwise distributed movement.

**Actual Play Emergence** began during this period, though it wouldn't peak until later. Shows like [[Rollplay]] (2013), early [[Critical Role]] (2015), and [[The Adventure Zone]] (2014) started demonstrating RPG play as entertainment. This created new audiences and changed how games were understood.

**Direct Sales** through creator websites, [[itch.io]], and [[Gumroad]] meant creators could avoid platform fees. This required marketing effort but maximized revenue.

**The Diversity Push** brought increased representation in creators and content. Women, LGBTQ+ designers, and creators of color gained visibility. Games explored diverse themes, settings, and characters. This attracted new demographics while creating backlash from traditionalists uncomfortable with changing demographics and politics.

**GamerGate Spillover** (2014) affected indie designers particularly. The harassment campaign targeting women and progressives in gaming hit several tabletop designers. This created trauma, drove some from public presence, but also galvanized community support for targeted creators.

**Academic Attention** increased. Scholars began studying indie games, designers spoke at conferences, and theoretical frameworks emerged. This legitimized indie games as cultural artifacts worth serious analysis.

**The Forge's Legacy** continued influencing design conversation. While [[Ron Edwards]]' site was less active, vocabulary and frameworks (GNS theory, creative agendas) shaped how designers articulated goals. Though later considered limiting, these frameworks provided common language.

**Small Publisher Sustainability** remained challenging. Few indie designers made full-time livings from games. Most had day jobs, treating design as passionate side projects. This reduced commercial pressure while limiting scale.

**Creator Burnout** from social media demands, community management, and constant project work affected many designers. The expectation of constant engagement and rapid responses created unsustainable pressures.

**International Communities** participated via digital distribution and social media. European, South American, Asian, and Australian indie designers contributed, though most visible conversation remained English-language.

## Legacy and Influence

The Indie Explosion permanently altered RPG design philosophy, expanding what games could be and who could make them.

**PbtA as Design Framework** became one of gaming's most influential systems. Hundreds of games used or adapted its mechanics, making "Powered by the Apocalypse" recognizable brand. The framework demonstrated that innovative systems could spawn ecosystems rivaling traditional games.

**Narrative Mechanics Normalization** made story-focused design respectable. Where narrative games had been fringe, indie success demonstrated their viability. This influenced mainstream design—D&D 5E incorporated narrative elements, and story-focused mechanics appeared widely.

**GM Principles Codification** influenced how games taught refereeing. Rather than assuming experienced GMs would figure it out, games provided structured guidance. This made GMing more accessible and improved actual play quality.

**Safety Tools Adoption** spread beyond indie games. [[X-Card]], [[Lines and Veils]], and similar tools became standard discussion in broader gaming culture. Awareness of consent and emotional safety influenced organized play, conventions, and mainstream games.

**Fail Forward Design** influenced resolution mechanics broadly. The principle that rolls should always advance situations, with failure creating complications rather than dead ends, appeared across game designs.

**Kickstarter as Funding Model** became standard across indie and mainstream publishing. The platform enabled ambitious projects and democratized publishing access. Almost every significant RPG project now uses crowdfunding.

**Playbook Design** influenced character creation across games. The archetype-template approach appeared in non-PbtA games, demonstrating its effectiveness for genre emulation and streamlined creation.

**Emotional Engagement** as design goal became respectable. Games could explicitly support emotional experiences, explore difficult themes, and create feelings beyond tactical satisfaction or power fantasy.

**GM-less Play Viability** was proven. Games demonstrated that traditional authority structures weren't necessary, influencing later designs and showing different play structures could work.

**Fiction First Approach** influenced how games thought about resolution. Starting with narrative description then applying mechanics (rather than declaring mechanical actions) became widespread design wisdom.

**Diversity in Design and Themes** expanded what games portrayed and who made them. Increased representation in creators and content permanently altered the hobby's demographics and concerns.

**Open Licensing Benefits** were demonstrated. Games that opened mechanics for derivative works benefited from ecosystem effects—more games using the system reinforced its position and visibility.

**Session-Length Design** as valid goal influenced game scopes. Not every game needed years-long campaigns—bounded experiences were legitimate design goals.

The Indie Explosion proved that revolutionary game design could be commercially viable, critically acclaimed, and culturally significant. Its influence persists in how games think about narrative, safety, mechanics, and community. The movement demonstrated that gaming's creative boundaries were far wider than previously imagined.
