# OGL 1.1 Controversy (2023)

**Date**: January 5, 2023 (leak published)
**Historical Significance**: 5/5

## Overview

In early January 2023, a leaked draft of a proposed "OGL 1.1" license from Wizards of the Coast triggered the largest community uprising in tabletop roleplaying history. The draft revealed plans to "deauthorize" the original Open Gaming License 1.0a (created in 2000), impose royalties on successful creators, grant Wizards broad rights to community content, and fundamentally restructure the open licensing that had defined D&D's third-party ecosystem for over two decades. The resulting backlash—featuring mass subscription cancellations, coordinated publisher resistance, public statements from industry leaders, mainstream media coverage, and threats of legal action—forced Wizards into an unprecedented retreat, ultimately placing the D&D 5E SRD under Creative Commons licensing.

The OGL 1.1 controversy represents a watershed moment for several reasons: it demonstrated the community's power to resist corporate overreach, validated the importance of open licensing to the TTRPG industry, exposed tensions between Hasbro's corporate interests and D&D's community culture, catalyzed the creation of alternative licenses and systems, and fundamentally altered the relationship between Wizards of the Coast and the D&D community. The crisis's resolution shaped the future of D&D licensing and industry structure for years to come.

## Background and Context

### The Original OGL (2000)

The Open Gaming License 1.0a was created by Ryan Dancey for Dungeons & Dragons Third Edition in 2000:

**Revolutionary Concept**: Allow third-party publishers to create compatible content using D&D's core mechanics (the d20 System).

**Business Theory**: Network effects—more content and publishers supporting D&D would grow the entire market, benefiting everyone.

**Legal Framework**: Clearly defined what was "Open Game Content" (shareable) versus "Product Identity" (protected).

**Perpetual and Irrevocable**: Section 9 stated the license was "perpetual" and could not be revoked for compliant content.

**Industry Impact**: Enabled a boom in third-party publishing during the d20 era (2000-2008).

**Proven Success**: Pathfinder's success using the OGL demonstrated its business value.

### Fifth Edition's OGL Implementation

When Fifth Edition launched in 2014, Wizards eventually released a Systems Reference Document (SRD) under OGL 1.0a:

**Delayed Release**: Unlike Third Edition, the 5E SRD came months after launch.

**Limited Content**: Included basic rules but far less than the 3E SRD—one subclass per class, limited spells, etc.

**DMs Guild Alternative**: Wizards created the DMs Guild platform where creators could sell content using all D&D IP in exchange for revenue sharing and platform lock-in.

**Third-Party Ecosystem**: Despite limitations, thousands of publishers created OGL content for 5E.

**Major Publishers**: Companies like Kobold Press, MCDM, Goodman Games, and many others built businesses on the 5E OGL.

**Economic Scale**: The 5E third-party ecosystem was worth tens of millions of dollars annually.

### Hasbro's Corporate Pressure (2020-2023)

Several factors created pressure for licensing changes:

**D&D's Success**: Fifth Edition's massive growth made D&D one of Hasbro's most valuable properties.

**Undermonetization Perception**: Hasbro executives believed D&D was undermonetized relative to its popularity and cultural presence.

**Third-Party Revenue**: Successful OGL publishers were making money on D&D mechanics without paying Wizards—executives saw lost revenue.

**Digital Future**: Plans for D&D Beyond integration, virtual tabletops, and microtransactions suggested a games-as-service model requiring tighter control.

**Wall Street Pressure**: Hasbro faced investor pressure to increase revenue and profit margins.

**Corporate Culture Clash**: Hasbro's corporate approach increasingly clashed with D&D's community-oriented culture.

### One D&D Development

The OGL controversy occurred during "One D&D" development (the evolution/update of Fifth Edition):

**Backward Compatibility**: One D&D was positioned as compatible with 5E, not a new edition.

**Digital Integration**: Heavy emphasis on D&D Beyond integration and digital tools.

**Playtesting**: Public playtesting began in 2022.

**Licensing Concerns**: Questions emerged about whether One D&D content would use the OGL.

**2024 Release**: Planned for 50th Anniversary of D&D.

### Warning Signs (2022)

Several developments suggested licensing changes were coming:

**Closed Playtesting Language**: One D&D playtesting materials lacked OGL licensing, raising concerns.

**Wizard Statements**: Vague statements about "updating" the OGL.

**Industry Rumors**: Third-party publishers heard rumors about licensing changes.

**Community Concerns**: Forums and social media featured speculation about OGL's future.

**Publisher Uncertainty**: Third-party publishers faced uncertainty planning One D&D products.

## The Event Itself

### The Leak (January 5, 2023)

On January 5, 2023, io9/Gizmodo published an article by Linda Codega revealing a leaked draft of OGL 1.1:

**Source**: An anonymous source provided a draft document to Gizmodo.

**Timing**: The leak was reportedly timed to coincide with Wizards planning to announce the new license.

**Draft Status**: Wizards later claimed this was a draft, not finalized—though evidence suggested it was close to implementation.

### OGL 1.1 Key Provisions

The leaked draft contained several explosive provisions:

**Deauthorization of OGL 1.0a**: Most controversially, OGL 1.1 claimed to "deauthorize" OGL 1.0a, effectively revoking the license most assumed was perpetual and irrevocable.

**Revenue Reporting Requirements**: All OGL users had to report revenue to Wizards, regardless of earnings.

**25% Royalty**: Creators earning over $750,000 annually would owe 25% royalties on revenue above that threshold—an extraordinarily high rate.

**Content Ownership**: Draft language suggested Wizards could use, reproduce, and distribute content created under OGL 1.1—potentially claiming ownership of creator work.

**Virtual Tabletop Restrictions**: Prohibited creating virtual tabletops or using OGL content in VTTs not approved by Wizards—protecting D&D Beyond's planned VTT.

**Registration Requirements**: All OGL users had to register with Wizards.

**Unilateral Modification**: Wizards reserved the right to modify terms unilaterally.

**Kickstarter Reporting**: Required reporting Kickstarter funding before campaigns completed.

### Immediate Community Response

Reaction was swift and overwhelmingly negative:

**Social Media Explosion**: Twitter, Reddit, and other platforms exploded with outrage. #OpenDnD trended.

**YouTuber Coverage**: D&D YouTubers with millions of collective subscribers condemned the proposed changes.

**Publisher Statements**: Third-party publishers publicly opposed OGL 1.1.

**D&D Beyond Cancellations**: Thousands canceled D&D Beyond subscriptions in protest.

**Mainstream Media**: The controversy was covered by mainstream outlets, not just gaming media.

**Legal Analysis**: Lawyers (including industry experts) questioned whether OGL 1.0a could legally be "deauthorized."

### Wizards' Initial Response (January 6-12)

Wizards' initial responses worsened the situation:

**Delayed Statement**: Wizards took days to respond, allowing anger to build.

**Dismissive Tone**: Initial statements minimized concerns and defended the approach.

**"Bad Actors" Framing**: Wizards claimed OGL 1.1 targeted "bad actors" misusing the license—a claim the community rejected as disingenuous.

**No Concrete Changes**: Initial responses didn't commit to substantive changes.

**Community Trust Collapse**: Each inadequate response further damaged trust.

### Publisher Coordination

Major publishers coordinated responses:

**Paizo's Leadership**: Paizo (having fought this battle before with Fourth Edition) led publisher response.

**Open RPG Creative License (ORC)**: Paizo announced development of a new open license as alternative to OGL.

**Publisher Coalition**: Kobold Press, Chaosium, Green Ronin, and many others joined ORC development.

**Coordinated Statements**: Publishers released aligned statements opposing OGL 1.1.

**Paizo's "Subscriptions Speak Louder"**: CEO Lisa Stevens' statement emphasizing economic pressure resonated widely.

### The Escalation (January 13-26)

Pressure on Wizards intensified:

**D&D Beyond Subscriber Exodus**: Estimates suggest 20,000-40,000 subscription cancellations.

**Petition Signatures**: Change.org petition opposing OGL 1.1 gathered over 65,000 signatures.

**Kickstarter Delays**: Creators postponed Kickstarter campaigns, costing Wizards potential licensing revenue.

**Media Coverage**: Washington Post, Bloomberg, and other major outlets covered the controversy.

**Creator Responses**: Matt Colville, Ginny Di, and other prominent creators condemned the proposed changes.

**Investor Concerns**: Some analysts suggested the controversy could damage Hasbro's value.

**Legal Threat**: Paizo and other publishers prepared potential legal challenges to deauthorization.

### Wizards' Reversal (January 27)

On January 27, 2023, Wizards announced a dramatic reversal:

**OGL 1.1 Abandoned**: The proposed OGL 1.1 was scrapped entirely.

**OGL 1.0a Remains**: Acknowledged that OGL 1.0a would remain available.

**SRD Under Creative Commons**: Announced the 5E SRD would be released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0—an irrevocable open license.

**Apology**: Kyle Brink (Wizards' head of D&D) acknowledged mistakes and apologized.

**Community Input**: Committed to community input on future licensing.

### Creative Commons Release (February 2023)

Following through on the commitment, Wizards released the 5.1 SRD under Creative Commons CC-BY-4.0:

**Irrevocable License**: Creative Commons licenses are truly irrevocable—stronger protection than OGL.

**Broad Permissions**: Allowed any use with attribution—more permissive than OGL in some ways.

**Legal Clarity**: Creative Commons licenses have extensive legal precedent and clarity.

**Community Victory**: Widely celebrated as a major victory for the community.

**Unprecedented Action**: Wizards had never placed D&D core content under such permissive licensing.

## Immediate Reactions

### Community Triumph

**Celebration**: The Creative Commons release was celebrated as a major victory.

**Power Demonstration**: The community proved it could resist corporate overreach through coordinated action.

**Trust Damage**: Despite the reversal, trust in Wizards was significantly damaged.

**Ongoing Skepticism**: Many remained skeptical of Wizards' future intentions.

**Economic Impact Awareness**: The community understood its economic power.

### Publisher Relief and ORC Continuation

**Crisis Averted**: Publishers could continue OGL businesses without legal uncertainty.

**ORC Development Continued**: Despite the victory, Paizo and partners continued developing ORC as insurance against future attempts.

**ORC License Released (Summer 2023)**: The Open RPG Creative License 1.0a released, providing a community-controlled alternative.

**Publisher Confidence**: Having an alternative reduced dependence on Wizards' goodwill.

**Industry Resilience**: The episode proved the industry could survive without Wizards' blessing.

### Hasbro Corporate Impact

**Executive Embarrassment**: The public reversal was embarrassing for Hasbro leadership.

**Revenue Impact**: D&D Beyond cancellations directly cost revenue.

**Brand Damage**: The controversy damaged D&D's brand value.

**Investor Concerns**: The mishandling raised questions about Hasbro's digital strategy.

**Leadership Questions**: Some questioned leadership decisions that created the crisis.

### Legal Precedent

**Irrevocability Affirmed**: While not legally tested, the retreat suggested Wizards believed OGL 1.0a couldn't be deauthorized.

**Creative Commons Strength**: CC-BY-4.0 provided stronger protection than OGL ever did.

**Industry Standards**: Established expectations for open licensing in TTRPGs.

**Future Protection**: Made future licensing restrictions much harder.

## Short-Term Impact (2023-2024)

### ORC License Adoption

The Open RPG Creative License became an industry standard:

**Launch (Summer 2023)**: ORC 1.0a released under community governance.

**Broad Adoption**: Major publishers adopted ORC for new products.

**Community Control**: Licensed to a foundation, preventing any single company from controlling it.

**Legal Review**: Extensive legal review ensured robustness.

**Industry Standard**: Became a viable alternative to OGL for many publishers.

### Wizards' Rehabilitation Efforts

Wizards attempted to rebuild trust:

**Community Engagement**: Increased communication and community consultation.

**Product Quality**: Focused on high-quality official releases.

**Licensing Clarity**: Clearer communication about licensing terms.

**One D&D Adjustments**: Adjusted One D&D approach based on feedback.

**Leadership Changes**: Some leadership and structural changes.

### Third-Party Publishing Shift

Publishers adjusted strategies:

**System Diversification**: Many publishers reduced D&D dependency, developing their own systems.

**ORC Adoption**: New products often used ORC instead of OGL.

**Caution**: Wariness about building businesses entirely on Wizards' platforms.

**Alternative Systems**: Increased investment in non-D&D products.

**Kickstarter Recovery**: After initial freeze, 5E Kickstarters resumed with strong performance.

### Community Culture Shift

The controversy changed community dynamics:

**Skepticism**: Increased skepticism toward Wizards' corporate motives.

**Awareness**: Greater awareness of licensing and business issues.

**Activism**: Willingness to organize and protest corporate decisions.

**Alternative Support**: Increased support for non-Wizards products and creators.

**Memory**: The episode became a cautionary tale remembered by the community.

### Industry Structure Evolution

The industry structure shifted:

**Reduced Monopoly**: D&D's market dominance seemed less absolute.

**Publisher Power**: Publishers recognized their collective power.

**Open Licensing Value**: The value of truly open licensing was affirmed.

**Community Governance**: Interest in community-controlled licenses increased.

**Industry Independence**: Greater emphasis on industry independence from any single company.

## Long-Term Consequences

### Permanent Trust Damage

Despite the reversal, long-term trust damage persists:

**Corporate Suspicion**: Ongoing skepticism about Hasbro/Wizards' motives.

**Future Vigilance**: Community remains alert to potential future attempts.

**Relationship Change**: The relationship between Wizards and community fundamentally altered.

**Memory**: The OGL 1.1 attempt will be remembered as a betrayal attempt.

**Reputation Cost**: Wizards' reputation for community partnership was permanently damaged.

### ORC as Industry Infrastructure

The ORC License became permanent industry infrastructure:

**Alternative to OGL**: Viable alternative preventing Wizards' monopoly on open licensing.

**Community Governed**: Foundation governance ensures no single company control.

**Broad Adoption**: Used by major and indie publishers across the industry.

**Legal Robustness**: Extensive legal review and precedent.

**Industry Standard**: Became an accepted standard alongside OGL and Creative Commons.

### Creative Commons D&D Core

The CC-BY-4.0 SRD has lasting implications:

**Permanent Availability**: 5E SRD is permanently and irrevocably available.

**Strongest Protection**: Creative Commons provides stronger protection than OGL.

**Clones and Derivations**: Enables D&D clones and derivatives indefinitely.

**Future-Proofing**: Protects against future licensing changes.

**Precedent**: Sets expectation for how core rules should be licensed.

### Publisher Business Model Changes

Publishers permanently adjusted business models:

**Reduced Dependence**: Consciously reducing dependence on D&D.

**Own Systems**: Increased investment in proprietary systems.

**Diversification**: Product portfolio diversification across multiple systems.

**Platform Caution**: Wariness about building on Wizards-controlled platforms.

**Community Focus**: Emphasis on direct community relationships over platform dependence.

### Industry Pluralism

The controversy accelerated industry pluralism:

**Multiple Major Systems**: D&D, Pathfinder, and other systems all viable.

**System Diversity**: Encouraged development of diverse systems.

**Market Fragmentation**: Less centralized around a single game.

**Healthy Competition**: Multiple competing systems and publishers.

**Innovation**: Diversity encouraged innovation and experimentation.

### Corporate-Community Relations

Changed how companies approach community relations:

**Community Power**: Demonstrated community economic and organizational power.

**Transparency Value**: Showed value of transparency in licensing and business decisions.

**Trust Fragility**: Demonstrated how quickly trust can be damaged.

**Listening Importance**: Emphasized importance of listening to community.

**Goodwill Value**: Quantified the economic value of community goodwill.

## Key Figures Involved

### Wizards of the Coast / Hasbro

**Kyle Brink**: Head of D&D during the controversy. Became public face of the response and eventual reversal. His apology and commitment to Creative Commons release were crucial to resolution.

**Cynthia Williams**: President of Wizards of the Coast. Made strategic decisions about licensing approach and reversal.

**Chris Cocks**: Hasbro CEO. Corporate pressure for increased monetization likely drove initial OGL 1.1 push.

**Dan Rawson**: Senior Vice President at Wizards. Involved in licensing decisions.

### Community Leaders

**Ryan Dancey**: Original OGL creator (no longer at Wizards). Publicly discussed OGL's legal status and history, providing crucial context.

**Matt Colville**: Popular D&D YouTuber and publisher (MCDM). His videos condemning OGL 1.1 reached millions. Led by example in encouraging D&D Beyond cancellations.

**Ginny Di**: D&D content creator whose video explanations helped broader audiences understand the controversy.

**Professor DM (Dungeon Craft)**: YouTuber who consistently covered the controversy, keeping pressure on Wizards.

### Publisher Leaders

**Lisa Stevens** (Paizo CEO): Led publisher response. Her statement "subscriptions speak louder than surveys" encapsulated the economic pressure strategy. Spearheaded ORC development.

**Jeff Grubb, Wolfgang Baur** (Kobold Press): Major third-party publisher leaders who joined ORC coalition and publicly opposed OGL 1.1.

**Jason Tondro**: Paizo Senior Designer who publicly discussed the issues with authority and clarity.

**Multiple Publishers**: Leaders from Chaosium, Green Ronin, Legendary Games, and others joined coordinated response.

### Media and Legal

**Linda Codega** (io9/Gizmodo): Journalist who published the original leak, breaking the story.

**Various Legal Experts**: Attorneys who analyzed the OGL's legal status, including whether it could be "deauthorized."

**Mainstream Journalists**: Writers from Washington Post, Bloomberg, and other outlets who brought mainstream attention to the controversy.

## Related Developments

### One D&D Branding Change

Following the controversy, "One D&D" branding was quietly de-emphasized:

**Return to Edition Language**: Materials began referring to "2024 Core Rulebooks" rather than "One D&D."

**Brand Damage**: The One D&D brand became associated with the OGL controversy.

**Marketing Shift**: Shift toward emphasizing continuity with 5E rather than new platform.

**Community Preference**: Community preferred traditional edition framing.

### Virtual Tabletop Competition

The VTT restrictions in OGL 1.1 highlighted VTT importance:

**D&D Beyond VTT**: Wizards' planned VTT faced skepticism post-controversy.

**Competitor Strength**: Roll20, Fantasy Grounds, Foundry VTT, and others strengthened positions.

**Market Fragmentation**: VTT market remained fragmented rather than consolidating under Wizards.

**Open Alternatives**: Interest in open-source VTT solutions increased.

### Third-Party System Development

The scare accelerated proprietary system development:

**MCDM RPG**: Matt Colville's company accelerated development of their own system.

**Level Up: Advanced 5th Edition (A5E)**: EN Publishing's advanced 5E variant gained attention.

**Draw Steel**: MCDM's forthcoming system as complete D&D alternative.

**Publisher Independence**: Multiple publishers began developing systems to reduce D&D dependence.

### DMs Guild Reconsideration

The controversy prompted reconsideration of DMs Guild:

**Platform Lock-In**: Recognition of risks in platform-exclusive content.

**Revenue Split**: Questions about whether 50% revenue split was fair.

**Ownership Concerns**: Concerns about content ownership on the platform.

**Alternative Platforms**: Increased use of itch.io, DriveThruRPG, and direct sales.

### Community Organization

The controversy demonstrated community organization power:

**Coordinated Action**: Successful coordination across platforms and communities.

**Economic Leverage**: Effective use of subscription cancellations and purchase boycotts.

**Information Sharing**: Rapid information dissemination and education.

**Sustained Pressure**: Maintaining pressure over weeks until achieving results.

**Replicable Model**: Created template for future community actions.
