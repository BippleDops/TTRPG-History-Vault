# Rifts

**Publisher**: [[Palladium Books]]
**Designer**: [[Kevin Siembieda]]
**Year**: 1990
**System**: Palladium Megaversal System (Mega-Damage)
**Genre**: post-apocalyptic, science fiction, fantasy, kitchen-sink multi-genre
**Status**: in-print

## Overview

Rifts, designed by Kevin Siembieda and published by Palladium Books in 1990, represents one of gaming's most audacious and polarizing creations—a "kitchen-sink" post-apocalyptic setting where magic, technology, dinosaurs, giant robots, dragons, vampires, psychics, aliens, demons, and gods coexist in chaotic fusion of every genre imaginable. Set 300 years after magical apocalypse destroyed modern civilization and reopened dimensional barriers, Rifts Earth became crossroads where infinite realities collided, creating setting where techno-wizards piloted mecha while dragon hatchlings fought alongside cyborg warriors against Coalition States' fascist armies and alien invaders.

The game emerged from Palladium's successful Robotech RPG (1986) and long-running Palladium Fantasy RPG (1983), combining both properties' strengths into original setting. Siembieda envisioned post-apocalyptic Earth transformed by returning magic—ley lines (mystical energy channels) crisscrossed the planet, and where ley lines intersected, dimensional Rifts opened randomly, dumping creatures, civilizations, and technology from infinite parallel worlds. This premise justified literally anything appearing: Japanese samurai, Atlantean crystal technology, Aztec gods, vampiric intelligences, Tolkienesque elves, anime-style mecha, cosmic horrors, and more.

Rifts' mechanical innovation was the Mega-Damage system—introducing damage scale where normal weapons were useless against armored targets. A Mega-Damage point equaled 100 normal damage points, meaning unarmored humans died instantly to weapons designed for mecha combat, while power armor and robots shrugged off small arms entirely. This created tactical environment where equipment choice determined survivability fundamentally. Players needed Mega-Damage protection (power armor, magic, cybernetics, or supernatural toughness) to survive, while enemies ranged from human-scale threats to kaiju-sized monsters and  miles-long fortresses.

The Coalition States provided central antagonist—fascist human supremacist nation-state controlling much of former American Midwest and South, employing advanced technology while suppressing literacy (preventing magic use), persecuting D-Bees (dimensional beings—all non-humans), and maintaining military dictatorship. Coalition's iconic skull-faced SAMAS power armor and Dead Boy soldiers became setting's visual identity, representing humanity's xenophobic response to transformed world.

Rifts achieved extraordinary commercial success throughout 1990s and 2000s, becoming Palladium's flagship property. The game spawned over 100 supplements exploring Earth's regions (Coalition States, Atlantis, Russia, China, Africa, South America, Space, Underwater civilizations) and connected dimensions (Rifts: England, Phase World, Wormwood). The sheer volume of content created one of gaming's most extensively detailed—if bewilderingly complex—settings.

The game's cultural impact was substantial despite—or perhaps because of—its maximalist "everything plus the kitchen sink" approach. Rifts demonstrated that genre fusion could succeed commercially, that power-gaming fantasy appealed to significant audience, and that consistent world-building across dozens of books could sustain devoted fanbase. It influenced how designers thought about campaign settings, power scaling, and genre mixing.

However, Rifts also became infamous for mechanical complexity, balance problems, and editorial sprawl. The Palladium system's d20+bonuses resolution required constant modifier calculation. Character classes varied wildly in power—Glitter Boy pilots commanded walking tank armor, while Vagabonds scavenged with no particular advantages. Combat against high-MDC enemies could last hours. The setting's anything-goes philosophy made tonal consistency challenging.

Despite these criticisms, Rifts maintained devoted following. Players who loved its specific combination—tactical mecha combat, kitchen-sink setting, power fantasy, extensive lore—found nothing else quite like it. The game became cultural touchstone in gaming—everyone had opinion about Rifts, whether celebrating its ambitious scope or criticizing its execution.

## Historical Context

In 1990, Palladium Books was established publisher known for Palladium Fantasy RPG (1983), Heroes Unlimited (1984), and especially Robotech RPG (1986). Robotech's success demonstrated that mecha anime aesthetics appealed to American audiences and that Palladium's house system could support complex combat and equipment. However, Robotech's license came with restrictions—Palladium couldn't fully control the property or exploit it indefinitely.

Kevin Siembieda, Palladium's founder and lead designer, wanted original property combining his company's strengths: the mechanical complexity and combat focus of Robotech, the fantasy worldbuilding of Palladium Fantasy, and the multi-genre flexibility of Heroes Unlimited. He conceived Rifts as post-apocalyptic setting justifying unlimited genre mixing—magical catastrophe and dimensional rifts meant anything from any reality could appear.

The premise brilliantly solved creative and commercial problems. Creatively, Rifts' anything-goes setting meant Siembieda could publish supplements exploring any concept without continuity concerns. Want anime mecha? Phase World sourcebook. Want Lovecraftian horror? Dimension Book Two: Phase World Sourcebook. Want cyberpunk? Chi-Town 'Burbs. Want underwater kingdoms? Rifts Underseas. Want African mythology? Rifts Africa. The dimensional rifts premise justified all of it canonically.

Commercially, this meant virtually unlimited supplement potential—every new book explored different genre or region, preventing market exhaustion. Palladium could chase trends, explore staff favorites, or respond to fan requests without continuity restrictions. This strategy proved extraordinarily successful—Rifts received more supplements than almost any non-D&D property.

The game's development drew on diverse influences. Post-apocalyptic settings were popular in 1980s (Gamma World, Twilight: 2000, After the Bomb). Mecha anime had influenced Robotech and BattleTech. Fantasy gaming dominated through D&D. Superhero comics explored power escalation. Horror gaming thrived with Call of Cthulhu and World of Darkness. Siembieda synthesized these influences into comprehensive setting where all coexisted.

Rifts Earth's backstory explained the transformation. In 2098 CE, magical apocalypse called "Coming of the Rifts" destroyed modern civilization. Nuclear strikes and massive death released psychic energy that reactivated Earth's dormant ley lines—channels of mystical power. Where ley lines intersected, dimensional Rifts opened, connecting Earth to infinite parallel dimensions. Magic returned, ancient races reawakened, and creatures from countless realities poured through Rifts.

Humanity survived in scattered communities, but three centuries of chaos, warfare, and dimensional incursions transformed Earth beyond recognition. Alien civilizations established kingdoms. Atlantis rose from ocean floor as continental superpower. Supernatural predators hunted freely. Technology and magic evolved in parallel, creating techno-wizards, power armor sorcerers, and cyber-knights. By 2290s (default campaign date), Earth was dangerous frontier where survival required power—Mega-Damage weaponry, magical ability, or supernatural nature.

The Coalition States emerged as dominant human power in former North America. Founded by survivors who blamed magic and non-humans for apocalypse, the Coalition built fascist state emphasizing technology, military power, and human supremacy. They controlled population through propaganda, suppressed literacy (literate people could learn magic), and waged war against D-Bees, practitioners of magic, and anyone challenging their authority. Coalition's Dead Boy soldiers (cybernetically enhanced troops in skull-faced power armor) became iconic imagery.

Palladium published Rifts core rulebook in 1990 with striking painted cover showing Coalition SAMAS power armor fighting dragons and demons—immediately communicating the setting's "everything at once" approach. The 250-page rulebook included character creation, Mega-Damage rules, equipment, world overview, and sample adventures. Production values were mixed—evocative but inconsistent art, dense text layout, occasional typos—but content fascinated.

Critical reception was divided. Some reviewers praised the setting's ambition and detail. Others criticized mechanical complexity and balance issues. Commercial reception was unambiguously positive—strong sales justified immediate expansion. Palladium published supplements rapidly: Sourcebook One (1991), Conversion Book (1991, adapting other Palladium games), Coalition War Campaign (started 1996), and region-specific sourcebooks.

The supplement line became extraordinary in scope. By 2000s, Rifts had over 70 core books exploring every continent, multiple dimensions, underwater civilization, orbital communities, and more. World Books detailed Atlantis, Africa, England, China, Japan, Russia, and more. Dimension Books explored Phase World (galactic civilization), Wormwood (living planet), and other realities. Coalition sourcebooks covered the fascist state exhaustively. This created daunting but impressively comprehensive setting.

Rifts attracted particular audience—players who wanted power fantasy, complex equipment systems, detailed worldbuilding, and tactical combat. The game became especially popular among younger male audiences drawn to robots, weapons, and tactical gameplay. Convention games showcased elaborate miniature battles using Rifts rules.

The game's longevity proved remarkable. While many 1990s games faded, Rifts maintained consistent publication and sales. Palladium released Rifts Ultimate Edition (2005) updating and reorganizing rules while maintaining compatibility. New sourcebooks appeared regularly through 2010s and 2020s, demonstrating sustained viability.

However, Rifts also became cautionary tale about complexity and sprawl. The game's mechanical overhead frustrated many players. The setting's scope became barrier—new players faced dozens of books, unclear canon status, and overwhelming options. The Palladium system's idiosyncrasies (skill percentiles, saving throws, MDC, attribute bonuses) created learning curve. This limited audience growth despite dedicated fanbase.

## Core Mechanics

Rifts used Palladium's house system (Megaversal System) with Mega-Damage expansion:

**Character Creation**: Players selected Occupational Character Class (OCC)—profession defining abilities. Options included:

- **Men-at-Arms**: Combat specialists (Coalition Soldiers, SAMAS pilots, Glitter Boy pilots, Mercenaries, Headhunters, Cyber-Knights)
- **Practitioners of Magic**: Magic users (Ley Line Walkers, Mystics, Techno-Wizards, Shifters)
- **Psychics**: Psionic characters (Mind Melters, Bursters, Mind Bleeders)
- **Adventurers & Scholars**: Skill-focused classes (City Rats, Operators, Rogues, Vagabonds)
- **Racial Character Classes**: Species with inherent abilities (Dragons, Adult Dragons, Crazies)

Each OCC provided specific skills, abilities, equipment, and advancement path. Power variance was notorious—Glitter Boy pilots started with nigh-invulnerable power armor and devastating rail guns, while Vagabonds started with basic equipment and scavenging skills.

**Attributes**: Eight attributes (IQ, ME, MA, PS, PP, PE, PB, Spd) rated numerically, typically 3-24. High attributes provided bonuses to skills, combat, and saves. Extraordinary/Superhuman/Supernatural strength categories applied different damage scales.

**Skills**: Percentile-based competencies (starting around 25-65%, advancing with levels). Characters selected skills from OCC lists and secondary skill selections. Skill checks rolled d100 attempting to roll under skill percentage. This mixed with MDC's scale-based combat created mechanical dissonance.

**Mega-Damage System**: The defining innovation. Damage came in two types:

- **SDC (Structural Damage Capacity)**: Normal damage for unarmored targets, structures, vehicles. Humans had SDC and Hit Points.
- **MDC (Mega-Damage Capacity)**: Armor and weapons for serious combat. 1 MDC point = 100 SDC points.

This meant:
- Normal firearms dealt SDC—useless against MDC armor
- Energy weapons and heavy weapons dealt MDC—instantly killing unarmored humans
- Power armor, robots, and monsters had MDC—impervious to normal weapons
- Combat divided into MDC-scale (serious battles) and SDC-scale (street-level conflict)

The system created harsh barrier: characters needed MDC protection or died instantly in serious combat. This mandated equipment dependence for survivability.

**Combat**: Turn-based initiative system with attacks per round determined by class and level. Combat involved:

1. Roll initiative (d20 + bonuses)
2. Actions in initiative order
3. Attack rolls: d20 + strike bonuses vs. target's dodge/parry
4. Damage rolls if hit
5. Resolve damage against MDC/SDC

**Saving Throws**: When exposed to hazards (magic, poison, psionic attacks), characters rolled d20 + bonuses attempting to meet target number (typically 12-16+). Success negated or reduced effects.

**Magic**: Ley Line Walkers and mages cast spells spending PPE (Potential Psychic Energy)—magical resource pool. Spell effects ranged from utility (See the Invisible, Chameleon) to combat (Call Lightning, Fireball) to transportation (Rift Teleportation). Spells at ley line nexus points received power bonuses. Magic provided versatility but required memorization and resource management.

**Psionics**: Psychics spent ISP (Inner Strength Points) activating powers. Abilities included telepathy, telekinesis, pyrokinesis, and more. Psionics competed with magic for mystical character niche.

**Equipment**: Extensive catalogs of weapons, armor, vehicles, and gear. Energy weapons (lasers, particle beams, ion blasters) dealt MD, making them vastly superior to conventional firearms. Power armor (SAMAS, Glitter Boy, Triax) provided MDC protection and enhanced capabilities. Combat vehicles ranged from hoverbikes to flying fortresses.

**Ley Lines and Rifts**: Magical energy lines crisscrossed Earth. At nexus points (intersections), dimensional Rifts opened. Practitioners gained bonuses near ley lines and could tap power for enhanced spells. Rifts allowed dimensional travel—plot device for literally anything appearing.

**Experience and Advancement**: Characters earned experience defeating enemies, completing missions, and good roleplaying. Accumulating XP increased level (typically 2000-4000 XP per level). Levels improved combat bonuses, skills, PPE/ISP, and sometimes granted new abilities.

The mechanical philosophy prioritized tactical combat and equipment management over streamlined resolution. Rifts rewarded system mastery—players who optimized character builds and equipment loadouts gained significant advantages. This appealed to tactical gamers but frustrated those preferring narrative focus or simplified rules.

## Setting and Themes

**Rifts Earth (circa 109 PA - Post-Apocalypse, 2290s CE)**:

**North America**:

- **Coalition States**: Fascist human supremacist empire controlling former Midwest/South. Centered on Chi-Town (futuristic fortress-city), with satellite states Missouri, Lone Star, and Free Quebec. Employed advanced technology, suppressed literacy and magic, persecuted D-Bees. Dead Boy soldiers in iconic skull-faced SAMAS power armor enforced control. Coalition represented humanity's xenophobic militaristic response to transformed world.

- **Tolkeen**: Magic-using kingdom in Minnesota opposing Coalition. Major campaign setting for Coalition Wars storyline where Coalition invaded and destroyed Tolkeen despite heroic resistance.

- **Federation of Magic**: Loose alliance of magic practitioners opposing Coalition from southern territories.

- **Lazy Wolf Clan and Native American Nations**: Various tribes reclaiming ancestral lands using magic and technology.

- **Northern Gun**: Corporate manufacturer of weapons and armor—neutral arms dealer.

- **Ishpeming/Bandito Arms**: Competing equipment manufacturers.

- **Vampire Kingdoms**: Mexico overrun by vampiric intelligences and their minions—deadly threat.

**Global Locations** (explored in sourcebooks):

- **Atlantis**: Risen continent ruled by evil Splugorth (alien intelligence) enslaving dimensions. Source of rune weapons and bio-wizardry. Major power and adventure location.

- **England**: Neo-Victorian society fighting demon plague. Fog shrouded island with gothic horror elements.

- **Russia**: Warlords, mystical traditions, and ancient threats. Harsh wasteland with competing factions.

- **China**: Resurgent empire blending tradition and technology. Dragon kings and celestial bureaucracy.

- **Japan**: Republic of Japan using tech and supernatural allies against demons. Anime-influenced mecha and samurai themes.

- **Africa**: Diverse kingdoms, primal magic, phoenixes, and ancient civilizations. Source of millennium trees and powerful magic.

- **South America**: Inca/Aztec gods returned, competing empires, dangerous jungles filled with supernatural threats.

- **Rifts Underseas**: Underwater kingdoms, lemurians, biowizardry, marine threats.

**Dimensional Locations**:

- **Phase World**: Galactic civilization—space opera setting with multiple spacefaring races, Cosmo-Knights (space paladins), and intergalactic conflicts. Connected to Rifts Earth through dimensional rifts.

- **Wormwood**: Living planet-dimension locked in eternal battle between Light and Dark. Gothic horror meets bio-organic technology.

- **Palladium World**: Connection to Palladium Fantasy RPG setting.

- **Countless Others**: Dimensional rifts meant infinite parallel worlds could appear, justifying any concept.

**Key Factions**:

- **Coalition States**: Human supremacist military dictatorship
- **Cyber-Knights**: Psionic warriors using techno-wizard weapons, defending innocents
- **Tolkeen (destroyed)**: Tragic magic kingdom annihilated by Coalition
- **Triax Industries**: German corporate state producing advanced power armor and weapons
- **New German Republic**: Government reclaiming territory from gargoyle hordes
- **Splugorth Slavers**: Atlantean alien intelligence enslaving dimensions for profit
- **Xiticix**: Insectoid hive mind expanding aggressively—existential threat
- **Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse**: Supernatural beings periodically appearing as major threats

**Themes**:

- **Survival in Hostile World**: Rifts Earth was dangerous—characters needed power to survive.
- **Genre Mixing**: Magic and technology, fantasy and sci-fi, horror and adventure coexisted.
- **Power Fantasy**: Characters wielded extraordinary capabilities—piloting mecha, casting reality-altering magic, possessing supernatural powers.
- **Moral Complexity**: Coalition was fascist antagonist yet included heroic soldiers. Magic users included heroes and villains. D-Bees ranged from peaceful refugees to monstrous threats.
- **Exploration and Discovery**: Dimensional rifts meant unknown wonders and terrors constantly appeared.
- **Faction Conflict**: Coalition vs. Magic Users, Atlantean Slavers vs. Freedom Fighters, Xiticix expansion, etc.
- **Adaptability**: Surviving required using whatever worked—magic, tech, psionics, alliances.
- **Human Identity**: What defined humanity in world transformed by magic and filled with dimensional beings?

The setting's maximalist approach meant anything could appear. This created incredible creative freedom and occasional tonal whiplash when serious drama collided with absurdist excess.

## Cultural Impact

Rifts' cultural influence extended beyond commercial success:

**Genre Fusion Pioneer**: Demonstrated comprehensive multi-genre settings could succeed commercially. Influenced later kitchen-sink games and genre-blending properties.

**Mega-Damage System**: The MDC mechanic influenced discussions about damage scaling, equipment dependence, and power tiers in RPGs. Later games exploring similar concepts (Exalted's overwhelming damage, Warhammer 40K RPG toughness tiers) acknowledged Rifts' precedent.

**Power Fantasy Gaming**: Validated extreme power-gaming—mecha pilots, dragon characters, dimension-traveling wizards. Showed significant audience wanted high-power campaigns.

**Supplement Line Scope**: Over 100 books created one of gaming's most extensively detailed settings. Demonstrated both potential and pitfalls of exhaustive documentation.

**Coalition States Iconography**: The skull-faced Dead Boy imagery became recognizable even among non-players. Coalition's aesthetic influenced subsequent military sci-fi.

**Commercial Viability Without System Streamlining**: Proved complex, idiosyncratic games could sustain audiences despite mechanical barriers to entry.

**Convention Gaming**: Rifts became convention staple for elaborate tactical battles using miniatures, showcasing the hobby's wargaming roots.

**Community Creativity**: Extensive fan materials, conversions, and homebrew demonstrated engaged community. Online forums maintained vibrant Rifts discussion decades after peak popularity.

**Worldbuilding Ambition**: Inspired designers to pursue comprehensive setting development rather than shallow frameworks.

**Cautionary Tale**: Also became example of complexity, balance problems, and editorial sprawl—lessons about moderation, playtesting, and accessibility.

**Influence on Video Games**: Elements of Rifts appeared in games exploring post-apocalyptic genre fusion (Fallout's retro-futurism, Borderlands' sci-fantasy mixing).

**Major Sourcebooks** (selection from 100+ books):

- **Sourcebook One** (1991): Expanded equipment, classes, world info
- **Conversion Book** (1991): Adapting Palladium games to Rifts
- **Coalition War Campaign** (1996-2000): Epic multi-book campaign

- **Siege on Tolkeen** (2000-2001): Coalition's conquest campaign

**Ongoing Support**: Palladium continued publishing Rifts sourcebooks through 2010s and 2020s, with Northern Gun, Black Market, Sovietski, Disavowed, and others expanding setting.

The sheer supplement volume created comprehensive setting and significant barrier—new players faced overwhelming content with unclear starting points.

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Ambitious, detailed setting
- Compelling genre fusion
- Extensive equipment and options
- Tactical combat depth
- Creative freedom
- Longevity and consistent support
- Passionate fanbase

**Criticism**:
- Mechanical complexity and learning curve
- Severe balance problems between classes
- MDC system's harsh equipment dependence
- Palladium system's idiosyncrasies
- Editorial sprawl and inconsistent canon
- Combat slowness against high-MDC enemies
- Barrier to entry from overwhelming content
- Production value inconsistencies

**Community Division**:
- **Fans**: Loved setting scope, tactical depth, power fantasy
- **Critics**: Considered it bloated, unbalanced, mechanically dated

**Legacy Assessment**:

Rifts succeeded commercially and created devoted community despite significant mechanical and editorial issues. It demonstrated that ambitious worldbuilding and genre mixing appealed strongly to specific audience willing to tolerate complexity. The game became both exemplar of comprehensive setting development and cautionary tale about mechanical accessibility.

## Legacy and Influence

Rifts' legacy encompasses both achievements and lessons:

**Achievements**:
- Proved multi-genre kitchen-sink settings commercially viable
- Created one of gaming's most extensively documented worlds
- Pioneered damage scaling approaches (MDC system)
- Sustained 30+ year publication run
- Cultivated devoted fanbase

**Lessons**:
- Mechanical complexity limits audience growth
- Balance matters even in power fantasy games
- Editorial sprawl creates barriers to entry
- Production value consistency affects perception

**Ongoing Relevance**:

Rifts remains available and supported, maintaining niche audience appreciating its unique combination. The game influenced designers thinking about genre mixing, power scaling, and worldbuilding scope while providing cautionary examples about complexity management.
