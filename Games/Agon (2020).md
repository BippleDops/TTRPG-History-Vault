# Agon (Second Edition)

**Publisher**: [[Evil Hat Productions]]
**Designer**: [[John Harper]], [[Sean Nittner]]
**Year**: 2020
**System**: Agon System
**Genre**: fantasy, mythological, competitive-cooperation
**Status**: in-print

## Overview

Agon (Second Edition), released in 2020 by Evil Hat Productions, represents the complete redesign of John Harper's 2006 original, transforming a beloved but mechanically rough gem into one of the most elegant heroic fantasy games ever created. Co-designed with Sean Nittner, the game casts players as Greek demigods—children of the Olympian gods—sailing from island to island in the Aegean, undertaking heroic quests to earn kleos (glory) and secure their legendary status. The game's central tension emerges from its competitive-cooperation structure: heroes work together to overcome challenges while simultaneously vying to become the greatest hero of the age.

The second edition maintains the original's thematic brilliance while completely overhauling the mechanical framework. Where the 2006 version felt experimental and sometimes clunky, the 2020 edition achieves mechanical elegance rivaling Harper's later masterwork Blades in the Dark. The game introduces the strife mechanic (resource management creating dramatic tension), contest resolution (elegant opposed challenge system), and structured island exploration (episodic adventure framework) that transform Greek heroic adventure from interesting concept to smooth, compelling gameplay.

The competitive-cooperation dynamic distinguishes Agon from traditional cooperative RPGs. Heroes genuinely compete—one player will be declared greatest hero. Yet they must cooperate to survive divine trials, defeat monsters, and complete quests. This creates fascinating social dynamics where players celebrate each other's successes (needed for collective victory) while jockeying for individual glory. The game mechanically supports this tension through glory-earning opportunities, divine favor mechanics, and the final reckoning where heroes' deeds are tallied.

The game's episodic structure—sail to island, face trials, earn glory, move to next island—creates perfect pacing for campaigns. Each island is self-contained adventure lasting 2-4 sessions, preventing narrative drag while building toward epic conclusion. The island generator and quest templates enable game masters to create new content quickly or improvise sessions with minimal preparation, making Agon remarkably easy to run despite its thematic richness.

Agon achieved significant critical and commercial success upon release. The Kickstarter campaign (2019) raised over $108,000, far exceeding its goal. The game won multiple awards including the 2021 ENnie Gold for Best Game and Best Writing. More importantly, it demonstrated how to modernize classic game concepts—the 14-year gap between editions allowed Harper and Nittner to apply lessons from contemporary narrative game design to heroic fantasy gaming.

## Historical Context

The original Agon (2006) emerged during the indie RPG revolution's early phase, when designers were experimenting with narrative authority distribution, player-driven content, and non-traditional resolution mechanics. John Harper, influenced by The Forge's narrativist theories and games like Sorcerer and Dogs in the Vineyard, designed Agon to explore competitive cooperation and glory-seeking as primary character motivations.

The 2006 edition innovated conceptually but struggled mechanically. The core ideas—demigod heroes competing for glory, island-hopping adventures, Greek mythological setting—resonated strongly. However, the execution felt incomplete. The competitive elements sometimes overshadowed cooperation; pacing could drag; character advancement felt uneven. The game garnered cult following among indie gamers but never achieved mainstream breakthrough.

Between 2006 and 2020, John Harper's design skills evolved dramatically. Lady Blackbird (2009) demonstrated his mastery of concise, elegant design. Blades in the Dark (2017) showcased sophisticated mechanical frameworks supporting specific fictional experiences. These developments, combined with Evil Hat's interest in publishing polished indie designs, created opportunity for Agon's complete redesign.

Sean Nittner brought complementary expertise to the collaboration. As experienced game master, designer, and Evil Hat's project manager, Nittner understood both practical play concerns and production realities. His involvement ensured the redesign addressed the original's playability issues while maintaining thematic integrity.

The 2019 Kickstarter campaign framed the project as "returning to Agon" rather than simply updating it. Harper and Nittner emphasized they were creating the game Agon always wanted to be—mechanically elegant, thematically rich, and practically playable. The campaign's transparency about design process and commitment to quality attracted both original Agon fans and players discovering the concept freshly.

Development involved extensive playtesting across different groups, particularly focusing on pacing, competitive balance, and game master ease. The playtest materials circulated publicly, incorporating community feedback. This open development process built anticipation while refining mechanics through real play experience.

The game's release in 2020 coincided with renewed interest in Greek mythology driven by popular media (Hades video game, Lore Olympus webcomic, Song of Achilles novel). This cultural moment primed audiences for Greek heroic adventure, though Agon's approach—focused on demigod protagonists, episodic quests, and glory-seeking—offered fresh perspective distinct from mainstream adaptations.

Evil Hat's production values elevated the final product. The hardcover book features beautiful illustration, clear layout, and comprehensive examples. The physical quality matches the mechanical elegance, creating complete package worthy of the fifteen-year development journey from original concept to polished second edition.

## Core Mechanics

Agon's mechanics elegantly support competitive-cooperation gameplay and Greek heroic themes:

### Core Resolution: Name and Epithets

Characters are defined by their Name (their divine heritage and core identity) and Epithets (heroic qualities earned through deeds). When facing challenges, players frame their approach using their Name and relevant Epithet, then roll dice pools.

**Example**: Alexios, Son of Athena, might invoke his Epithet "The Clever" when solving a riddle, or "Shield of His Companions" when defending allies.

This framing requirement ensures players constantly reinforce their heroic identity through mechanical choices, not just narrative description.

### Dice Pools and Divine Favor

Challenge resolution uses d6, d8, and d10 dice pools:
- Characters roll their Name die (typically d6 or d8)
- Plus Epithet die (d6, d8, or d10 depending on Epithet strength)
- Plus Domain die (based on challenge type: Battle, Grace, Resolve, Spirit, Truth)
- Optionally spend Divine Favor for additional dice

Take the two highest dice and sum them—this is your total. Higher rolls succeed more impressively.

**Divine Favor**: Characters begin with three Divine Favor tokens (representing their god-parent's blessing). Spending Favor adds additional dice to pools, enabling heroic success when stakes are highest. Favor refreshes between islands, creating resource management: spend freely to succeed dramatically or conserve for crucial moments?

### Contests: Competitive Resolution

Many challenges are Contests where multiple heroes attempt the same task (who climbs the cliff fastest? who impresses the oracle most?). Each hero rolls their pool:
- Highest total wins the contest and earns Glory
- Others may still succeed (reaching cliff top) but don't earn Glory
- Ties split Glory equally

This creates the core competitive-cooperation tension: you want allies to succeed (helps collective mission) but you want to succeed better (earns individual Glory).

### Strife: The Tension Mechanic

Heroes have four Strife boxes. When you make a roll and your lowest die shows 1, 2, or 3, mark Strife equal to that number. When all four Strife boxes are marked, your hero is Spent—they can no longer contribute their Name or Epithet dice to rolls, severely limiting effectiveness.

Strife represents exhaustion, minor injuries, divine disfavor, and mounting challenges. It creates escalating tension: early in adventure, heroes perform mighty deeds easily. As Strife accumulates, they become vulnerable. This drives dramatic pacing—easy beginning, challenging middle, desperate climax.

**Clearing Strife**: Heroes can orate (tell story of their deeds), rest, or achieve dramatic victories to clear Strife, but opportunities are limited. Managing Strife becomes crucial strategic consideration.

### Pathos: The Fallibility Mechanic

When your highest die shows 4 or less, you earn Pathos—reminder that even demigods face setbacks. Pathos can be:
- Spent immediately to add +1 to your total (turning potential failure into marginal success)
- Saved to help overcome Strife later
- Used to invoke divine aid at crucial moments

This transforms low rolls from pure failure into dramatic opportunities: do you accept setback to build toward future comeback, or spend Pathos to barely succeed now?

### Glory: The Victory Condition

Heroes earn Glory through:
- Winning Contests (defeating rivals in challenges)
- Completing Quest objectives
- Performing Mighty Deeds
- Defeating Worthy Foes
- Earning Divine Boons

Glory accumulates throughout campaign. At campaign's conclusion, the hero with most Glory is declared Greatest Hero of the Age. This provides clear victory condition while allowing all heroes to achieve legendary status.

### Island Exploration Structure

Each island adventure follows structured format:
1. **Arrival**: Heroes reach island, learn of troubles
2. **Trials**: Face challenges revealing quest nature
3. **Wagers**: Heroes make personal commitments to quest
4. **Quest**: Pursue main objective through challenges
5. **Contest of Wits**: Final test before confrontation
6. **Climax**: Face the Antagonist
7. **Aftermath**: Resolve island's fate, earn rewards

This structure provides pacing framework while remaining flexible for creative content. Game masters know what components to include; how they manifest varies per island.

### Island Generator

The game provides extensive random tables for generating islands:
- Island type and features
- Mortal populace and their troubles
- Divine influence present
- Antagonist type and motivation
- Quest structure and objectives
- Treasures and boons available

These tables enable quick island creation or improvisation, making preparation straightforward.

### Vows and Bonds

Heroes can make Vows (personal commitments to quests) earning bonus Glory if fulfilled. Bonds represent relationships between heroes, providing mechanical benefits when supporting bond-mates but creating vulnerability if bonds break. These systems encourage developing character relationships and personal stakes beyond simple Glory accumulation.

### Advancement and Epithet Growth

Heroes don't level up traditionally. Instead:
- They earn new Epithets through mighty deeds
- Existing Epithets can grow stronger (d6 → d8 → d10)
- They acquire divine boons and treasures
- Their reputation and legend grow

This creates advancement through narrative achievement rather than numerical progression, appropriate for heroic fantasy where deeds define greatness.

## Setting and Themes

Agon assumes setting inspired by Greek mythology but doesn't require canonical accuracy:

### The Aegean: Island-Hopping Campaign

Heroes sail from island to island across the wine-dark sea. Each island presents self-contained adventure—troubles to resolve, monsters to defeat, glory to earn. The islands can be mythological Greece, fantasy archipelago, or any island-based setting fitting Greek heroic themes.

This episodic structure creates natural campaign arc: heroes undertake series of heroic labors, building toward legendary confrontation or divine ascension.

### Demigod Heroes: Children of Gods

Player characters are explicitly demigods—children of Olympian gods (Zeus, Athena, Poseidon, Apollo, Artemis, Ares, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Hermes, Dionysus, Hades). Their divine heritage grants supernatural capabilities while keeping them mortal and vulnerable.

Divine parentage influences:
- Character's Name die (inherited power)
- Relationship with divine parent
- Conflicts with parent's rivals
- Access to divine boons and aid

Heroes can petition their divine parent for aid, though such intervention comes with strings attached.

### Glory (Kleos): The Heroic Currency

Greek heroism fundamentally concerns kleos—glory, renown, imperishable fame. Heroes don't seek wealth or power for its own sake but to achieve legendary status, earning place in songs and stories eternal.

Mechanically, Glory provides clear metric. Thematically, it drives heroes toward ever-greater challenges and dramatic deeds. The game asks: what price will you pay for immortal fame?

### Tragedy and Triumph: Heroic Duality

Greek heroes embody contradiction—capable of magnificent deeds and catastrophic failures, often simultaneously. Agon's Pathos mechanic captures this: your setbacks aren't merely failures but dramatic moments revealing character, building toward eventual triumph or tragic downfall.

The game encourages playing heroes as flawed, ambitious, driven—not perfect paragons but complex individuals pursuing glory despite personal failings.

### The Aegean Pantheon: Active Gods

The Olympian gods aren't distant creators but active, involved, petty, and powerful beings meddling in mortal affairs. Divine parents help favored children, divine rivals create obstacles, gods make demands and offer boons.

This creates gameplay where divine politics matter. Heroes navigate relationships with gods, earn or lose favor, and become pawns in immortal conflicts.

### Fated Doom: Inescapable Destiny

Optional rule framework allows incorporating Greek fate—heroes know their doom but struggle against it anyway. This adds tragic dimension where greatest heroes' deaths are foretold yet they pursue glory regardless.

### Community and Companions

While heroes compete for greatest glory, they sail together, face challenges cooperatively, and form bonds. The game celebrates both individual excellence and camaraderie—you can't succeed alone (cooperative) but can't share ultimate glory (competitive).

## Cultural Impact

**Competitive-Cooperation Design**: Agon popularized mixed-motive gameplay where players simultaneously cooperate and compete. This influenced subsequent designs exploring player competition within cooperative frameworks.

**Glory-Seeking Mechanics**: The game demonstrated how mechanical systems could incentivize specific character motivations (glory-seeking) creating emergent roleplaying rather than imposed characterization.

**Island-Crawl Structure**: The episodic island format influenced campaign structures in other games, providing alternative to traditional hex-crawls or dungeon-delves.

**Heroic Fantasy Innovation**: Showed heroic fantasy could explore themes beyond D&D's adventurer-focused gameplay, embracing mythological heroism's competitive, glory-driven nature.

**Indie Design Maturation**: The fourteen-year gap between editions demonstrated indie designers could return to earlier works with new skills, creating definitive versions rather than abandoning imperfect originals.

**ENnie Gold Success**: Winning Best Game and Best Writing at 2021 ENnies validated Agon's design excellence and Evil Hat's editorial support.

**Critical Acclaim**: Reviewers praised elegant mechanics, thematic coherence, and how systems support intended experience—making it design case study.

**Actual Play Presence**: Regular appearances on actual-play streams demonstrated game's spectator appeal—the competitive elements create narrative tension in recorded format.

**Greek Mythology Gaming**: Renewed interest in Greek mythological RPGs, showing market existed for settings beyond Tolkien-inspired fantasy.

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Elegant mechanical systems perfectly supporting competitive-cooperation
- Strife and Pathos creating dramatic pacing naturally
- Island structure providing excellent campaign framework
- Clear, beautiful production values
- Genuine improvement over beloved original
- Thematic coherence between mechanics and Greek heroism
- Accessible rules while maintaining depth
- Glory-seeking creating emergent character motivations

**Criticisms**:
- **Narrow Setting Focus**: Greek mythology specificity limits appeal to players wanting other settings
- **Competition Concerns**: Some groups uncomfortable with player competition, preferring pure cooperation
- **GM Preparation**: Island generation, while easier than many games, still requires creativity and preparation
- **Campaign Length**: Episodic structure sometimes makes long-term arcs challenging
- **Character Death**: While not as lethal as OSR games, heroes can die, frustrating some players
- **Glory Imbalance**: Some scenarios allow certain heroes to dominate Glory earning
- **Divine Favor Management**: Resource tracking sometimes slows play

**Community Response**:
- Strong following among indie gamers
- Active Discord community sharing island designs
- Numerous actual-play recordings
- Fan-created content expanding setting options
- Discussion of adapting mechanics to other mythologies

**Comparison to Other Heroic Games**:
- More focused than D&D's broad fantasy
- More competitive than most PbtA games
- Less lethal than OSR games
- More mechanically elegant than many heroic fantasy options
- Different feel from traditional quest-based adventures

## Legacy and Influence

**Design Excellence Recognition**: Established Agon as exemplar of tight thematic-mechanical integration, studied by designers learning craft.

**Competitive-Cooperation Framework**: Influenced games exploring player competition within cooperative structures, validating mixed-motive gameplay.

**Glory-Seeking Mechanics**: Inspired advancement systems based on pursuing specific character goals rather than accumulating experience points.

**Episodic Campaign Structures**: The island-crawl format influenced how designers think about campaign pacing and adventure structure.

**Harper's Evolution**: Demonstrated Harper's continued design excellence post-Blades, showing his range beyond heist gaming.

**Evil Hat's Curation**: Validated Evil Hat's strategy of publishing polished indie designs, adding Agon to their catalog of design-forward games.

**Mythological Gaming**: Helped establish market for mythology-based RPGs, showing players interested in cultural specificity over generic fantasy.

**Second Edition Success**: Proved complete redesigns could succeed, encouraging designers to revisit and improve earlier works rather than abandoning them.
