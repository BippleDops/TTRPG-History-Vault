# Dream Askew / Dream Apart

**Publisher**: [[Buried Without Ceremony]]
**Designers**: [[Avery Alder]] (Dream Askew), [[Benjamin Rosenbaum]] (Dream Apart)
**Year**: 2018
**System**: Belonging Outside Belonging
**Genre**: Queer Community Building, Historical Jewish Community
**Status**: in-print

## Historical Context

Dream Askew / Dream Apart, published together as collaborative project in 2018, represents watershed moment in queer gaming and GM-less design. Designed by Avery Alder (Dream Askew) and Benjamin Rosenbaum (Dream Apart), the paired games introduced the "Belonging Outside Belonging" framework—revolutionary approach to GM-less play using tokens to distribute narrative authority while exploring marginalized communities building homes in hostile worlds. Dream Askew portrayed queer enclave surviving post-apocalypse; Dream Apart depicted Jewish shtetl navigating pogroms and upheaval in early 20th century Eastern Europe. Both games centered experiences of communities existing precariously, finding belonging despite systems designed to destroy them.

Avery Alder, already established through Monsterhearts and The Quiet Year, brought queer perspective and design innovation to exploring how LGBTQIA+ communities create spaces of safety, intimacy, and resistance in apocalyptic contexts. The game imagined queer enclave after societal collapse—not despite apocalypse but because of it, finding freedom in ruins where heteronormative structures failed. This wasn't escapist fantasy but grounded exploration of how queer communities have always created homes in margins and disasters, building families and societies that straight/cis world denied them.

Benjamin Rosenbaum's Dream Apart explored parallel themes through Jewish historical experience. The shtetl—small Jewish town in Eastern Europe—existed precariously between pogroms, discrimination, and cultural erasure, yet sustained vibrant community, rich tradition, and deep belonging. Rosenbaum, drawing on Jewish heritage and extensive research, created game exploring how marginalized communities maintain identity and connection despite existential threats.

Publishing these games together wasn't accidental. Both explored belonging outside belonging—finding community and home precisely because mainstream society excludes you. Both mechanized the precariousness of marginalized existence. Both centered joy, intimacy, and connection alongside danger and loss. The pairing demonstrated that queer and Jewish experiences of marginalization, while distinct, shared structural similarities that Belonging Outside Belonging framework could explore.

The framework itself proved revolutionary. Rather than traditional GM distributing and controlling narrative authority, Belonging Outside Belonging distributed authority through Setting Elements—roles representing different aspects of community and world:

**Dream Askew's Setting Elements:**
- **The Outrider**: Scouts and scavengers
- **The Stitcher**: Medical care and body modification
- **The Hawker**: Trader and resource manager
- **The Torch**: Visionary and spiritual guide
- **Threats**: External dangers, psychic maelstrom, hostile forces

**Dream Apart's Setting Elements:**
- **The Outside**: Non-Jewish world's pressures
- **The Spirit**: Supernatural and mystical forces
- **The Tradition**: Orthodox religious structures
- **The Messiah**: False messiahs and radical movements
- **Rumors**: Gossip, news, and fears

Each player took one Character and one Setting Element, creating scenes from both perspectives. This distributed narrative control while ensuring world-building emerged collaboratively.

The token economy replaced dice entirely. Characters had Moves they could perform normally, but using Moves created vulnerability—requiring spending tokens to avoid "weak Moves" (complications). Tokens came from playing Setting Elements, creating beautiful reciprocity: you gained narrative power by portraying challenges and world elements, using that power to protect your character when needed. This created dynamic where everyone benefited from introducing complications and everyone needed to engage with world-building.

The games included no conflict resolution mechanics in traditional sense. Instead, characters made declarations about what they attempted; other players (through Setting Elements or character reactions) introduced complications; the table collaboratively determined outcomes through conversation and token expenditure. This required trust and communication but created organic, consensual storytelling impossible with dice and rigid resolution.

Both games emphasized queerness explicitly. Dream Askew's enclave was unapologetically queer—trans people, non-binary folk, same-sex relationships, polyamory, chosen family—as default rather than exception. Dream Apart, while historical, explored Jewish queerness, gender nonconformity in traditional communities, and how marginalized groups include further margins within.

The 2018 release achieved immediate critical acclaim. The games won Ennie Awards for Best Game and Innovation, demonstrating mainstream recognition of queer-focused, GM-less design. They inspired numerous Belonging Outside Belonging hacks exploring different marginalized communities—disabled communities, Indigenous resistance, immigrant enclaves, neurodiverse collectives—proving the framework's versatility.

Dream Askew / Dream Apart stands as crucial achievement in queer gaming and innovative mechanics. The games proved that centering marginalized experiences could create mechanically sophisticated, emotionally resonant designs. They demonstrated that GM-less play could work elegantly through thoughtful authority distribution. They showed that exploring precarity, marginalization, and community-building created compelling gameplay. Most importantly, they gave queer and Jewish players games seeing them, their histories, their communities, their struggles for belonging.

## Mechanical Innovations

Dream Askew / Dream Apart introduced revolutionary Belonging Outside Belonging framework:

**No Game Master**: Neither game uses traditional GM. Instead, narrative authority distributes through Setting Elements that all players engage with. This creates truly collaborative storytelling without centralized control.

**Token Economy Replaces Dice**: Rather than rolling dice to determine outcomes, players use tokens to manage narrative power:
- **Starting Tokens**: Begin with two tokens
- **Earning Tokens**: Play Setting Element scenes to earn tokens
- **Spending Tokens**: Use tokens to perform "strong Moves" (asserting outcomes) rather than "weak Moves" (inviting complications)

This creates reciprocity—you gain power by introducing world elements and challenges, using that power to protect your character.

**Character and Setting Element Dual Roles**: Each player has:
- **One Character**: Personal protagonist with relationships, moves, and vulnerabilities
- **One Setting Element**: Aspect of world/community they portray

This ensures everyone engages with both personal story and world-building.

**Dream Askew Characters:**
- **The Iris**: Psychic seer navigating visions
- **The Gatekeeper**: Protector managing enclave security
- **The Housemaker**: Community builder and caretaker
- **The Skinner**: Body modifier and flesh artist
- **The Emissary**: Diplomat navigating external relationships
- **The Arrival**: Newcomer learning community

Each has Moves reflecting their role in queer enclave.

**Dream Askew Setting Elements:**
- **The Outrider**: Scouts, scavengers, external threats
- **The Stitcher**: Medicine, modification, transformation
- **The Hawker**: Trade, resources, economic pressures
- **The Torch**: Vision, spirituality, inspiration
- **Threats**: Hostile forces, psychic maelstrom, danger

**Dream Apart Characters:**
- **The Devout**: Religious scholar and tradition keeper
- **The Rebel**: Questioner challenging norms
- **The Shmendrik**: Well-meaning fool causing problems
- **The Matchmaker**: Relationship facilitator
- **The Apostate**: One who left community, now returned
- **The Visitor**: Outsider entering shtetl

**Dream Apart Setting Elements:**
- **The Outside**: Gentile world's pressures and violence
- **The Spirit**: Supernatural, mystical, divine
- **The Tradition**: Orthodox structures and expectations
- **The Messiah**: False messiahs, radical movements
- **Rumors**: Gossip, fears, information

**Moves Without Dice**: Characters have Moves they perform, but instead of rolling:
- **Strong Move**: Spend token to assert successful outcome
- **Weak Move**: Don't spend token, outcome includes complication or vulnerability
- **No Wrong Moves**: Either succeeds with cost or succeeds cleanly, never pure failure

This removes failure states while maintaining dramatic tension.

**Scene Framing**: Any player can frame scenes:
- **Character scenes**: Personal moments, relationships, challenges
- **Setting Element scenes**: Introducing world elements, complications, opportunities

This distributed scene authority ensures everyone shapes narrative.

**Questions as Mechanics**: Both games include question lists prompting exploration:
- Questions about community relationships
- Questions about personal history
- Questions about world state
- Questions creating complications

Answering questions develops characters and world organically.

**Collaborative World-Building**: Before play, group answers questions establishing:
- Community specifics
- Key locations
- Important NPCs
- Current threats and challenges
- Relationships between characters

This invests everyone in setting from start.

**No Stats or Numbers**: Characters have no numerical ratings. Competence comes from narrative positioning and token expenditure, not attribute scores.

**Intimacy Mechanics**: Both games include frameworks for portraying intimacy, vulnerability, and connection—central to communities sustaining themselves through hardship.

**Safety Integration**: Explicit safety tools including content negotiation, X-Card, and boundary-setting, acknowledging games deal with marginalization, trauma, and violence.

## Design Philosophy

Dream Askew / Dream Apart embodied sophisticated design philosophy about community and belonging:

**Belonging Through Exclusion**: Both games explored how marginalized communities create belonging precisely because mainstream society excludes them. This sophisticated understanding recognized that margins create connections mainstream cannot.

**Joy and Struggle**: Rather than portraying marginalization as pure suffering, both games balanced struggle with joy, intimacy, celebration, and resistance. This wholeness reflected actual marginalized experiences.

**Community as Protagonist**: While players controlled individual characters, the community itself was collective protagonist. Success meant community surviving and thriving together, not individual achievement.

**Distributed Authority**: The framework's token economy and Setting Element distribution embodied philosophy that everyone shares storytelling responsibility. No one controls narrative; everyone contributes.

**Precarity as Condition**: Both games mechanized precarious existence—communities under constant threat, survival never guaranteed, external forces always pressing. This reflected marginalized realities.

**No Failure, Only Complication**: Removing failure states while maintaining tension through weak Moves recognized that marginalized communities don't have luxury of catastrophic failure—they adapt, continue, resist. Complications create drama without annihilation.

**Intimacy and Vulnerability**: Both games centered intimate connections, vulnerability, and care as survival strategies. This recognized that marginalized communities sustain themselves through relationships.

**Queerness as Default**: Dream Askew didn't make queerness exceptional or tragic but default assumption, showing design where straight/cis wasn't baseline.

**Historical Grounding**: Dream Apart's research and cultural specificity demonstrated importance of authentic portrayal rather than generic "marginalized people" narratives.

**Mechanics Reflect Themes**: Every mechanical choice served thematic goals—token economy creating reciprocity, dual roles ensuring world engagement, no-dice creating collaborative truth-making.

**Safety as Central**: Acknowledging games explored trauma and marginalization, both prioritized safety, consent, and boundary negotiation.

## Setting and Themes

Dream Askew and Dream Apart explored parallel themes through different communities:

**Dream Askew's Queer Enclave**:

Post-apocalyptic world where societal collapse destroyed heteronormative structures. Queer people gathered in marginal spaces, building community on their terms. The enclave exists precariously—surrounded by hostile forces, limited resources, psychic maelstrom threatening stability—but also free from previous oppression.

**Setting elements:**
- Ruined urban environments
- Salvaged resources and technology
- Psychic phenomena and mutations
- Hostile outsiders and rival groups
- Internal tensions and growth

**Characters navigate:**
- Building queer family and chosen community
- Defending enclave from threats
- Managing limited resources
- Negotiating internal conflicts
- Creating art, intimacy, and meaning
- Transforming bodies and identities freely

**Dream Apart's Jewish Shtetl**:

Early 20th century Eastern European Jewish community navigating pogroms, discrimination, and cultural upheaval. The shtetl maintains rich tradition, vibrant community, and deep faith despite existential threats.

**Setting elements:**
- Traditional shtetl architecture and geography
- Orthodox religious structures and practice
- Gentile world's pressures and violence
- Internal community dynamics
- Mystical and supernatural elements

**Characters navigate:**
- Maintaining tradition amid change
- Protecting community from pogroms
- Balancing orthodoxy with individual needs
- Economic survival
- Internal conflicts and gossip
- Faith, doubt, and false messiahs

**Parallel Themes**:

**Precarious Belonging**: Both communities exist under constant threat, survival never guaranteed, yet maintain connection and culture.

**Internal Diversity**: Neither community is monolithic—both explore internal tensions, different perspectives, conflicts alongside solidarity.

**Joy and Resistance**: Both games balance struggle with celebration, showing how marginalized communities find joy, create art, build intimacy despite oppression.

**Chosen Family**: Both explore how communities form deep bonds through shared struggle and mutual care.

**Transformation and Tradition**: Both navigate tension between maintaining identity/tradition and evolving/transforming through circumstances.

**External Threats**: Both face hostile forces seeking destruction while maintaining internal community focus.

**Intimacy as Survival**: Both recognize that deep connections, vulnerability, and care sustain communities through hardship.

**Queerness**: Dream Askew centers explicitly; Dream Apart explores through historical Jewish queerness and gender nonconformity.

**Spirituality**: Both include mystical, spiritual elements—psychic phenomena in Dream Askew, Jewish mysticism in Dream Apart.

**Economic Precarity**: Both communities struggle with resources, survival, and economic sustainability.

**Final Assessment**: Dream Askew / Dream Apart stands as landmark achievement in queer and Jewish representation while pioneering innovative GM-less mechanics. The games proved that centering marginalized experiences created mechanically sophisticated, emotionally resonant designs. The Belonging Outside Belonging framework demonstrated elegant GM-less authority distribution. The token economy created reciprocal narrative power impossible in traditional structures.

Most importantly, the games gave queer and Jewish players experiences seeing their communities, struggles, and joys centered rather than marginalized. They validated that games could explore precarity, marginalization, and belonging while celebrating intimacy, resistance, and joy.

For designers, Dream Askew / Dream Apart demonstrated that marginalized-specific design could achieve critical and commercial success, that innovative mechanics could emerge from community needs, and that centering rather than marginalizing created richer, more authentic games.

For gaming broadly, the games advanced conversations about representation, who designs games about which experiences, how mechanics can explore marginalization, and what purposes games can serve beyond traditional adventure narratives.

The influence extends beyond direct play to how we think about authority distribution, community-focused narratives, token economies, and whose stories deserve centering. For these contributions, Dream Askew / Dream Apart deserves recognition as genuinely revolutionary design advancing both queer/Jewish gaming and innovative mechanics toward futures where marginalized voices shape what tabletop gaming can be.
