# Advanced Dungeons & Dragons

**Publisher**: [[TSR]]
**Designer**: [[Gary Gygax]]
**Year**: 1977
**System**: d20
**Genre**: fantasy
**Status**: out-of-print

## Historical Context

Advanced Dungeons & Dragons (AD&D) represented Gary Gygax's attempt to systematize, formalize, and expand the original D&D rules into a comprehensive fantasy roleplaying system. Published between 1977-1979 across three core rulebooks (Monster Manual 1977, Players Handbook 1978, Dungeon Masters Guide 1979), AD&D became the industry standard for fantasy gaming throughout the 1980s.

The "Advanced" designation served multiple purposes. Legally, it allowed TSR to create a separate product line under complete company control, avoiding royalty complications with Dave Arneson. Creatively, it gave Gygax freedom to revise and expand the original game's concepts without being bound by its precedents. Commercially, it established AD&D as the "serious" game for committed players while Basic D&D targeted newcomers.

AD&D introduced systematic approaches to character creation, combat, magic, and world-building that influenced virtually every subsequent fantasy RPG. The game's comprehensive scope—covering everything from mounted combat to underwater adventuring to planar travel—demonstrated RPGs' potential for simulating complex fantasy worlds.

The system dominated the market for over a decade, with the Players Handbook becoming one of the best-selling RPG books in history. AD&D established TSR's commercial dominance and created the template for professional RPG publishing.

## Mechanical Innovations

AD&D systematized and formalized many concepts introduced in original D&D:

**Unified Core Mechanics**: The d20 for attack rolls and saving throws became standardized across all situations, creating consistent resolution.

**Expanded Class System**: Added new classes beyond the original three—Paladin, Ranger, Druid, Illusionist, Assassin, Monk, and later Bard. Each class gained distinct identity through unique abilities and progression.

**Non-Weapon Proficiencies** (later editions): Skill-like system allowing character customization beyond class abilities.

**Multiclassing and Dual-Classing**: Demihumans could multiclass (advance in multiple classes simultaneously) while humans could dual-class (switch classes permanently), creating hybrid character concepts.

**Alignment System**: Expanded from Law-Neutral-Chaos to the iconic nine-alignment grid (Lawful Good, Neutral Evil, etc.), defining character morality and cosmic philosophy.

**Formalized Magic System**: Spell levels, memorization slots, and casting times received precise definitions. The spell list expanded dramatically, with detailed descriptions replacing original D&D's vague guidelines.

**Armor Class and THAC0**: Descending AC reached its fullest expression, with THAC0 (To Hit Armor Class 0) systematizing attack probability calculations.

**Saving Throw Categories**: Five save types (Paralyzation/Poison/Death Magic, Petrification/Polymorph, Rod/Staff/Wand, Breath Weapon, Spell) created distinct defense mechanisms.

**Experience Point Tables**: Each class gained unique XP requirements for leveling, with some classes advancing faster than others, creating strategic character choices.

**Encumbrance System**: Detailed weight tracking affecting movement rates, adding simulationist depth.

**Initiative and Weapon Speed**: Weapons had speed factors affecting initiative, creating tactical combat considerations beyond raw damage.

## Cultural Impact

AD&D's cultural impact extended far beyond gaming:

**Market Dominance**: From 1979-1989, AD&D effectively was fantasy RPGs. Competitors existed, but AD&D defined the category.

**Literary Influence**: The game's extensive spell lists, monster catalog, and setting material drew from and influenced fantasy literature. Terms like "Vancian magic" entered gaming vocabulary.

**Art Direction**: The iconic Dave Trampier, David Sutherland, Jeff Dee, and Erol Otus artwork defined fantasy art aesthetics for a generation, influencing everything from book covers to metal album art.

**Satanic Panic Target**: AD&D bore the brunt of 1980s moral panic concerns. The Monster Manual's demons and devils, plus occult-themed covers, made the game controversial in some communities.

**Tournament Culture**: AD&D tournaments at conventions established competitive play standards, creating shared experiences across the gaming community.

**Setting Development**: Greyhawk, Forgotten Realms, Dragonlance, Ravenloft, Planescape, and other settings created rich fictional worlds extending beyond gameplay.

**Mainstream Recognition**: AD&D achieved sufficient cultural presence that references appeared in movies (E.T.), television shows, and mainstream media, making "playing D&D" a recognizable activity.

**Economic Engine**: AD&D's success made TSR a major publisher, employed dozens of game designers, artists, and writers, and created an entire industry ecosystem.

## Design Philosophy

Gygax's design philosophy for AD&D emphasized several principles:

**Comprehensive Simulation**: AD&D attempted to provide rules for every conceivable situation, from underwater combat to astral projection to bardic music effects. This thoroughness created the sense of a completely realized world.

**DM Authority**: Despite extensive rules, the Dungeon Master remained final arbiter, empowered to override, modify, or ignore any rule as needed.

**Challenge-Based Play**: The game rewarded player skill, clever tactics, and strategic thinking over character statistics. Gygax designed deadly encounters expecting smart play, not character optimization, to ensure victory.

**Gygaxian Naturalism**: Despite fantasy elements, the world should follow internal logic and consistency. Societies function realistically, ecosystems make sense, and consequences matter.

**Adversarial DMing**: The DM's role included challenging players, creating difficult encounters, and allowing character death. The game wasn't meant to be "fair" in modern game design terms—it was meant to be conquerable through skill.

**Accumulation Through Play**: Character power, wealth, and magic items accumulated through actual adventuring, not planning or optimization. What your character gained reflected their history.

## Setting and Themes

While officially setting-neutral, AD&D's implied setting drew heavily from:

**Greyhawk**: Gygax's own campaign world, serving as the default setting for examples and adventures. Greyhawk combined Tolkienesque fantasy with sword & sorcery elements.

**Appendix N**: Gygax's famous "Appendix N: Inspirational and Educational Reading" listed source material including Fritz Leiber, Jack Vance, Robert E. Howard, Michael Moorcock, Roger Zelazny, and many others—creating a literary canon for the game.

**European Medieval Fantasy**: Knights, castles, feudal societies, and medieval weaponry provided the baseline, though filtered through fantasy lenses.

**Mythological Pluralism**: The game incorporated creatures and concepts from Norse, Greek, Egyptian, and other mythologies without attempting historical accuracy or cultural sensitivity.

**Cosmic Morality**: The alignment system positioned moral choices as cosmic forces, with the Great Wheel cosmology reflecting these philosophical positions in planar geography.

**Dungeon Delving**: Despite wilderness and urban options, the dungeon remained the archetypal adventuring location—underground complexes filled with monsters, traps, and treasure.

## Reception and Legacy

AD&D's reception established it as the definitive RPG:

**Critical Acclaim**: Gaming magazines praised the systematic approach and comprehensive coverage, though some criticized complexity and Gygax's prose style.

**Commercial Dominance**: The Players Handbook sold millions of copies, remaining in print continuously from 1978 until Second Edition in 1989.

**Setting Proliferation**: Numerous campaign settings expanded the AD&D multiverse, each supporting extensive product lines.

**Tournament Scene**: The RPGA (Role-Playing Game Association) organized AD&D events nationwide, creating competitive play communities.

**Cultural Controversy**: The Satanic Panic targeted AD&D specifically, creating lasting stigma but also mainstream awareness.

**Second Edition Transition**: AD&D 2nd Edition (1989) refined without revolutionizing, maintaining essential compatibility while addressing controversy and modernizing presentation.

**Long-Term Influence**: AD&D established design patterns, terminology, and concepts that persist in D&D Fifth Edition and countless other games. Its influence appears in everything from video games (Baldur's Gate, Neverwinter Nights) to fantasy literature.

The game proved RPGs could be serious hobbies supporting extensive published materials, professional design, and dedicated communities. Every subsequent edition of D&D builds on AD&D's foundation.
