# Earthdawn

**Publisher**: [[FASA Corporation]]
**Designer**: [[Greg Gorden]], [[Bob Charrette]]
**Year**: 1993
**System**: Step System
**Genre**: high fantasy, post-apocalyptic fantasy
**Status**: in-print (current edition by FASA Games, Inc.)

## Overview

Earthdawn, designed by Greg Gorden and Bob Charrette and published by FASA Corporation in 1993, emerged as one of the 1990s' most ambitious and innovative fantasy RPGs—a game that dared to ask "what if the traditional fantasy dungeon crawl existed in a post-apocalyptic world recovering from magical catastrophe?" Set in the province of Barsaive, where civilization emerges from centuries-long hiding in sealed underground kaers (protective strongholds) from the Scourge (demonic Horror invasion), Earthdawn combined traditional fantasy adventuring with horror elements, archaeological exploration, and one of gaming's most unique magic systems.

The game's central innovation was treating magic as living, interconnected force woven through reality. Characters didn't merely cast spells—they formed magical bonds with items through "threading," discovered their disciplines' secret knowledge through advancement, and navigated a world where magic permeated everything. Magical items weren't simple +1 swords; they were thread items with histories, powers unlocked through attuning to them, and potential for growth alongside characters. This approach made magic feel mysterious, powerful, and deeply integrated into character development rather than mechanical resource management.

Earthdawn's connection to Shadowrun generated massive interest. FASA explicitly designed Earthdawn as Shadowrun's distant past—the Fourth World to Shadowrun's Sixth World. The Scourge in Earthdawn corresponded to magic's ebb before returning in Shadowrun's cyberpunk future. Horrors that plagued Earthdawn's characters would eventually return in Shadowrun's timeline. This shared universe approach created unprecedented metaplot connectivity between two distinct game lines, allowing players to see how magical catastrophe in fantasy past created cyberpunk future.

The Step System, Earthdawn's core mechanic, used innovative step-and-dice approach where character abilities translated to "steps" on table determining which dice to roll. A character with Step 7 ability rolled d12, while Step 15 rolled d20+d8. Higher steps meant better capabilities translated into larger dice pools. Exploding dice (rolling maximum meant rolling again and adding) created potential for dramatic successes. This system handled everything from combat to magic to social interactions with unified mechanic.

What distinguished Earthdawn from contemporary fantasy RPGs was its thoughtful worldbuilding and mechanical integration. The setting wasn't generic medieval fantasy—it was specific post-apocalyptic recovery period where every element connected to the Scourge's aftermath. Why were characters exploring dungeons? Because kaers held pre-Scourge treasures and knowledge. Why did Horrors matter? Because they corrupted magic and threatened returning civilization. Why was magic so important? Because understanding pre-Scourge magical knowledge meant survival and power in recovering world.

The game achieved critical acclaim, winning Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules in 1994. It developed devoted following and extensive supplement line exploring Barsaive's regions, magical traditions, Horror lore, and metaplot developments. However, FASA's financial troubles in the late 1990s disrupted support. The company sold Earthdawn's rights, leading to complex publication history across multiple publishers (Living Room Games, RedBrick, FASA Games Inc.) and editions.

Despite these challenges, Earthdawn maintained loyal community. The game's unique magic system, compelling setting, and innovative mechanics continued attracting players decades after initial publication. Modern designers recognize Earthdawn's influence on magic system design, metaplot storytelling, and creating mechanically distinctive fantasy games.

## Historical Context

In 1993, fantasy RPG market was dominated by TSR's AD&D Second Edition, with most competitors offering either lighter alternatives (GURPS Fantasy) or specific subgenre focuses (Warhammer's grimdark, Ars Magica's wizard focus). The market seemed saturated—what new approach could fantasy RPG take?

FASA Corporation, riding high on Shadowrun's success (released 1989), saw opportunity in connecting their cyberpunk game to fantasy predecessor. The idea was audacious: create fantasy RPG that was simultaneously standalone game and prequel to Shadowrun, separated by thousands of years but sharing magical cosmology, racial histories, and metaplot elements.

Greg Gorden and Bob Charrette led design team, bringing experience from Shadowrun and other FASA projects. Their goal was creating fantasy game that felt fresh—not another "dungeons and dragons" clone but something mechanically innovative and thematically distinctive while maintaining enough familiar elements to attract fantasy gamers.

The setting concept—post-apocalyptic fantasy—provided that distinctiveness. Rather than stable medieval fantasy world, Barsaive was recovering civilization. Four hundred years earlier, magical theory predicted the Scourge—periodic rise in magic levels that enabled extradimensional Horrors to invade. The great dragon Mountainshadow warned the world, and civilizations built kaers—magically sealed underground refuges. Populations hid for centuries while Horrors ravaged surface. When magic levels dropped enough for safety, survivors emerged to ruined world.

This premise brilliantly justified traditional RPG activities. Dungeon crawling? You're exploring sealed kaers seeking pre-Scourge knowledge and treasures. Monster fighting? Horrors and their corrupted servants still lurked. Mysterious magic? Much knowledge was lost; rediscovering it drove advancement. The setup gave narrative weight to standard fantasy gaming while enabling fresh approaches.

The Shadowrun connection generated enormous marketing buzz. FASA promoted Earthdawn as revealing Shadowrun's mythical past. Shadowrun fans curious about their game's deep history bought Earthdawn. Fantasy fans interested in innovative game discovered Earthdawn. This cross-pollination benefited both lines.

The Step System represented deliberate innovation. Rather than using d20 rolls or percentile dice like most competitors, FASA created step-based progression with exploding dice. This created different feel—more granular advancement, more dramatic swings, more flexibility in difficulty scaling. The system had learning curve but rewarded mastery with elegant resolution.

Magic system design consumed significant development effort. Rather than spell slots or mana points, Earthdawn used spell matrices (mental frameworks holding spells), thread weaving (connecting to magical patterns), and blood magic (permanent sacrifice for power). This complexity aimed to make magic feel significant and mysterious rather than mundane tool.

Disciplines—Earthdawn's character classes—reflected setting specificity. You weren't generic "fighter" or "wizard"; you were Warrior (combat master), Sky Raider (aerial cavalry), Nethermancer (underworld magic specialist), Troubadour (magical performer), or other culturally-specific profession. Each discipline had mechanical distinctiveness and setting integration.

First Edition launched 1993 with distinctive production values—full-color hardcover with elaborate artwork depicting Barsaive's unique aesthetic. The book was dense—complex mechanics and extensive setting information created substantial page count. This depth attracted players seeking rich campaign frameworks but intimidated those wanting accessibility.

Critical reception was largely positive. Industry publications praised innovation, compelling setting, and mechanical distinctiveness. Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules validated design quality. However, reviews noted complexity—character creation and gameplay required investment, making casual play difficult.

Commercial performance was strong initially. FASA supported Earthdawn extensively with supplements exploring Barsaive's regions, advancing metaplot, detailing Horror lore, expanding magical options, and connecting to Shadowrun deeper. Major releases included:

- **Prelude to War** - Advancing metaplot toward Theran conflict
- **Parlainth: The Forgotten City** - Detailed city exploration campaign
- **Horrors** - Extensive Horror bestiary and lore
- **Magic: A Manual of Mystic Secrets** - Expanded magic rules
- Various regional sourcebooks and adventures

However, FASA Corporation faced mounting financial pressures through late 1990s. BattleTech and Shadowrun remained profitable, but other lines struggled. Corporate restructuring in 1999 led to selling several properties. Earthdawn rights went to Living Room Games, beginning complicated publication history.

Living Room Games published Second Edition (1999) with streamlined mechanics and setting advances. However, that company also faced challenges, leading to RedBrick acquiring rights and publishing Third Edition (2009). Most recently, FASA Games, Inc. (separate company from original FASA Corporation but connected through founders) has maintained the line.

This convoluted history fragmented community and complicated organized play. Different editions, publishers, and rules versions made tracking current status difficult. However, core fanbase remained devoted throughout transitions, demonstrating game's lasting appeal.

Earthdawn's influence on gaming extended beyond direct play. Its magic system influenced thinking about making magic systems mechanically distinctive. Metaplot connectivity between game lines became more common. Post-apocalyptic fantasy as subgenre gained legitimacy. Thread items and character-specific magical progression appeared in various forms elsewhere.

The game demonstrated that fantasy RPG innovation was possible in seemingly saturated market. By committing fully to distinctive setting and mechanical vision rather than trying to please everyone, Earthdawn created passionate niche following that sustained it through decades and multiple publishers.

## Core Mechanics

Earthdawn's Step System and interconnected mechanics created distinctive play experience:

**The Step System**: Core resolution used steps—numerical ratings determining which dice to roll. Character abilities, difficulties, and effects all used step notation:

- Steps 1-7: Single die (d4 through d12)
- Steps 8+: Multiple dice (d20+d4, d20+d6, etc.)
- Step table in rulebook showed exact dice for each step

When attempting action, find your step, roll indicated dice, add results plus modifiers, compare to difficulty.

**Exploding Dice**: Rolling maximum on any die meant roll again and add to total. This could cascade—rolling maximum repeatedly meant adding each result. This created potential for spectacular successes while maintaining bell curve for typical results.

**Action Tests**: Most actions required rolling step vs. difficulty number. Beating difficulty meant success; margin of success sometimes mattered for degree of effect.

**Disciplines**: Character classes in Earthdawn, but deeply integrated with setting. Each discipline represented specific magical tradition and social role:

- **Warrior**: Combat masters using weapon magic
- **Scout**: Wilderness experts with tracking and stealth
- **Thief**: Urban specialists in stealth and acquisition
- **Nethermancer**: Death and underworld magic users
- **Elementalist**: Elemental force manipulators
- **Illusionist**: Masters of perception and deception
- **Sky Raider**: Aerial cavalry riding magical beasts
- **Troubadour**: Performer-magicians weaving art and power
- **Swordmaster**: Blade masters achieving supernatural skill
- **Archer**: Ranged combat specialists with magical accuracy
- **Cavalryman**: Mounted warriors bonding with steeds
- **Horror Stalker**: Specialized Horror hunters

Each discipline had talent lists (abilities learned advancing), discipline-specific powers, and cultural context.

**Talents**: Disciplines' special abilities, representing their magical training. Talents ranged from combat techniques (Melee Weapons, Unarmed Combat) to magical effects (Astral Sight, Thread Weaving) to social abilities (Persuasion, Streetwise). Advancing discipline increased available talents and their ranks.

**Circles**: Instead of levels, characters advanced through circles (1st Circle, 2nd Circle, etc.). Each circle required mastering certain talents, spending legend points, and meeting requirements. This created granular advancement and clear progression.

**Legend Points**: Experience points earned through achieving goals, overcoming challenges, good roleplaying. Spent to increase talent ranks and advance circles. The system encouraged heroic actions by rewarding legendary deeds more than routine success.

**Karma**: Resource representing characters' connection to patterns of magic. Each discipline had karma pool, spent to add bonus dice to important rolls. This represented discipline training and magical nature. Managing karma—when to spend for critical moments—added tactical layer.

**Thread Weaving**: Unique mechanic for connecting to magical patterns. Thread items (magical objects) required weaving threads—researching item's history, performing actions connecting you to its pattern, making Weaving Tests to forge connection. Successfully weaving thread unlocked item's powers, with multiple ranks revealing deeper abilities.

This made magical items earn their power through engagement rather than simply acquiring loot. A thread sword might start moderately powerful but reveal spectacular abilities as you learned its history and wove more threads.

**Blood Magic**: Dark but sometimes necessary practice of using permanent physical sacrifice (attributes, wounds, even death) to power magic. Blood magic produced powerful effects but at terrible cost. The mechanics made it viable but deterred casual use—you could boost spells enormously by sacrificing attributes, but recovering those sacrifices took significant time and resources.

**Spell Matrices**: Mental frameworks holding spells ready. Spellcasters had limited matrices based on circle, forcing choices about which spells to keep prepared. Casting from matrix was fast; casting without matrix (raw magic) was slower and riskier. This created resource management without spell slots.

**Armor**: Reduced damage rather than making you harder to hit. Physical and mystic armor protected against respective damage types. This meant combat often hit but armor absorbed damage, creating different feel from D&D-style AC.

**Damage**: Wounds were subtracted from abilities directly. Taking damage reduced attributes and talents, creating death spiral—wounded characters were less effective, making further wounds more likely. This encouraged avoiding excessive damage and tactical withdrawal.

**Healing**: Required time and skill. Natural healing was slow; magical healing (from healing talents or items) accelerated recovery but wasn't instant. Serious wounds meant characters needed recovery time, creating campaign pacing where injuries mattered.

**Horror Encounters**: Horrors used special rules reflecting their extradimensional nature and corruption powers. Many could mark characters (creating ongoing connection enabling Horror to find and influence them), corrupt magic, or inflict psychological damage. Fighting Horrors was dangerous not just physically but spiritually.

**Horror Marks**: Getting marked by Horror created lasting vulnerability. The Horror could sense you, influence you, potentially corrupt you. Removing marks required difficult quests or powerful magic. This made Horror encounters have lasting consequences.

**Dragon Magic**: Dragons were immortal magical beings of immense power. The mechanics reflected this—interacting with dragons involved different scale entirely, with their magic operating at levels player characters couldn't match. This maintained dragons as mysterious, dangerous forces.

**Name-Giving Races**: Playable races each had distinct mechanical and cultural identities:

- **Humans**: Versatile, ambitious
- **Dwarfs**: Sturdy, loyal, strongly community-focused
- **Elves**: Graceful, magical, connected to nature
- **Orks**: Strong, passionate, artistic
- **Trolls**: Largest, strongest, honorable warrior culture
- **T'skrang**: Reptilian, flamboyant, river traders
- **Windlings**: Tiny flying pixie-like beings
- **Obsidimen**: Living stone beings, slow but enduring

Each race offered different play experiences and cultural perspectives.

**Bestiary and NPCs**: Creatures and characters used same step system as players but often had special abilities, Horror powers, or unique magic. This created consistent resolution while allowing distinctive opposition.

## Setting and Themes

**Barsaive**: The primary setting—province in Earthdawn's world, emerging from Scourge's aftermath. Barsaive featured diverse geography (forests, mountains, swamps, jungles), numerous settlements, ancient ruins, and varied cultures. The province wasn't unified nation but collection of communities, city-states, and tribal territories navigating post-apocalyptic recovery.

**The Scourge**: Central historical event—magical cycle bringing Horrors into world. Four centuries earlier, the dragon Mountainshadow predicted the Scourge based on studying magical cycles. Theran Empire (dominant pre-Scourge civilization) built kaers and citadels—magically protected refuges where populations sealed themselves for duration.

Not everyone believed the warnings. Some communities didn't build protection; they were consumed. Some kaers failed; their populations were destroyed or worse. Emerging survivors discovered world fundamentally changed—civilizations destroyed, knowledge lost, Horrors still lurking.

**Horrors**: Extradimensional entities entering world during high magic periods. Horrors fed on suffering, corrupted magic, and marked victims for ongoing torment. They ranged from lesser spirits to godlike entities. Each Horror was unique with distinct powers, motivations, and methods.

What made Horrors particularly terrifying was their corruption—they twisted magic, marked victims psychologically, and created lasting trauma. Fighting Horrors wasn't just combat but spiritual/psychological struggle. Some Horrors couldn't be truly killed, only banished temporarily.

**Theran Empire**: Pre-Scourge dominant civilization, survived in flying citadel city. Post-Scourge, Thera sought to reconquer former territories including Barsaive. Therans were imperialistic, magically powerful, slaveholding culture. They served as primary antagonist—organized military threat representing old order trying to reimpose control.

Thrall revolt—escaped Theran slaves forming resistance—added complexity. Not all Therans were evil; not all resistance was heroic. The conflict provided ongoing campaign framework without being simplistic good-versus-evil.

**Passion**: Spiritual entities embodying ideals (love, honor, struggle, growth, etc.). Unlike gods in many settings, Passions didn't grant divine magic but represented philosophical/spiritual frameworks. Characters could follow Passions, drawing inspiration and guidance.

**Questors**: Followers of Passions who earned special abilities through devotion. Questor paths provided additional character development option, allowing characters to combine discipline abilities with Passion-granted powers.

**Kaers and Citadels**: The sealed refuges where populations survived Scourge. Sealed kaers—undiscovered, potentially intact—were legendary treasure troves and knowledge sources. Exploring sealed kaer was dangerous (Horrors might be trapped inside, defenses might activate, resources might be claimed by current inhabitants) but potentially enormously rewarding.

This justified dungeon-crawling activities with narrative weight—you weren't raiding random dungeons but recovering civilization's lost knowledge and resources.

**Living Magic**: Magic wasn't external force but woven through reality. Everything contained magical pattern (astral signature). Magic users could see and interact with these patterns. This made magic feel integrated into world rather than separate supernatural overlay.

**Name-Giver Philosophy**: Sentient races were "Name-Givers"—beings capable of true names and thereby accessing magic deeply. This philosophical framework connected identity, magic, and consciousness in ways affecting gameplay (true names had power, knowing something's pattern granted influence over it).

**Themes**:

- **Recovery and Rebuilding**: Core theme—civilization recovering from apocalypse, individuals piecing together lost knowledge
- **Collective Trauma**: The Scourge's aftermath affected everyone; dealing with cultural trauma pervaded setting
- **Knowledge as Power**: Rediscovering pre-Scourge knowledge drove advancement and power
- **Horror and Corruption**: Ever-present threat of Horror influence and magical corruption
- **Identity and Memory**: True names, patterns, and histories shaped power and identity
- **Community vs. Imperialism**: Local communities resisting Theran imperial reconquest
- **Magic's Double Edge**: Magic enabled survival and power but also created Scourge cycle and Horror vulnerability
- **Heroism Through Sacrifice**: Many victories required blood magic's permanent sacrifice or accepting Horror marks

## Cultural Impact

**Magic System Innovation**: Earthdawn's approach to magic—thread items, pattern weaving, living magic as world feature—influenced subsequent fantasy game design. Later games adopted similar concepts of earning magical item power through engagement.

**Post-Apocalyptic Fantasy**: The game legitimized post-apocalyptic fantasy as subgenre distinct from generic medieval fantasy. This opened space for similar settings exploring aftermath themes.

**Metaplot Connectivity**: Explicit connection between Earthdawn and Shadowrun demonstrated transmedia storytelling potential within gaming. The idea that fantasy past and cyberpunk future could be mechanically separate games sharing cosmology influenced later shared universe approaches.

**Step System Influence**: While not widely adopted directly, the step system showed alternative resolution mechanics to d20 or percentile. Some later games experimented with similar step-based approaches.

**Horror Integration**: Blending traditional fantasy adventuring with horror elements influenced how designers thought about tone mixing. Earthdawn showed horror could enhance rather than replace fantasy adventure.

**Origins Award**: Winning Best Roleplaying Rules (1994) validated the design's innovation and quality, bringing broader attention.

**Commercial Challenges**: The game's complicated publication history (multiple publishers, editions, rights transfers) became cautionary tale about intellectual property management and publisher transitions.

**Devoted Community**: Despite challenges, Earthdawn maintained loyal following through decades. Fan sites, continued play, and community support demonstrated strong attachment.

**Setting Depth**: Barsaive's detailed worldbuilding influenced expectations for campaign setting development. The interconnected cultures, metaplot, and historical depth set high bar.

**Influence on Shadowrun**: The connection worked both ways—Earthdawn details influenced Shadowrun's magical history, adding depth to that game's world.

**Academic Interest**: Game studies scholars examined Earthdawn's approach to metaplot, trauma narratives in gaming, and post-apocalyptic fantasy themes.

**Limited Mainstream Success**: Despite innovation, Earthdawn never achieved D&D-level market penetration. This demonstrated that mechanical innovation and critical acclaim didn't guarantee commercial dominance in crowded fantasy market.

**Critical Acclaim**:
- Origins Award Best Roleplaying Rules (1994)
- Praised for innovative magic system
- Recognized for detailed worldbuilding
- Appreciated for fresh fantasy approach
- Noted for mechanical distinctiveness

**Player Reception**:
- Devoted following embraced setting and mechanics
- Thread item system particularly beloved
- Appreciated connection to Shadowrun
- Enjoyed post-apocalyptic fantasy approach

**Criticisms**:
- **Complexity**: Steep learning curve deterred casual players
- **Character Creation**: Time-consuming and mechanically intricate
- **Combat Speed**: Detailed wound system slowed resolution
- **Magic Density**: Heavy magic integration felt overwhelming to some
- **Metaplot Dependency**: Advancing metaplot made jumping in later difficult
- **Publication Instability**: Multiple publishers created confusion
- **Edition Fragmentation**: Different editions with incompatible rules split community

**Edition Reception**:
- **First Edition**: Most beloved for original vision but most complex
- **Second Edition**: Streamlined some mechanics but felt less distinctive
- **Third Edition**: Modern mechanical updates but different feel
- Debates about "best" edition persist

**Long-term Community**:
- Sustained play decades after initial publication
- Fan-created content and conversions
- Online communities maintaining resources
- Recognition as underappreciated gem

**Comparison to Competitors**:
- More complex than D&D but more distinctive
- More setting-specific than GURPS
- More mechanically innovative than most 1990s fantasy
- Less successful commercially but critically respected
