# Champions

**Publisher**: [[Hero Games]]
**Designer**: [[George MacDonald]], [[Steve Peterson]]
**Year**: 1981
**System**: HERO System
**Genre**: superhero
**Status**: in-print

## Overview

Champions, designed by George MacDonald and Steve Peterson and published by Hero Games in 1981, revolutionized superhero roleplaying and influenced RPG design broadly by introducing comprehensive point-buy character creation and effect-based power construction that allowed players to build virtually any superhero concept through systematic mechanical framework. As the first commercially successful superhero RPG—predating Marvel Super Heroes (1984) and DC Heroes (1985)—Champions established the genre's conventions while pioneering mechanical approaches that would influence countless subsequent games across all genres.

The game emerged at moment when superhero comics enjoyed mainstream popularity through Marvel and DC's Bronze Age classics, yet tabletop gaming had barely explored the genre. Previous attempts (Superhero 2044, Villains & Vigilantes) offered basic frameworks, but Champions provided comprehensive system where players could faithfully recreate Superman, Spider-Man, Wolverine, or original concepts through detailed customization. The game's tagline captured its ambition: "Champions: The Super Roleplaying Game."

Champions' revolutionary contribution was the point-buy system. Instead of rolling random attributes or selecting from preset character classes, players received point pool (typically 100-150 points initially, later standardized at higher totals) and purchased every aspect of their character: attributes, powers, skills, advantages, and disadvantages. Every mechanical element had point cost calculated through transparent formulas. This created unprecedented customization—players could build speedsters, energy projectors, mentalists, martial artists, power armor users, or hybrid concepts, all mechanically balanced through point expenditure.

The powers construction system was equally innovative. Rather than predetermined spell lists or power sets, Champions offered building blocks—Energy Blast, Telekinesis, Force Field, Flight, etc.—that players modified through Advantages (enhancements like Area Effect or Armor Piercing) and Limitations (restrictions like Charges or Activation Roll). This effect-based approach meant mechanically identical powers could represent vastly different narrative sources: an Energy Blast could be fire, ice, cosmic rays, or psychic force, all with identical game statistics but different special effects.

The tactical combat system emphasized positioning, movement, and power interactions. Initiative operated in phases within 12-second turns, allowing faster characters multiple actions. Combat tracked location-based damage (hitting specific body locations), knockback (powerful attacks sending targets flying), and stunning (attacks overwhelming defenses without causing injury). This created comic-book action where heroes and villains maneuvered dynamically, where energy blasts sent opponents crashing through walls, where speedsters circled slower opponents striking repeatedly.

Champions succeeded commercially and critically, winning Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules of 1981. Hero Games supported it extensively with supplements exploring powers, villains, equipment, and campaign settings. The game evolved through multiple editions (Second 1982, Third 1984, Fourth 1989, Fifth 2002, Sixth 2009), each refining the system while maintaining core design. The HERO System itself became generic framework adapted for fantasy (Fantasy Hero), science fiction (Star Hero), horror (Dark Champions), and other genres.

The game's cultural impact extended far beyond superhero gaming. The point-buy approach influenced countless RPGs—GURPS (1986) refined Champions' concept into comprehensive generic system, while games across all genres adopted point-based character creation. The effect-based power construction inspired magic systems, psionics, and equipment design in numerous games. The tactical combat influenced superhero games and tactical RPGs generally.

However, Champions also became infamous for complexity. Calculating power costs required mathematical facility. Building characters took hours for new players. Combat with multiple powered combatants could slow dramatically. The system's comprehensiveness came at accessibility cost—Champions rewarded system mastery but intimidated casual players.

Despite complexity, Champions maintained devoted following appreciating its specific virtues: unparalleled customization, mechanical balance through point costs, faithful simulation of comic book action, and comprehensive power coverage. For players wanting to build the superhero they imagined without mechanical compromises, Champions remained unmatched.

## Historical Context

In 1981, superhero comics dominated popular culture. Marvel Comics' X-Men, Spider-Man, and Avengers enjoyed enormous popularity. DC Comics' Superman, Batman, and Justice League remained cultural icons. The medium's Bronze Age (roughly 1970-1985) saw sophisticated storytelling, morally complex characters, and expanding fictional universes. Yet tabletop gaming had barely tapped superhero potential.

Early superhero RPGs existed but remained primitive. Superhero 2044 (1977) was innovative but mechanically crude. Villains & Vigilantes (1979) offered simplicity but limited customization. Both sold modestly but didn't capture superhero gaming's potential. The market awaited comprehensive system that could faithfully simulate comics' breadth—from street-level vigilantes to cosmic powerhouses.

George MacDonald and Steve Peterson, both superhero enthusiasts and experienced gamers, recognized the gap. They wanted system allowing players to build any superhero imaginable—Superman's strength and invulnerability, Spider-Man's agility and web-slinging, Professor X's telepathy, Iron Man's powered armor—through consistent mechanical framework. Existing games forced compromises; MacDonald and Peterson sought to eliminate them.

Their solution was radical: comprehensive point-buy system where every mechanical element had explicit cost. Players designed characters by purchasing attributes, powers, skills, perks, and disadvantages within point budget. Want super strength? Each +5 Strength costs points. Want flight? Points based on speed. Want energy projection? Points based on damage and effects. This transparency created mechanical balance—more powerful abilities cost more points, preventing inadvertent imbalances.

The effect-based power design was equally revolutionary. Instead of naming specific powers ("Fire Blast," "Ice Beam"), Champions offered generic effects ("Energy Blast") that players customized through Advantages and Limitations. An Energy Blast dealing 10d6 damage was mechanically identical whether it represented fire, cosmic rays, or magical force—the "special effects" were narrative rather than mechanical. This separated mechanics from fiction elegantly, allowing infinite concepts within finite ruleset.

The designers developed Champions in late 1970s, playtesting extensively before publication. They drew on superhero comics for inspiration but prioritized mechanical consistency over literal simulation. The game abstracted superhero action into tactical system emphasizing movement, positioning, power interactions, and dramatic confrontations.

Hero Games published Champions in 1981 as boxed set containing rulebooks, character sheets, and introductory materials. Production values were modest—black-and-white interior art, simple layout—but content was revolutionary. The 128-page rulebook detailed character creation, powers construction, combat, campaign frameworks, and sample characters.

Critical reception was extraordinary. Reviews praised the customization, mechanical balance, and comprehensive approach. Champions won Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules of 1981, validating its innovations. Players appreciated being able to build specific superhero concepts without settling for approximations.

Commercial success followed. Champions sold well enough to sustain Hero Games and justify immediate expansion. Second Edition appeared in 1982, refining rules based on play feedback. Third Edition (1984) expanded significantly, establishing definitive early Champions. Fourth Edition (1989) became standard for years. Each edition refined mechanics while maintaining core philosophy.

The HERO System itself became separate product line. Recognizing that Champions' mechanics could support other genres, Hero Games published generic HERO System (1984) and genre-specific applications: Fantasy Hero (fantasy), Star Hero (science fiction), Danger International (espionage), Dark Champions (street-level vigilantes), Ninja Hero (martial arts). This demonstrated Champions' mechanical flexibility beyond superheroics.

Supplements expanded Champions extensively. Enemies books provided villains. Equipment books detailed gadgets and vehicles. Setting books explored Champions Universe (default setting) and variants. Campaign books offered adventure frameworks. This support sustained active player community and demonstrated commercial viability.

Champions influenced competitor design significantly. When TSR published Marvel Super Heroes (1984) and Mayfair released DC Heroes (1985), both acknowledged Champions' precedent. Later superhero games—Aberrant, Mutants & Masterminds, Icons, Sentinels Comics—all showed Champions' influence in mechanical approaches and design priorities.

The game's complexity became both strength and limitation. Hardcore fans loved the tactical depth and customization. Casual players found character creation daunting and combat slow. This split market between Champions loyalists and players preferring simpler alternatives.

By 1990s and 2000s, Champions faced increasing competition. Mutants & Masterminds (2002) offered streamlined d20-based alternative. Other superhero games emphasized different play styles—narrative-focused, rules-light, licensed properties. However, Champions maintained core audience valuing its specific approach.

Hero Games went through various ownership changes—sold to DOJ, Inc. in 1996, later independent again—but continued supporting Champions. Fifth Edition (2002) and Sixth Edition (2009) modernized rules significantly while preserving fundamental design. The game remained available continuously from 1981 through 2025—44+ years—remarkable longevity demonstrating sustained viability.

## Core Mechanics

Champions' HERO System mechanics were comprehensive and mathematically rigorous:

**Point-Buy Character Creation**: Players received point pool (campaign-specific, typically 250-400 for standard heroes) to purchase all character elements:

**Characteristics**: Eight primary attributes:
- **STR** (Strength): Physical power and damage
- **DEX** (Dexterity): Agility and combat skill
- **CON** (Constitution): Endurance and toughness
- **BODY**: Physical resilience and hit points
- **INT** (Intelligence): Mental acuity
- **EGO**: Mental strength and willpower
- **PRE** (Presence): Charisma and force of personality
- **COM** (Comeliness): Physical attractiveness

Each point in Characteristics cost specific point amounts. Increasing STR from 10 to 20 cost points; decreasing below 10 provided extra points for other purchases.

**Derived Characteristics**: Calculated from primaries:
- **PD** (Physical Defense): Resistance to physical attacks
- **ED** (Energy Defense): Resistance to energy attacks
- **SPD** (Speed): Actions per turn in combat
- **REC** (Recovery): Healing rate
- **END** (Endurance): Power reserve
- **STUN**: Ability to resist stunning
- **BODY**: Hit points equivalent

**Powers**: The heart of Champions. Players purchased powers from extensive lists:

- **Movement Powers**: Flight, Swimming, Swinging, Teleportation, Running
- **Attack Powers**: Energy Blast, Hand-to-Hand Attack, Mind Control, Telekinesis
- **Defense Powers**: Force Field, Damage Resistance, Flash Defense
- **Mental Powers**: Telepathy, Mind Link, Mental Illusions
- **Sensory Powers**: Clairsentience, Enhanced Senses, Detect
- **Body-Affecting Powers**: Healing, Aid, Drain, Transfer
- **Special Powers**: Luck, Duplication, Growth, Shrinking

Each power had base cost and scaling—Energy Blast cost points per damage die, Flight cost points per meter movement.

**Advantages**: Enhancements increasing power effectiveness and cost:
- **Area Effect**: Affects multiple targets (+1/2 cost per level)
- **Armor Piercing**: Ignores defenses (+1/2 cost)
- **Continuous**: Remains active without concentration (+1/2 cost)
- **Range**: Extended targeting distance (variable)

**Limitations**: Restrictions decreasing power cost:
- **Charges**: Limited uses (-1/4 per type)
- **Activation Roll**: May fail to activate (-1/4 to -1 depending on difficulty)
- **Requires Skill Roll**: Success required for use (-1/4 to -1/2)
- **OAF/IAF**: Obvious/Inobvious Accessible Focus—power in object that can be removed (-1 to -1/4)
- **Gestures/Incantations**: Requires specific actions (-1/4)

The Advantages and Limitations system allowed fine-tuning powers. A base Energy Blast 10d6 (50 points) could become:
- +1/2 Armor Piercing (75 points total)
- -1/4 Charges 8 (40 points total)
- Result: Armor-Piercing Energy Blast with limited uses

**Skills**: Purchased with points. Skills provided percentage chance of success or roll modifications:
- **Combat Skills**: Martial Arts, Weapon Familiarity
- **Movement Skills**: Acrobatics, Climbing
- **Intellectual Skills**: Science, Computer Programming
- **Social Skills**: Persuasion, Seduction
- **Background Skills**: Professional knowledge

**Disadvantages**: Players could accept character limitations for additional points:
- **Physical Limitations**: Distinctive Features, Missing Limb
- **Psychological Limitations**: Code Against Killing, Overconfidence
- **Social Limitations**: Secret Identity, Public Identity
- **Enemy**: Recurring villain or organization
- **Hunted**: Actively pursued by antagonist
- **Dependence**: Requires specific substance
- **Vulnerability**: Extra damage from specific attack type

Disadvantages provided points for purchasing powers while adding character depth and campaign complications.

**Combat System**: Detailed tactical framework:

**Combat Turn**: 12 seconds divided into 12 phases. Character's SPD determined which phases they acted (SPD 4 = act on phases 3, 6, 9, 12).

**Actions**: Each phase, acting characters could:
- **Half Move and Attack**
- **Full Move**
- **Attack and Maneuver**
- **Recover** (regain END and STUN)
- **Use Powers**

**Attack Resolution**:
1. Attacker rolls 3d6 + OCV (Offensive Combat Value—attack skill)
2. Defender's DCV (Defensive Combat Value—defense rating) is target number
3. Success means hit; determine hit location (random or called shot)
4. Roll damage (dice based on power)
5. Defender subtracts PD (physical) or ED (energy) defense
6. Remaining damage applies to BODY and STUN
7. Check for Knockback (powerful hits send target flying)

**Damage**: Two types tracked separately:
- **BODY**: Actual injury to physical structure
- **STUN**: Shock, pain, stunning effect

Attacks typically dealt more STUN than BODY (ratio of 3:1 normal). Losing all STUN meant unconsciousness; losing all BODY meant death.

**Knockback**: Attacks exceeding defenses by specific margins sent targets flying backward. Distance determined by margin—superhuman battles sent combatants through buildings, creating comic-book drama.

**Hit Locations**: Called shots or random rolls determined where attacks landed:
- **Head**: Difficult to hit (+2 STUN multiplier)
- **Hands/Arms**: Disabling attacks
- **Body/Legs**: Standard targeting
- **Vitals**: Extra difficult (+5 STUN multiplier if hit)

**Special Effects**: Players and GM created narrative descriptions for mechanical effects—Energy Blast hitting might burn, freeze, or blast with cosmic force depending on special effects.

**Campaign Frameworks**: Rules for creating ongoing campaigns, balancing villains, designing adventures, handling progression.

The mechanical philosophy prioritized balance through point costs, customization through modular construction, and tactical engagement through detailed combat. This created complex but comprehensive system rewarding system mastery.

## Setting and Themes

Champions initially lacked default setting, providing generic superhero framework. Players created their own cities, villains, and campaign continuities. However, through supplements, the **Champions Universe** emerged:

**Generic Four-Color Superheroics**: The default tone was optimistic Silver Age superheroics—heroes fought colorful villains, protected innocents, and upheld justice. While darker stories were possible, the assumed baseline was heroic adventure.

**Urban Campaigns**: Most campaigns centered on fictional cities where heroes protected against supervillains, street crime, natural disasters, and cosmic threats. This allowed self-contained continuity without Marvel/DC baggage.

**Teams and Organizations**: Games typically featured superhero teams (à la Avengers or Justice League) working together. This justified diverse power sets and encouraged collaborative play.

**Villains and Threats**: Supplement villains ran from street-level criminals to cosmic threats:
- **Supervillains**: Powered antagonists seeking wealth, power, or destruction
- **Criminal Organizations**: VIPER (terrorist group), ARGENT (corporate crime)
- **Cosmic Threats**: Alien invaders, dimensional entities
- **Natural Disasters**: Earthquakes, floods requiring superhero intervention

**Secret Identities**: The classic dichotomy—heroes maintained civilian lives while protecting cities. Managing dual identities created personal drama beyond combat.

**Champions Universe** (later codified): Official setting included:
- **Millennium City**: Primary setting—rebuilt Detroit after alien invasion
- **PRIMUS**: Government superhuman organization (law enforcement)
- **VIPER**: International terrorist organization (primary villain group)
- **UNTIL**: United Nations Tribunal for International Law (UN superhuman response)
- **The Sentinels**: Premier superhero team

Supplements explored various tones:
- **Dark Champions**: Street-level vigilantes, gritty crime fighting
- **Champions Universe**: Four-color superheroics
- **Golden Age Champions**: 1940s-style heroics
- **Future Champions**: Dystopian or utopian futures

**Themes**:
- **Power and Responsibility**: Spider-Man's core lesson—heroes must use power ethically
- **Secret Identity Drama**: Balancing heroic duty with personal life
- **Team Dynamics**: Diverse heroes cooperating despite differences
- **Moral Choice**: Deciding how to use extraordinary power
- **Escalation**: Villain threats requiring increasingly powerful responses
- **Humanity in Costumes**: Exploring human characters behind masks
- **Customization Freedom**: "Build any hero" philosophy

The setting philosophy empowered game masters to create campaigns matching their preferred superhero tone—four-color optimism, grim vigilantes, cosmic opera, street-level crime fighting—using consistent mechanical framework.

## Cultural Impact

Champions' influence extended far beyond superhero gaming:

**Point-Buy Character Creation**: Revolutionized RPG design. GURPS (1986) explicitly built on Champions' foundation. Countless games adopted point-based creation: Hero System derivatives, White Wolf's New World of Darkness, d20 variants, and more.

**Effect-Based Powers**: The separation of mechanical effect from narrative description influenced magic systems, psionics, and abilities design across genres. Games learned to design "what it does" separately from "how it looks."

**Tactical Combat Standards**: Champions' detailed positioning, movement phases, and tactical options influenced superhero games and RPGs generally seeking crunchy tactical play.

**Balance Through Cost**: Demonstrating that mechanical balance could emerge from transparent point economics influenced countless games prioritizing balanced character options.

**Generic System Adaptation**: HERO System's successful adaptation to multiple genres (fantasy, sci-fi, horror, martial arts) proved that unified mechanics could support diverse settings.

**Superhero Gaming Legitimization**: As first commercially successful superhero RPG, Champions established the genre as viable tabletop gaming category, opening market for Marvel Super Heroes, DC Heroes, Aberrant, Mutants & Masterminds.

**Character Optimization Culture**: Champions rewarded system mastery—players who understood point optimization, power construction, and mechanical synergies gained advantages. This fostered character-builder culture that would pervade gaming.

**Mathematical Rigor in Design**: Champions demonstrated that mathematical precision could create balanced, predictable mechanics. This influenced designer expectations about rules clarity and mechanical transparency.

**Community Creativity**: Extensive character conversions (building Marvel/DC heroes in Champions), power discussions, and optimization theorizing created vibrant community culture that prefigured online optimization forums.

**Influence on Video Games**: Champions' point-buy and power customization influenced superhero video games (City of Heroes, Champions Online, various superhero builders) and RPGs with extensive customization.

**Academic Interest**: Game design courses and studies examined Champions as example of point-buy systems, effect-based design, and simulation-oriented game design philosophy.

## Editions and Supplements

**First Edition** (1981): Original boxed set—128 pages establishing core system.

**Second Edition** (1982): Refined rules, expanded content. Quick iteration showed active development.

**Third Edition** (1984): Major expansion—comprehensive rules, extensive power lists, detailed combat. Definitive early edition.

**Fourth Edition** (1989): Standardized presentation, refined balance, expanded supplements. Long-lived standard edition through 1990s.

**Fifth Edition** (2002): Significant overhaul modernizing mechanics, streamlining calculations, expanding options. Mixed reception—some preferred Fourth's familiarity.

**Sixth Edition** (2009): Complete redesign with radically different mathematics and presentation. Most controversial edition—dramatically different from predecessors. Some players embraced modernization; others rejected fundamental changes.

**Major Supplements** (selection):

**Champions Sourcebooks**:
- Various **Enemies books**: Villain collections
- **Champions Universe**: Default setting details
- **Dark Champions**: Street-level vigilantes
- **Golden Age Champions**: 1940s heroics

- **Millennium City**: Primary setting location

**HERO System Applications**:
- **Fantasy Hero**: Fantasy genre adaptation
- **Star Hero**: Science fiction adaptation
- **Dark Champions**: Crime/vigilante genre
- **Pulp Hero**: 1930s pulp adventures
- **Ninja Hero**: Martial arts focus

The extensive supplement line demonstrated sustained commercial viability and creative commitment across 40+ years.

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Unprecedented customization and character-building depth
- Mechanical balance through point-buy
- Comprehensive power coverage
- Tactical combat excellence
- Generic system adaptability
- Transparent, predictable mechanics
- Faithful comic book simulation

**Criticism**:
- Steep learning curve and complexity
- Character creation taking hours
- Combat slowness with multiple powered characters
- Mathematical calculations intimidating
- "System mastery required"—new players at disadvantage
- Analysis paralysis from excessive options
- Later editions' radical changes alienating existing fans

**Community Reception**:

Champions cultivated devoted but divided fanbase:
- **Enthusiasts**: Loved unparalleled customization and mechanical depth
- **Casual Players**: Found complexity barrier to enjoyment
- **Optimizers**: Relished building mechanically superior characters
- **Simulationists**: Appreciated faithful comic book mechanics
- **Storytellers**: Sometimes felt mechanics overshadowed narrative

**Legacy Assessment**:

Champions succeeded extraordinarily as:
- Pioneer of point-buy character creation
- Definitive tactical superhero RPG
- Influential mechanical framework
- Sustained 40+ year publication

It struggled with:
- Accessibility for casual players
- Competition from streamlined alternatives
- Complexity managing market appeal

Despite challenges, Champions demonstrated that mechanically complex, customization-focused games could sustain decades-long success with devoted audiences valuing specific virtues other games didn't provide.
