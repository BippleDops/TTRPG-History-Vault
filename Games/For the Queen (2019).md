# For the Queen

**Publisher**: [[Evil Hat Productions]]
**Designer**: [[Alex Roberts]]
**Year**: 2019
**System**: Story Game (Prompt Cards)
**Genre**: Fantasy, Storytelling
**Status**: in-print

## Overview

For the Queen revolutionized accessible story gaming by distilling collaborative narrative creation into a simple deck of question cards, proving that sophisticated storytelling experiences could emerge from elegant minimalist design. Created by Alex Roberts and published by Evil Hat Productions in 2019, the game asks players to draw cards containing evocative questions, building improvisational narratives about companions traveling with their Queen to peace negotiations that may not succeed. The Queen card lurks in the deck; when drawn, peace talks fail and players must choose whether to save the Queen or let her face consequences.

The game's brilliance lies in its accessibility and emergent depth. Rules fit on a single card. No preparation required. No character sheets, no stats, no GM. Just draw a card, read the question, and answer if you choose—or pass and draw another. Questions like "What do you love most about the Queen?" or "The Queen asked you to do something terrible once. What was it?" build layered characters and relationships entirely through players' improvised answers. By game's end, usually 60-90 minutes later, players have collaboratively created rich narrative about loyalty, duty, betrayal, and difficult choices.

For the Queen sparked a revolution in accessible story gaming, spawning an entire genre of "descended games"—card-based storytelling experiences using similar question-prompt structures for countless genres and themes. Evil Hat's Descended from the Queen license formalized this, creating framework for designers to create their own prompt-card games. Within months, dozens of descended games appeared: For the Drama (teen romance), For the Dungeon (adventurer origins), For My Country (war stories), and hundreds more exploring every conceivable setting and theme.

The game succeeded as remarkable gateway experience. People who found traditional RPGs intimidating discovered For the Queen made storytelling accessible. The simple draw-answer structure removed barriers while supporting sophisticated emergent narratives. Teachers used it for creative storytelling exercises. Therapists employed it for exploring relationships and loyalty. Convention attendees played it as quick engaging one-shots. The game demonstrated that elegant simplicity could create meaningful experiences equal to complex systems.

For the Queen's impact extends beyond its own success into broader cultural phenomenon. It proved card-based oracular storytelling had massive untapped potential. It demonstrated that permissive structures (answer or pass, no wrong answers) could work as well as restrictive ones. It showed that collaborative worldbuilding could happen entirely through questions rather than declarative statements. It created framework so compelling that designers across globe adopted it for their own storytelling visions, making For the Queen not just successful game but generative design template inspiring creative explosion.

## Historical Context

For the Queen emerged from Alex Roberts's extensive experience with story games, improv theater, and accessibility advocacy in the late 2010s. Roberts, already known in indie circles for thoughtful design commentary and pushing for safer, more inclusive gaming practices, wanted to create something radically accessible while maintaining narrative depth.

The late 2010s story game scene had achieved sophistication but sometimes struggled with accessibility. Games like Apocalypse World and Blades in the Dark created rich experiences but required learning moves, stats, and procedures. Even elegant games like Fiasco needed explaining playsets and scene structures. Roberts recognized that many potential players found these entry barriers daunting despite games' relative simplicity compared to traditional RPGs.

The core insight came from improv theater exercises. Improv frequently uses prompts—questions or scenarios triggering spontaneous responses. What if entire game was just prompts? No mechanics to learn, no procedures to memorize—just questions building narrative through accumulated answers. The collaborative storytelling would emerge from how answers interconnected and built on each other.

The oracle card structure drew inspiration from The Quiet Year's card-based prompts and tarot reading traditions. Cards provided randomness (you don't know what question comes next) and materiality (physical deck to shuffle and draw from). Using cards rather than digital prompts created tactile ritual enhancing engagement.

The Queen framework—traveling companions building relationships through questions before crisis forces choice—provided just enough structure without overprescribing. Every game would be about loyalty, duty, and difficult choices, but specific characters, relationships, and circumstances would emerge uniquely each play through answers to questions.

Development involved extensive playtesting across diverse groups. Roberts refined questions relentlessly—which prompts generated interesting answers? Which created productive tension? Which built relationships effectively? Early versions had more explicit rules; final version stripped to essentials. The "answer or pass" permissiveness emerged from recognizing not every question resonates with every player—forcing answers felt uncomfortable, while allowing passes maintained safety and comfort.

The X-Card integration demonstrated Roberts's commitment to safety. Every For the Queen deck includes X-Card instructions, normalizing safety tools for potentially thousands of players who might otherwise never encounter them. This quiet activism embedded safety culture into mainstream game product.

Evil Hat Productions, known for Fate and progressive publishing practices, provided perfect publisher. Their distribution network and marketing reach would expose For the Queen to audiences beyond indie game circles. Their commitment to accessibility and safety aligned with Roberts's values. Their willingness to publish unusual experimental design showed confidence in Roberts's vision.

For the Queen launched at Gen Con 2019 to immediate enthusiastic reception. The simple pitch—"Draw cards, answer questions, tell stories"—communicated instantly. Demo games at the Evil Hat booth generated excitement. Reviewers praised the elegant design and emergent depth. Social media buzz amplified awareness rapidly.

The Descended from the Queen license announcement shortly after release proved transformative. Evil Hat established framework allowing designers to create their own prompt-card games using For the Queen's structure. This open invitation sparked creative explosion. Designers worldwide began creating descended games exploring their interests—horror, romance, comedy, science fiction, historical drama, everything imaginable.

Within months, descended games numbered in dozens. Within a year, hundreds existed. The itch.io platform became descended games repository, with new titles appearing regularly. This explosion demonstrated pent-up demand for accessible design framework enabling creative expression. For the Queen hadn't just created successful game but generative template empowering designers.

The game found multiple audiences simultaneously. Story gamers appreciated it as elegant design. Traditional RPG players discovered it as palate cleanser or gateway for non-gamer friends. Teachers adopted it for creative writing. Therapists explored it for relationship and ethics discussions. Convention organizers scheduled it for one-shots. Actual-play creators featured it for accessible content. This broad appeal expanded For the Queen's influence beyond typical indie game reach.

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated adoption. As gaming moved online, For the Queen's simple structure adapted easily to video calls. Digital implementations appeared. The game's short runtime and minimal components made it perfect for virtual play when more complex games struggled with remote adaptation.

By 2020-2021, For the Queen had become phenomenon. The descended games family numbered in hundreds. The game won awards and critical acclaim. It appeared in mainstream media coverage of modern tabletop gaming. Academic game scholars examined it as example of minimalist design and oracular storytelling. Most importantly, it introduced countless people to story gaming who might never have tried more complex systems.

For the Queen proved that radical simplicity could achieve commercial success, critical recognition, and cultural impact. It demonstrated that removing barriers created access without sacrificing depth. It showed that generative design templates could empower creative communities. It established that safety tools could be normalized through product design. In these respects, For the Queen represents watershed moment in accessible story game design.

## Core Structure and Mechanics

For the Queen's mechanics create emergent storytelling through deceptively simple card-drawing and question-answering procedures.

**Setup**: Players need only the deck of cards and themselves. Optional: tokens or objects for optional rules variants. No character sheets, no dice, no GM prep. Place the deck face-down within reach. That's setup.

**The Basic Turn**: On your turn, draw a card. Read it aloud. The card contains a question about you, the Queen, or the situation. You must either:

1. **Answer the question**: Respond in character, improvising details about your relationship with the Queen, your background, your feelings, your actions. Your answer becomes truth—what you establish is real in the emerging story.

2. **Play an X-Card**: If the question makes you uncomfortable, you can use the X-Card (included safety tool) to pass on that question without explanation.

3. **Pass**: If the question doesn't resonate or you're not ready to answer, pass and draw another card. You can pass as often as needed—no penalty, no judgment.

After answering or passing, play proceeds to the next player. The process continues around the table—draw, answer or pass, next player—building collaborative narrative through accumulated answers.

**The Questions**: Each card contains a question. Questions vary in type and focus:

**Relationship Questions**: "What do you love most about the Queen?" "When did you first meet the Queen?" "The Queen laughed at you once. What about?"

**Background Questions**: "You come from a place very different from here. Describe it." "You carry something that reminds you of home. What is it?"

**Tension Questions**: "The Queen asked you to do something terrible once. What was it?" "You don't trust someone else in this group. Who, and why?"

**Situational Questions**: "We camped somewhere dangerous last night. Where?" "Someone in the group is keeping a secret. What do you suspect it is?"

Questions are open-ended, requiring creative interpretation. They prompt specific character development (background, personality, relationships) while allowing complete creative freedom in answering. No right answers exist—only the story players create.

**The Queen Card**: Buried in the deck (usually near bottom) is the Queen card. When drawn, read its text: "We have arrived. The Queen goes to the negotiation. But something is wrong. We are undone. The Queen is in danger."

Crisis has arrived. Peace talks failed. The Queen faces mortal danger. Each player must now decide: Do you rush to save the Queen, or let her face consequences alone?

Going around the circle, each player declares their choice and narrates what happens. "I rush to her side, drawing my sword against the assassins." Or "I turn away, unable to forgive what she asked me to do." These final declarations resolve the story—does the Queen survive? Is she saved? Abandoned? The ending emerges from players' choices based on relationships built through the game.

After everyone has made their choice and narrated outcome, the game ends. The story is complete.

**Optional Rules**:

**The Oracles**: Players can invoke "the Oracles" (collective group) to add details or answer questions collaboratively when one player feels stuck or wants input.

**Tokens of Affection**: Give physical tokens to other players when their answers move or inspire you. These tokens don't affect mechanics but provide positive feedback and recognition.

**Directed Questions**: Some questions can be directed to specific players rather than answered personally, creating interactive moments.

**That's the Complete System**: Draw card. Read question. Answer or pass. Next player. When Queen card appears, make final choice. The elegant simplicity creates accessibility while emergent answers build sophisticated narratives.

**Emergent Complexity**: Despite minimal mechanics, complex dynamics emerge:

**Building on Prior Answers**: Each answer creates facts others incorporate. If someone establishes "The Queen burned my village," later answers reference that event, creating interconnected narrative tapestry.

**Evolving Feelings**: Early answers might express loyalty: "I would die for the Queen." Later answers might introduce doubts: "She asked me to betray my family." This evolution creates genuine character development and moral complexity.

**Relationship Webs**: Questions about other players create connections. "You don't trust someone. Who?" creates interpersonal tension. "You would follow someone here anywhere. Who?" creates bonds. These player-to-player relationships enrich the narrative beyond just Queen relationships.

**Thematic Accumulation**: Patterns emerge organically. If multiple answers suggest the Queen is ruthless but necessary, that theme develops. If answers emphasize loyalty despite cost, that becomes the story's heart. Themes arise from accumulated choices rather than predetermined design.

**The Climactic Choice**: The final decision—save the Queen or don't—carries weight because preceding cards built investment. You know what the Queen means to you because you've answered questions establishing that relationship. The choice feels earned rather than arbitrary.

**Safety Through Permissiveness**: The pass option and X-Card create safety without explicit content restrictions. If a question prompts discomfort, pass. If content emerges that troubles you, X-Card. This permissive structure prevents forcing anyone into uncomfortable positions while maintaining game flow.

**No Character Creation**: Characters emerge entirely through answers. You don't design a character before play—the character develops through responses to questions. This eliminates prep time while creating organic character development.

**Variable Length**: Games typically run 60-90 minutes depending on:
- How many players (more players = longer game)
- Answer length (detailed vs. concise responses)
- When Queen card appears (shuffled into bottom third, but exact position varies)
- How much discussion happens between answers

**Repeatability**: Because questions are drawn randomly and answers are improvised, every play differs completely. The same group can play multiple times with entirely different stories emerging.

These mechanics create framework elegant enough for complete newcomers yet robust enough for sophisticated emergent storytelling. The simplicity removes barriers; the emergent complexity creates depth.

## Narrative Philosophy and Design Goals

For the Queen's narrative philosophy prioritizes accessibility, emergence, and collaborative truth creation through minimalist elegant design.

**Radical Accessibility**: The design philosophy centers on removing all possible barriers to entry. No rules to learn beforehand—you can explain the game in 30 seconds. No prep time required—open the box and play. No specialized knowledge needed—anyone can answer questions about feelings and relationships. This radical accessibility democratizes storytelling, making narrative gaming available to anyone willing to draw cards and answer questions.

**Emergence Over Planning**: Traditional RPG design often provides extensive mechanical frameworks. For the Queen inverts this—minimal mechanics create space for maximal emergence. The story isn't planned or predetermined. It emerges unpredictably from accumulated answers. This emergent narrative generation creates genuine discovery and surprise—players don't know what story they're telling until they've told it.

**Questions Over Statements**: Rather than declaring facts ("My character is a knight"), players answer questions that reveal facts ("When did you swear your oath to the Queen?"). This question-driven approach feels less authorial and more exploratory. You're discovering your character through reflection prompted by questions rather than creating them through deliberate design.

**Permissiveness as Safety**: The pass mechanic and X-Card integration demonstrate commitment to player safety and comfort. You're never forced to engage with uncomfortable content. This permissiveness creates psychologically safe space where players can take creative risks knowing they can disengage if needed. Safety isn't added-on afterthought but integral design feature.

**Collaborative Truth**: When you answer a question, your answer becomes true. Other players must respect and incorporate your established facts. This collaborative truth-creation requires generosity—building on others' ideas rather than contradicting them. The game only works if everyone commits to "yes, and" principles from improv theater.

**The Power of Constraints**: Paradoxically, the restrictive format (you must answer this specific question or pass) creates freedom. Having infinite possibility can paralyze creativity. The question provides focus—you're not inventing everything from scratch, just answering this specific prompt. The constraint channels creativity productively.

**Delayed Payoff**: The Queen card creates narrative structure—rising tension as relationships develop, climactic crisis when Queen faces danger, resolution through final choices. This structure emerges naturally from the deck's progression without requiring explicit pacing rules. The design builds toward payoff automatically.

**Moral Complexity Through Accumulation**: The game doesn't tell you whether the Queen is good or bad. She's complex, revealed through accumulated answers. Some answers might show her kindness; others her ruthlessness. She becomes morally complex character through players' varied responses, creating genuine ambiguity and difficult final choices.

**No Losers, No Winners**: The game has no victory condition. You're not trying to achieve goals or overcome challenges. You're collectively creating and experiencing a story. This removes competitive pressure and performance anxiety. There's no way to play "wrong" except failing to engage generously with others' contributions.

**Stories About Loyalty**: The framework—companions traveling with their Queen—creates consistent thematic focus. Every For the Queen game explores loyalty, duty, trust, betrayal, and difficult choices. This thematic consistency grounds the design while allowing infinite variation in how themes manifest.

**The Unspoken Queen**: The Queen never speaks. No player portrays her. She exists only through others' descriptions and relationships. This keeps her somewhat mysterious and allows everyone to contribute to her character without anyone owning her. She's collectively created through accumulated perspectives.

**Ritual and Rhythm**: The turn-taking card-drawing creates ritual rhythm—draw, read, answer, pass the deck. This ritualized structure provides comfortable predictability within improvisational uncertainty. You always know what to do (draw a card) even if you don't know what you'll say.

**Physical Engagement**: Using cards creates tactile ritual. Shuffling the deck, drawing cards, passing cards to next player—these physical actions enhance engagement beyond pure dialogue. The materiality makes the experience feel special and intentional.

**Improvisation Accessibility**: Many people find improv intimidating. For the Queen makes improv accessible by providing questions as springboards. You're not improvising from nothing—you're responding to specific prompts. This guided improvisation feels less daunting than freeform creation.

**Optional Depth**: The basic game is extremely simple—draw, answer, pass. Optional rules (Oracles, Tokens of Affection, directed questions) add layers for groups wanting more complexity without burdening newcomers with rules they don't need. This modular complexity accommodates different play preferences.

**Time-Bounded**: Games have natural endpoint (Queen card). You're not committing to ongoing campaign or uncertain duration. You know play will conclude within session, making the game perfect for time-limited contexts—conventions, lunch breaks, first dates, anywhere you have 60-90 minutes.

**Gateway Drug Philosophy**: For the Queen explicitly embraces role as gateway to story gaming. The design prioritizes accessibility over depth because introducing people to narrative gaming matters more than maximizing complexity for experienced players. Once hooked by For the Queen's simplicity, players might explore more complex story games.

**Descended Philosophy**: By creating and licensing the Descended from the Queen framework, Roberts demonstrated commitment to empowering other designers. For the Queen isn't precious or protected—it's generative template meant to inspire iterations. This generosity reflects story game community values—lift each other up, share knowledge, enable creativity.

These philosophical commitments create design that's simultaneously accessible to complete newcomers and satisfying for experienced story gamers. The elegance lies in achieving both goals through radical simplification rather than complicated attempts to serve multiple audiences.

- Templates for creating prompt-card games
- Guidance on question design and structure
- Permission to use "For the [X]" naming convention
- Support for self-publishing descended games

## Play Experience, Safety, and Community Practice

For the Queen creates distinct play experience centered on collaborative improvisation, with safety considerations and community practices enhancing engagement.

**The Play Experience**: For the Queen sessions typically unfold through distinct phases:

**Opening** (5-10 minutes):
- Quick rules explanation (literally 2-3 minutes)
- X-Card introduction and safety discussion
- Optional: Establishing tone and boundaries
- Beginning card draws and tentative first answers

**Building** (30-50 minutes):
- Players finding rhythm and comfort
- Relationships and characters developing through accumulated answers
- Story emerging from interconnected responses
- Emotional investment growing as details accumulate
- Laughter, surprise, tension building naturally

**Crisis** (5-10 minutes):
- Queen card drawn, creating immediate dramatic shift
- Players processing betrayal/danger/crisis
- Reflection on established relationships
- Building toward individual choices

**Resolution** (5-10 minutes):
- Each player declares and narrates their choice
- Story concluding through individual decisions
- Emotional payoffs as choices reflect developed relationships
- Collective witnessing of story's completion

**Debrief** (Optional, 5-15 minutes):
- Processing emotional experience
- Appreciating emergent story
- Discussing surprising moments
- Celebrating collaborative creation

**Emotional Dynamics**: For the Queen can generate genuine emotional experiences:

**Investment**: Answering questions in character creates real investment in relationships and story. Players often report being surprised by how much they care about characters they improvised moments ago.

**Vulnerability**: Some questions prompt genuine vulnerability—sharing feelings, admitting failures, revealing fears. This vulnerability creates intimate shared experience when handled with respect and care.

**Surprise**: Not knowing what questions will emerge creates genuine surprise. Players discover their own characters through answering unexpected prompts.

**Catharsis**: The final choice often creates cathartic moment—declaring loyalty or betrayal after building relationship through play carries emotional weight.

**Safety Considerations and Best Practices**:

**X-Card Integration**: Every deck includes X-Card. Use it:
- X-Card any question making you uncomfortable
- X-Card content emerging in others' answers
- No explanation required—just tap X-Card
- Rewind, skip, or adjust as needed
- X-Card supersedes all game procedures

**Pre-Game Safety Discussion**:
- Establish Lines (content that's off-limits completely)
- Discuss Veils (content handled off-screen/implied)
- Calibrate tone (light comedy vs. dark tragedy)
- Agree on backup safety tools if desired (Script Change, etc.)
- Establish comfort with vulnerability and emotional content

**Common Lines/Veils**:
- Sexual content (usually veiled or lined)
- Graphic violence (often veiled)
- Specific traumas or triggers (individual lines)
- Betrayal intensity (some prefer lighter treatment)
- Political/religious content (depending on group)

**The Pass Mechanic as Safety**: Passing isn't just for irrelevant questions—it's safety valve. If question makes you uncomfortable but not X-Card worthy, pass. No judgment, no explanation needed. This creates low-stakes exit option.

**Consent and Comfort**:
- Never pressure anyone to answer specific question
- Respect passes without comment or pushback
- Check in if someone seems uncomfortable
- Pause game if anyone needs break or discussion
- Prioritize player comfort over "good story"

**Question Sensitivity**: Some questions touch sensitive topics:
- "The Queen asked you to do something terrible" (content warning: may prompt dark responses)
- Questions about trust/betrayal (can create real tension)
- Questions about home/family (may trigger genuine feelings)
- Questions about violence or loss

Being aware of potential sensitivities helps facilitators support players.

**Group Composition Considerations**:

**Ideal Groups**:
- Players comfortable with improvisation (or willing to try)
- Groups trusting each other for vulnerability
- Mixed experience levels work perfectly
- 3-5 players optimal (2 works, 6+ can drag)

**Challenging Groups**:
- Competitive players seeking "winning"
- Players uncomfortable with lack of mechanical structure
- Groups with poor interpersonal dynamics
- Players wanting specific predetermined outcomes

**Facilitation Tips**:

**For Facilitators/Hosts**:
- Model generous engagement with first few answers
- Demonstrate that vulnerability is safe and welcomed
- Build on others' details in your answers
- Celebrate creative and emotional responses
- Manage pacing—encourage concise answers if game drags
- Ensure everyone gets relatively equal speaking time
- Watch for discomfort and check in privately if needed

**Teaching New Players**:
- Explain pass mechanic immediately—reduces anxiety
- Demonstrate answer with first card
- Emphasize no wrong answers
- Encourage building on established details
- Remind them X-Card is always available

**Managing Difficult Situations**:

**Player Dominating**: If someone gives lengthy answers every turn:
- Gently suggest concise responses keep game moving
- Model shorter answers yourself
- Private conversation during break if needed

**Player Contradicting**: If someone contradicts established facts:
- Gentle reminder: "Actually, earlier we established..."
- Encourage finding creative connections instead
- If persistent, private conversation about collaborative truth

**Player Disrupting**: If someone makes joke answers undermining tone:
- Check if group tone needs recalibrating (maybe everyone wants comedy)
- If mismatched, private conversation about tone
- Sometimes better to end game early than force mismatched play

**Uncomfortable Content Emerging**: If content emerges making people uncomfortable:
- Anyone can X-Card, including facilitator
- Pause, discuss what's uncomfortable, adjust
- Rewind if necessary
- End game early if it's not working

**Accessibility Considerations**:

**Physical Access**: Minimal physical demands—reading cards, speaking. Can accommodate:
- Someone else reading cards aloud for vision impaired
- Text-to-speech for digital versions
- Pass-the-deck adapted for mobility needs

**Cognitive Access**:
- Clear straightforward questions
- No complex rules to remember
- Can take time thinking about answers
- Pass option removes pressure

**Social Access**:
- Structured turn-taking reduces social navigation stress
- Clear expectations (answer or pass) reduce ambiguity
- No cross-talk or interruption reduces overwhelm
- Optional debrief for processing

**Emotional Access**:
- Pass and X-Card provide exit options
- Clear boundaries can be established pre-game
- No pressure for "correct" emotional responses
- Space for whatever emotional engagement feels right

**Remote Play Adaptation**: For the Queen works well remotely:
- Video calls for face-to-face connection
- Digital card draws via various tools
- Screen-share for physical deck
- Built-in turn structure prevents cross-talk
- Shorter runtime suits online attention spans

**Post-Game Processing**: Many groups benefit from debrief:
- Share favorite moments or answers
- Discuss surprising story developments
- Process emotional content if needed
- Appreciate collaborative creation
- Clear boundary between game and reality

**Community Best Practices**:

**The Story Game Community** has developed practices around For the Queen:
- Always introduce X-Card and safety tools
- Establish tone and boundaries pre-game
- Model generous collaborative engagement
- Respect passes without comment
- Check in during and after play
- Share credit for collaborative creation
- Process emotions if play got intense

**Convention Play**: At conventions, additional considerations:
- Strangers need extra safety discussion
- Establish tone explicitly since group doesn't know each other
- Be prepared to end early if group doesn't gel
- Extra emphasis on X-Card availability
- Professional friendly facilitation

**Teaching Context**: For educational use:
- Frame learning objectives clearly
- Connect to course material explicitly
- Debrief learning outcomes
- Allow opting out if students uncomfortable
- Consider classroom power dynamics in content

For the Queen's success relies on creating safe, generous collaborative space where improvisation and vulnerability can flourish. When players commit to building on each other's contributions with respect and creativity, the simple card-drawing structure generates profound emergent experiences.
