# Mouse Guard RPG

**Publisher**: [[Archaia Entertainment]]
**Designer**: [[Luke Crane]], based on David Petersen's Mouse Guard
**Year**: 2008
**System**: Simplified Burning Wheel
**Genre**: Anthropomorphic Adventure Fantasy
**Status**: in-print

## Historical Context

Mouse Guard RPG, designed by Luke Crane and published in 2008, adapted David Petersen's beloved comic series into one of gaming's most successful all-ages games by simplifying The Burning Wheel's sophisticated mechanics while maintaining its philosophical core. The result was accessible game capturing the comics' spirit—brave mice protecting their communities from predators, weather, and internal threats while navigating beliefs, instincts, and personal growth.

David Petersen's Mouse Guard comics, launched in 2006, depicted medieval mouse civilization in forests and fields where mice built communities, maintained roads, and protected against natural predators like snakes, owls, and weasels. The comics combined Redwall-style anthropomorphic adventure with sophisticated character development and beautiful artwork. The Guard—elite mice defending mouse territories—served as protagonists in stories balancing action, drama, and ecological realism.

Luke Crane recognized Mouse Guard's gaming potential. The comics' focus on beliefs, duty, and personal growth aligned perfectly with Burning Wheel's philosophy. However, Burning Wheel's complexity would overwhelm the all-ages audience Mouse Guard attracted. Crane needed to drastically simplify while preserving core concepts.

The design challenge was substantial. How do you maintain beliefs-driven play, systematic conflict resolution, and meaningful advancement while making mechanics accessible to children and non-gamers? How do you capture the comics' tone—serious but not grimdark, dangerous but not gratuitously violent, sophisticated but not dense?

Crane's solution was brilliant simplification. Keep beliefs, goals, and instincts but streamline their mechanics. Maintain systematic conflict resolution but reduce steps. Preserve advancement through play but simplify skill improvement. Use smaller dice pools and simpler math. The result was recognizably Burning Wheel philosophy in dramatically more accessible package.

The GM Turn/Player Turn structure proved crucial innovation. Sessions divided into two phases. During GM Turn, the GM framed missions and conflicts challenging the mice. During Player Turn, players pursued personal goals and recovered from mission hardships. This created clear structure while ensuring both mission-driven adventure and character development.

Character creation used simplified lifepaths from Burning Wheel—mice chose backgrounds like Tenderpaw (young guard), Guard Captain, Patrol Leader, or various civilian paths. These provided skills, contacts, and traits. The process took minutes rather than hours while creating distinctive characters.

The conflict system simplified Burning Wheel's Fight! and Duel of Wits into universal conflict resolution handling physical fights, arguments, chases, and survival challenges. All conflicts used same structure—teams scripted three actions, revealed simultaneously, resolved based on action matchups, tracked disposition, and determined victor when one side's disposition reached zero. This provided tactical depth accessible to children.

Publishing through Archaia Entertainment (the comics publisher) rather than Burning Wheel HQ meant different audience—comic fans, not just RPG enthusiasts. This required the game to welcome newcomers while satisfying experienced players. The hardcover's beautiful production, filled with Petersen's artwork, made it gorgeous object that honored the source material.

Mouse Guard RPG won multiple awards including Origins Awards for Best Roleplaying Game and Best Game-Related Publication (2009), plus several ENnie Awards. More importantly, it succeeded as crossover hit—comic fans who'd never played RPGs tried it, parents played with children, experienced gamers enjoyed its streamlined elegance.

The game demonstrated several crucial lessons: sophisticated narrative mechanics could be simplified without losing effectiveness, comic adaptations required capturing themes and tone not just setting, all-ages didn't mean dumbed-down, and Luke Crane could design across complexity spectrum.

Second edition (2015) refined mechanics and incorporated years of feedback while maintaining accessibility. The game's sustained success proved the design's soundness and Mouse Guard's enduring appeal.

## Mechanical Innovations

Mouse Guard simplified Burning Wheel while maintaining philosophical core.

**GM Turn/Player Turn Structure**: Revolutionary session structure. GM Turn: GM frames mission obstacles and conflicts. Player Turn: Players pursue personal goals, recover from mission. This created clear phases ensuring both mission and character focus.

**Beliefs, Goals, Instincts**: Simplified from Burning Wheel but maintaining concept. Belief (what you believe), Goal (session objective), Instinct (automatic reaction). These drove character behavior and earned rewards.

**Fate and Persona**: Metacurrency for rerolls and special actions. Earned through playing beliefs/goals/instincts and through Player Turn activities. Spent for mechanical advantages. This maintained Artha's thematic reinforcement in simpler form.

**Universal Conflict System**: Single conflict resolution for fights, arguments, chases, weather survival, etc. All used same structure:
  - Script three actions from available choices
  - Reveal simultaneously
  - Resolve based on action matchups
  - Track disposition (health/morale/position)
  - Victor determined when opponent reaches zero disposition

This unified diverse conflict types into accessible framework.

**Simplified Dice Pools**: Roll six-sided dice equal to skill plus help. Count successes (4, 5, 6). Compare successes to obstacle or opposition. This straightforward resolution required minimal math.

**Helping**: Mice could help each other, adding dice to rolls. This encouraged cooperation and reflected Guard's teamwork emphasis.

**Conditions**: Instead of complex injury tracking, mice suffered conditions—Hungry, Thirsty, Tired, Injured, Sick, Angry. Each imposed penalties. This simplified status tracking while maintaining mechanical impact.

**Traits**: Personality traits provided mechanical benefits when relevant and complications when working against you. "Stubborn" helps when resisting influence, hinders when needing flexibility. This made personality mechanically meaningful.

**Skills**: Simplified skill list focusing on activities relevant to mice—Pathfinder, Nature, Fighter, Persuader, Scout, Weather Watcher, etc. Each rated 2-6, determining dice pool.

**Nature**: Special rating representing how mouse-like you act. Acting according to mouse nature (hiding, foraging, climbing, escaping) used Nature. Success reduced Nature; failure increased it. This created interesting tension between civilization and animal instinct.

**Advancement**: Skills improved through use—pass tests to mark skill. Earn enough marks to increase rating. This maintained learning-through-play while simplifying tracking.

**Circles (Contacts)**: Social network rating for finding helpful mice. Need to know someone? Test Circles. Success means you have contact. This abstracted social connections.

**Resources**: Wealth and supply abstraction. Test Resources to acquire items. This eliminated inventory management.

**Wises**: Special knowledge areas (Guard-wise, Weasel-wise, etc.) providing situational bonuses and information. These represented specialized expertise.

**Versus Tests**: Simple opposed rolls for quick resolution when full conflict wasn't needed.

**Teamwork Emphasis**: Multiple mechanics encouraged cooperation—helping, granting advantage, working together. This reflected comics' teamwork themes.

## Cultural Impact

Mouse Guard's cultural impact operated through accessibility, crossover success, and demonstrating Burning Wheel's adaptability.

**All-Ages Success**: Proved sophisticated mechanics could be family-friendly. Parents played with children. This expanded RPG audiences beyond typical demographics.

**Comic Crossover**: Successfully brought comic fans to RPGs. Many bought it as Mouse Guard fans, discovered RPGs through it. This crossover demonstrated comics' RPG adaptation potential.

**Burning Wheel Accessibility**: Made Crane's design philosophy accessible to broader audiences. Players intimidated by Burning Wheel could try Mouse Guard, then potentially graduate to BW.

**GM/Player Turn Adoption**: The turn structure influenced subsequent games using similar GM/player phase divisions. This structured approach appeared in various forms across design space.

**Simplification Without Loss**: Demonstrated that sophisticated systems could be drastically simplified while preserving core philosophy. This influenced designers seeking accessible versions of complex games.

**Beautiful Production**: The hardcover's gorgeous production raised standards for comic adaptations. Games based on comics should honor source material's visual quality.

**Awards Success**:
- Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Game (2009)
- Origins Award for Best Game-Related Publication (2009)
- Multiple ENnie Awards
- Critical acclaim across gaming media
- Recognition beyond RPG enthusiast circles

**Convention Family Gaming**: Became popular convention option for families and all-ages gaming. This expanded con demographics.

**Educational Use**: Teachers and librarians used it for creative storytelling and collaboration exercises. The all-ages nature made it suitable for educational contexts.

**Luke Crane's Range**: Demonstrated Crane could design across complexity spectrum—from Burning Wheel's depth to Mouse Guard's accessibility.

**Torchbearer Bridge**: Mouse Guard's simplification approaches influenced Torchbearer's adaptation of BW for dungeon crawling.

## Design Philosophy

Mouse Guard's philosophy balanced accessibility with sophisticated narrative mechanics.

**Beliefs Drive Stories**: Even in simplified form, character beliefs drove play. This maintained BW's core philosophy while making it accessible.

**Cooperation Matters**: The game emphasized teamwork through helping mechanics, group conflicts, and thematic focus. Mice survived through cooperation.

**Structure Supports Play**: GM Turn/Player Turn structure created clear framework reducing GM burden and ensuring balanced focus.

**Simplified Not Dumbed Down**: Accessibility didn't mean lack of depth. The game maintained meaningful choices, tactical resolution, and character development.

**Honor Source Material**: Every design choice served capturing comics' spirit. Mechanics reinforced themes of duty, belief, cooperation, and mouse-scale adventure.

**Nature Tension**: The Nature mechanic created interesting philosophical tension—remain true to animal nature or embrace civilization? This thematic element came from comics.

**Failure Drives Story**: Like Burning Wheel, failed tests created complications and advancement opportunities. Failure wasn't dead end but story driver.

**All-Ages Appropriate**: Design consciously avoided excessive violence, mature themes, or complex moral ambiguity while maintaining sophisticated gameplay.

**Accessibility Through Simplification**: Streamlined everything that could be simplified while preserving elements essential to intended experience.

**Player Turn Ensures Character**: By structuring player-focused time into session, design guaranteed character development wasn't lost to mission focus.

## Setting and Themes

Mouse Guard's setting came directly from Petersen's comics—mouse civilization in dangerous natural world.

**The Territories**: Scattered mouse settlements across forested territories. Mice built towns (Lockhaven, Barkstone, Copperwood, etc.), maintained roads, traded goods, and lived civilized lives despite being prey animals.

**The Guard**: Elite mice protecting territories. Guards maintained roads, defended settlements, delivered mail, mediated disputes, and handled threats. Guard service was honor and duty.

**Predators**: Natural threats included snakes, owls, weasels, foxes, and other predators. These were genuinely dangerous—mice were prey animals. Combat with predators was desperate survival.

**Weather and Seasons**: Harsh weather (snow, rain, heat) created obstacles as dangerous as predators. Mice struggled against elements constantly.

**Mouse Communities**: Various settlements with distinct characters. Lockhaven (Guard headquarters), Barkstone (mining town), Sprucetuck (grain producer), etc. Each had personality and problems.

**Internal Conflicts**: Not all threats were external. Political disputes, criminal mice, ambitious leaders, and factional conflicts created drama without predators.

**The Territories' Geography**: Familiar locations transformed to mouse scale—hollow logs became passages, roots created obstacles, streams were rivers, leaves provided shelter.

**Seasonal Campaigns**: Adventures often followed seasonal structure—Spring patrol, Summer's heat, Fall harvest, Winter's hardship. This mirrored comics' seasonal storylines.

**Themes**:
- **Duty and Service**: Guards sacrificed for greater good
- **Cooperation**: Survival required working together
- **Courage**: Facing predators despite being prey
- **Community**: Protecting mouse civilization
- **Nature's Challenges**: Weather and predators as constant threats
- **Beliefs vs. Duty**: Personal convictions versus Guard obligations
- **Scale**: Mouse-scale world where small is dangerous
- **Seasons**: Natural cycles affecting everything

**Tone**: Serious but hopeful. Dangerous but not grimdark. Characters faced real threats but worked together to overcome them. This balanced accessibility with genuine stakes.

## Reception and Legacy

Mouse Guard achieved critical and commercial success while expanding gaming audiences.

**Critical Acclaim**:
- Origins Award Best RPG (2009)
- Origins Award Best Game-Related Publication (2009)
- ENnie Gold for Best Rules (2009)
- ENnie Gold for Best Production Values (2009)
- ENnie Silver for Best Game (2009)
- Widespread praise from critics
- Recognition in mainstream and gaming media

**Commercial Success**:
- Strong sales across multiple years
- Second edition (2015) demonstrated sustained interest
- International translations
- Consistent convention presence
- Box set release expanding accessibility
- Maintained in-print status

**Crossover Audience**:
- Comic fans bought as Mouse Guard merchandise
- Parents seeking family RPGs
- Teachers and librarians for educational use
- New players using it as gateway to RPGs
- Experienced gamers enjoying streamlined BW

**Community Response**:
- Active player community
- Extensive fan content and scenarios
- Strong convention and organized play presence
- Actual-play shows demonstrating family play
- Online resources and teaching materials

**Educational Impact**: Used in schools and libraries for:
- Creative writing exercises
- Collaborative storytelling
- Problem-solving skills
- Teamwork emphasis
- Reading comprehension (comics + game)

**Second Edition**: 2015 revision refined mechanics, clarified rules, improved usability while maintaining core experience. This demonstrated committed support.

**Box Set**: Comprehensive box set with dice, cards, and materials made game even more accessible to newcomers.

**Actual-Play Success**: Featured in family-friendly actual-plays showing intergenerational play. This visibility attracted new audiences.

**Design Influence**: Influenced games seeking:
- Simplified versions of complex systems
- Comic adaptation approaches
- All-ages sophisticated gameplay
- GM/Player turn structures
- Simplified Burning Wheel mechanics

**Criticisms**:
- Some found it too simple compared to Burning Wheel
- All-ages focus limited mature themes
- Nature mechanic confused some players
- Turn structure felt rigid initially
- Limited setting scope for long campaigns
- Comic purists wanted more setting detail

**Luke Crane's Portfolio**: Demonstrated his design range and established him as designer capable of accessibility without sacrificing sophistication.

**Long-term Impact**: Remained popular years after release. New players continued discovering it. Comic fans tried RPGs through it. Families enjoyed playing together.

**Legacy**: Mouse Guard proved sophisticated mechanics could be family-friendly, that comic adaptations could succeed by capturing tone and themes, that all-ages didn't mean simplistic, and that Burning Wheel philosophy worked across complexity levels.

The game's greatest achievement was making narrative-focused gaming accessible to audiences who'd never try Burning Wheel while creating genuinely engaging experience for veteran gamers. It demonstrated that simplification could enhance rather than diminish, that structure supported rather than restricted creativity, and that games could be sophisticated and accessible simultaneously.

By honoring Petersen's beautiful comics while applying Crane's design philosophy, Mouse Guard created something rare—a crossover success introducing new audiences to RPGs while satisfying experienced players with elegant mechanics and meaningful choices.
