# Advanced Dungeons & Dragons Second Edition

**Publisher**: [[TSR]]
**Designer**: [[David "Zeb" Cook]]
**Year**: 1989
**System**: AD&D 2nd Edition
**Genre**: Fantasy
**Status**: Out-of-print

## Historical Context

Advanced Dungeons & Dragons Second Edition emerged from one of the most turbulent periods in TSR's history. Released in 1989, AD&D 2E represented a cautious revision of the First Edition rules, modernizing presentation and addressing cultural controversies while maintaining essential compatibility with the vast library of First Edition material.

The context of Second Edition's development shaped every design decision. Gary Gygax, AD&D's creator and TSR's public face, had been forced out of the company in 1985 following corporate maneuvering by Lorraine Williams, who gained controlling interest in TSR. Williams, granddaughter of Buck Rogers creator John F. Dille, had little connection to gaming culture but possessed sharp business acumen and authoritarian management style.

Without Gygax, TSR needed to demonstrate that AD&D could continue without its creator. Simultaneously, the company faced existential threats from multiple directions. The Satanic Panic of the 1980s had damaged D&D's reputation, with schools banning gaming clubs, parents burning books, and religious organizations condemning the game as occult indoctrination. Patricia Pulling's BADD (Bothered About Dungeons & Dragons) organization actively campaigned against TSR, threatening boycotts and legal action.

The 60 Minutes segment on D&D, the Mazes and Monsters TV movie, and countless local news stories portrayed the game as psychologically dangerous. Parents associated D&D with teenage suicide, occultism, and antisocial behavior. TSR faced pressure to sanitize content, removing or renaming potentially offensive material.

Commercially, TSR struggled with bloat and mismanagement. The company had expanded beyond gaming into novels, magazines, miniatures, and even toys and puzzles. Many ventures lost money. Distribution problems and retail relationships suffered. The Random House distribution deal, intended to expand market reach, created more problems than solutions.

First Edition's supplement proliferation created confusion. Unearthed Arcana, Oriental Adventures, Wilderness Survival Guide, Dungeoneer's Survival Guide, and dozens of other books added rules without coherent organization. Players couldn't determine what counted as "official." Tournament organizers struggled with standardization. New players faced overwhelming complexity.

Into this chaos stepped David "Zeb" Cook, veteran TSR designer who had created Expert D&D and numerous classic modules. TSR tasked Cook with updating AD&D for the 1990s while navigating political, cultural, and commercial constraints. The resulting Second Edition walked a delicate line between innovation and conservation, modernization and tradition.

The Player's Handbook appeared in February 1989, followed by the Dungeon Master Guide (notably losing the possessive apostrophe) later that year. The Monstrous Compendium replaced the Monster Manual with a loose-leaf binder format allowing expansion through supplementary sheets—an innovation that proved unpopular and was abandoned for the compiled Monstrous Manual in 1993.

Second Edition launched without Gygax's involvement or blessing. He publicly criticized the new edition as unnecessary, commercially motivated, and inferior to First Edition. Many longtime players agreed, creating an edition war that prefigured later Third/Fourth Edition conflicts. Others welcomed the cleaned-up presentation and streamlined rules.

TSR's marketing positioned Second Edition as compatible with First Edition material. Adventures, settings, and supplements could work with both editions with minimal conversion. This backward compatibility preserved TSR's extensive product catalog while enabling new releases to support both editions simultaneously.

The edition coincided with campaign setting proliferation. Forgotten Realms dominated sales, but Dragonlance, Greyhawk, Ravenloft, Dark Sun, Planescape, Birthright, and numerous other settings each received extensive support. This diversification spread development resources thin but created variety for players tired of traditional fantasy.

Second Edition's decade-long run (1989-2000) saw TSR's highest creative output and eventual financial collapse. The edition produced countless memorable products—Planescape's philosophical planar exploration, Dark Sun's post-apocalyptic survival, Ravenloft's gothic horror—while corporate mismanagement drove TSR toward bankruptcy.

The Player's Option series (Combat & Tactics, Skills & Powers, Spells & Magic) in the mid-1990s created advanced rules for campaign customization. These books allowed granular character building, point-buy systems, and tactical combat options—essentially creating "AD&D 2.5" for groups wanting more complexity.

By the late 1990s, Second Edition showed its age. Computer RPGs demonstrated what systematic, unified mechanics could achieve. Magic: The Gathering (also published by Wizards of the Coast) revolutionized game design with elegant, modular rules. Players increasingly recognized AD&D's inconsistencies, baroque subsystems, and accumulated cruft.

When TSR entered bankruptcy in 1997, Wizards of the Coast acquired the company primarily for the D&D brand. Wizards immediately began developing Third Edition, ending Second Edition's run after eleven years—the longest-running D&D edition until Fifth Edition surpassed it.

## Mechanical Innovations and Changes

Second Edition refined First Edition's mechanics while maintaining essential compatibility:

### Core Resolution

AD&D 2E maintained First Edition's core resolution: d20 for attacks and saves, ability checks rolling under ability score, skill checks using varying dice based on difficulty. The edition explicitly codified THAC0 (To Hit Armor Class 0), formalizing attack roll calculations: d20 + modifiers ≥ THAC0 - target AC = hit.

THAC0, while mathematically identical to First Edition's attack tables, created easier mental math. A fighter with THAC0 15 attacking AC 0 needed 15+. Against AC -3, needed 12+. Against AC 5, needed 20. The descending AC system remained counterintuitive, but THAC0 simplified calculations.

### Character Creation

Character creation followed First Edition's structure with refinements:

**Ability Score Generation**: The standard 3d6 method continued, with numerous alternative methods suggested (4d6 drop lowest, arrange to taste, point buy). The DMG acknowledged different campaigns needed different power levels, legitimizing varied generation methods.

**Racial Ability Adjustments**: Races gained fixed ability score modifiers rather than First Edition's minimum/maximum requirements. Dwarves gained +1 Constitution, -1 Charisma. This simplified character creation while maintaining racial distinctiveness.

**Class Requirements**: Minimum ability scores still restricted class access, though requirements generally decreased slightly. Paladins still required exceptional abilities, but rangers and druids became more accessible.

### Class System Revisions

Second Edition reorganized classes into four groups:

**Warrior Group**: Fighter, Paladin, Ranger. All warriors shared combat progression, hit dice (d10), and weapon/armor proficiency.

**Wizard Group**: Mage (replacing Magic-User nomenclature), Specialist Wizards (focused on specific schools of magic). Illusionist became a specialist wizard rather than separate class.

**Priest Group**: Cleric and Druid as distinct classes, with extensive rules for designing deity-specific priest classes with custom spell access and abilities.

**Rogue Group**: Thief and Bard. The convoluted First Edition Bard (requiring advancement in Fighter and Thief first) became a standard class, making bards actually playable.

### Proficiency System

Non-weapon proficiencies, optional in First Edition, became core Second Edition feature:

**Weapon Proficiencies**: Characters gained proficiency slots determining weapons they could use effectively. Using non-proficient weapons incurred attack penalties. Specialists could gain weapon mastery, improving attack and damage with favored weapons.

**Non-Weapon Proficiencies**: Skills like Riding, Survival, Reading/Writing, languages, and craft skills gave characters defined capabilities beyond class abilities. Proficiency checks rolled d20 under relevant ability score plus proficiency modifier.

The proficiency system added character customization and clarified what characters could attempt. However, proficiency proliferation across supplements created complexity rivaling later editions' feat systems.

### Combat Refinements

Combat followed First Edition procedures with clarifications:

**Initiative**: Simplified to d10 each side, with weapon speed as tiebreaker. This streamlined First Edition's complicated individual initiative system while retaining tactical weapon choices.

**Attacks of Opportunity**: Not systematically implemented, but backstab, flanking, and positioning rules created tactical considerations.

**Called Shots**: Optional rules for targeting specific body parts or attempting disarms added tactical depth.

**Combat Maneuvers**: Wrestling, overbearing, and unarmed combat received detailed procedures, though few groups used these complicated subsystems.

### Revised Saving Throws

Second Edition maintained five saves but reorganized categories:

- Paralyzation, Poison, or Death Magic
- Rod, Staff, or Wand
- Petrification or Polymorph
- Breath Weapon
- Spell

Save progressions by class remained similar to First Edition, with numerical values declining (improving) with level. The system's logic—why does paralysis group with poison?—remained obscure, but familiarity outweighed rationalization.

### Magic System Changes

The spell system continued Vancian memorization with notable changes:

**Spell Schools**: Specialist wizards focused on specific schools (Evocation, Necromancy, etc.), gaining bonus spells and improved saving throw DCs in their specialty while barred from opposition schools. This encouraged magical specialization beyond generic wizard.

**Reversible Spells**: Continued from First Edition, with alignment restricting good/evil versions.

**Clerical Spheres**: Rather than all clerics accessing all cleric spells, deity-specific priests received Major and Minor access to thematic spell spheres (Healing, Combat, Protection, etc.). A war god's priest gained different spells than a nature goddess's priest, encouraging divine differentiation.

**Spell Research**: Continued from First Edition, allowing wizards to create custom spells through gold investment and research time.

### Multi-Class and Dual-Class

The controversial multi-class/dual-class distinction persisted:

**Multi-Class** (demi-humans only): Characters advanced simultaneously in 2-3 classes, splitting experience between them. Hit points averaged across classes. All class abilities available simultaneously.

**Dual-Class** (humans only): Characters completely switched classes, abandoning previous class abilities until the new class exceeded old class level. This complicated system frustrated players but maintained First Edition compatibility.

### Experience and Advancement

XP awards balanced treasure and combat, with individual class XP requirements determining advancement speed. Prime requisite bonuses (extra XP for high ability scores) rewarded optimized ability placement.

The edition introduced story awards and individual XP awards for good roleplaying, encouraging DMs to reward characterization beyond treasure hunting.

### Level Limits and Racial Restrictions

Controversial level limits for demi-humans persisted, though maximum levels increased slightly. Dwarven fighters could reach level 15 (up from First Edition's level 12), elven wizards level 20, but humans remained unlimited. This restriction, defended as maintaining human dominance in human-centric settings, increasingly frustrated players wanting to play long-term demi-human characters.

## Response to Satanic Panic

Second Edition's most visible changes addressed moral panic concerns:

### Demons and Devils Renamed

Demons became "tanar'ri"; devils became "baatezu." The Monster Manual avoided the words "demon" and "devil" entirely, using descriptive planar taxonomy. Succubi became "succubi demons" in First Edition became "tanar'ri tempters" in Second Edition.

This transparent dodge satisfied no one. Critics recognized the renaming as superficial. Players found the new terms awkward and unnecessary. Eventually, Third Edition would restore original terminology.

### Assassination Restrictions

The Assassin class disappeared entirely. The Complete Book of Villains and other supplements later provided assassin rules, but the PHB contained no evil classes.

### Half-Orcs Removed

Half-orcs, implied products of human-orc sexual violence, were removed from core races. They returned in supplementary material but remained absent from the PHB.

### Toned-Down Art and Presentation

Gone were First Edition's potentially satanic or occult-seeming covers. Second Edition featured cleaner, more heroic artwork emphasizing adventure over darkness. Interior art avoided pentagrams, religious symbols, or anything potentially controversial.

### Alignment Emphasis

Good and evil alignment received increased emphasis, positioning D&D as morality-affirming rather than morally ambiguous. Heroes fought evil; evil was identifiable and defeatable.

These changes represented commercial capitulation to pressure groups rather than design philosophy. The Satanic Panic was already waning by 1989; Second Edition's sanitization came late and accomplished little beyond irritating longtime fans.

## Player Options Line

The mid-1990s Complete Handbooks and Player's Option series dramatically expanded Second Edition:

### Complete Handbooks

The Complete Fighter's Handbook, Complete Wizard's Handbook, Complete Priest's Handbook, and others provided extensive character options:

**Kits**: Character templates providing thematic packages of abilities, restrictions, and roleplaying guidance. The Berserker kit modified fighters into rage-driven warriors. The Duelist kit created swashbuckling fighters. Dozens of kits per class created unprecedented character variety.

Kits enabled character concepts impossible with base classes. A specialty priest of a specific deity, a gladiator fighter, an academician wizard—kits formalized these archetypes with mechanical support.

**Expanded Rules**: Each handbook included optional rules, equipment, spells, and setting material relevant to its class.

**Campaign Integration**: Advice on integrating each class into campaigns, handling class-specific challenges, and creating class-focused adventures.

The Complete Handbooks series eventually covered every class, several races, settings, and topics (Complete Book of Necromancers, Complete Book of Elves), creating a vast expansion of Second Edition's scope.

### Player's Option Series

The Player's Option books (Combat & Tactics, Skills & Powers, Spells & Magic) offered advanced customization:

**Skills & Powers**: Point-buy character creation allowing players to customize classes by purchasing specific abilities. Want a fighter with thief skills? Spend points on those abilities. This system essentially created classless AD&D, breaking the rigid class structure.

**Combat & Tactics**: Extensive tactical combat rules with facing, reach weapons, critical hits, armor by location, and detailed maneuver options. These rules transformed combat into tactical miniatures battles.

**Spells & Magic**: Spell point alternatives to memorization, custom spell design, and expanded spell options gave casters flexibility beyond Vancian restrictions.

The Player's Option line created "AD&D 2.5"—drastically different gameplay from core Second Edition. Groups using all Player's Option rules played nearly a different game, with point-buy characters, tactical combat, and flexible magic.

### Proliferation Problems

The supplement proliferation created unsustainable complexity. By the late 1990s, dozens of Complete Handbooks, setting-specific rulebooks, and Player's Option volumes meant no two groups played the same game. Determining "legal" material for characters became contentious.

Tournament and organized play struggled with standardization. The explosion of options, while creatively rich, made the game unwieldy and fragmented the player base.

## Campaign Setting Proliferation

Second Edition saw unprecedented setting development:

### Forgotten Realms Dominance

Ed Greenwood's detailed world became TSR's flagship setting. Extensive support—dozens of sourcebooks, hundreds of novels, computer games—made the Realms D&D's default setting for many players. The Realms emphasized high magic, established NPCs (Elminster, Drizzt Do'Urden), and interconnected metaplot.

### Dragonlance's Ongoing Story

Continued from First Edition, Dragonlance maintained its novel-driven storytelling approach. The setting's metaplot advanced through novels and adventures, creating shared narrative experience across the fan base.

### Ravenloft's Gothic Horror

Expanded from the classic module, Ravenloft created gothic horror campaigns in domains of dread ruled by dark lords. The setting blended horror themes with D&D mechanics, creating unique tonal experience.

### Dark Sun's Post-Apocalypse

Desert world with depleted magic, brutal survival, gladiatorial combat, and psionics as common power source. Dark Sun's harsh tone and metal-scarce economy created distinctly different gameplay from traditional fantasy.

### Planescape's Philosophical Planes

The Great Wheel cosmology became a campaign setting focused on planar travel, philosophical factions, and the multiverse's nature. Planescape's distinctive slang (berk, barmy, the chant) and philosophical themes attracted mature players seeking thoughtful campaigns.

### Birthright's Domain Management

Unique setting where PCs ruled kingdoms, managed domain economics, and engaged in warfare and diplomacy at strategic scale. Birthright integrated domain rules into character advancement.

### Al-Qadim, Spelljammer, and Others

Arabian Nights-inspired Al-Qadim, fantasy space travel Spelljammer, and other settings demonstrated D&D's versatility across genres and themes.

### Setting Bloat

The proliferation of settings spread TSR's resources thin. Each setting required sourcebooks, adventures, novels, and accessories to remain viable. Development costs increased while each setting's audience remained limited. This proliferation contributed to TSR's financial problems.

## Decline and Third Edition Transition

Second Edition's final years revealed systemic problems:

### Financial Collapse

TSR's financial mismanagement—overprinting, retail returns, failed Buck Rogers line, distribution problems, executive excess—drove the company toward bankruptcy. By 1997, TSR faced insolvency despite D&D's cultural presence.

### Market Changes

The RPG market contracted in the late 1990s. Magic: The Gathering and other collectible card games absorbed gaming dollars. Computer RPGs offered sophisticated experiences rivaling tabletop play. Internet gaming, MUDs, and early MMORPGs competed for player attention.

### Design Stagnation

Second Edition's conservative approach meant a decade without fundamental innovation. While supplements added content, core design remained rooted in 1970s paradigms. Other games explored narrative mechanics, unified resolution, and modern design philosophies while D&D maintained traditions.

### Edition Dissatisfaction

Players increasingly recognized Second Edition's quirks as design flaws rather than charming eccentricities. THAC0 confused newcomers. Descending AC defied intuition. Inconsistent subsystems frustrated systematically-minded players. Save categories made no logical sense.

The Player's Option series demonstrated both demand for innovation and Second Edition's inability to integrate that innovation into core design.

### Wizards Acquisition

When Wizards of the Coast acquired bankrupt TSR in 1997, they inherited D&D but also its problems. Wizards immediately began developing Third Edition, recruiting Jonathan Tweet, Monte Cook, and Skip Williams to completely redesign the game.

### Community Split

Second Edition's end created community division. Some players welcomed Third Edition's innovations. Others felt betrayed by radical changes to beloved systems. Many continued playing Second Edition despite Third Edition's release.

The Old School Renaissance movement decades later would celebrate Second Edition alongside earlier editions, creating retroclones like For Gold & Glory that kept Second Edition's approach alive under the OGL.

## Cultural Impact and Legacy

Second Edition's cultural impact, while significant, operated in First Edition's shadow:

### Sustained Market Presence

Despite financial problems, Second Edition maintained D&D's market leadership throughout the 1990s. The game remained synonymous with fantasy roleplaying, introducing millions to the hobby.

### Novel Lines

The Forgotten Realms and Dragonlance novel lines reached audiences far beyond players. R.A. Salvatore's Drizzt books became bestsellers, introducing D&D lore to readers who might never play.

### Computer Game Adaptations

Baldur's Gate (1998) adapted Second Edition rules to computer RPG format, creating one of gaming's most beloved franchises. Pool of Radiance, Eye of the Beholder, and other adaptations brought D&D to digital audiences.

### Setting Creativity

Second Edition's diverse settings demonstrated D&D's versatility. Planescape's philosophical depth, Dark Sun's post-apocalyptic harshness, and Ravenloft's gothic horror showed the system could support varied tones and themes.

### Community Resilience

Despite corporate chaos, the player community remained vibrant. Convention attendance, organized play, internet forums, and local game stores maintained community connection.

### Design Lessons

Second Edition taught important lessons about edition transitions, backward compatibility, and managing established brands. Its cautious approach satisfied no one completely—too conservative for innovation-seekers, too changed for tradition-lovers. Third Edition would embrace radical change rather than incremental revision, learning from Second Edition's middle-path limitations.

## Design Philosophy

Second Edition's design philosophy reflected competing pressures:

### Conservative Revision

The edition aimed to update without revolutionizing. Maintain First Edition compatibility while improving presentation and organization. This conservatism preserved existing investment but limited innovation.

### Cultural Accommodation

Responding to Satanic Panic required compromises: removing controversial content, emphasizing heroism, sanitizing presentation. These changes satisfied commercial pressures rather than design vision.

### Expanded Customization

The proficiency system, kits, and eventual Player's Option books moved toward character customization. This anticipated later editions' emphasis on character building and personalization.

### Setting Diversity

Supporting multiple campaign settings created variety but fragmented focus. Rather than one definitive D&D experience, Second Edition offered many parallel versions.

### Accessibility vs. Complexity

The PHB aimed for accessibility, simplifying First Edition's presentation. But supplements added complexity, eventually creating overwhelming option bloat.

## Reception

Second Edition received mixed reception reflecting its compromises:

**Critical Response**: Gaming magazines praised improved organization and presentation while noting limited innovation. Reviews acknowledged commercial necessities but wished for bolder design.

**Commercial Performance**: Strong initial sales and sustained market leadership demonstrated commercial success, though not enough to save TSR from financial problems caused by broader mismanagement.

**Player Division**: Edition warriors debated Second Edition's merits, with some embracing improvements while others saw unnecessary changes to functional systems. This prefigured later edition war intensity.

**Longtime Player Skepticism**: Many First Edition veterans saw no reason to switch, continuing their campaigns with familiar rules. Second Edition struggled to convince satisfied customers they needed a new edition.

**New Player Adoption**: For those discovering D&D in the 1990s, Second Edition provided entry point into a vast campaign worlds and rich gaming culture.
