# Alice is Missing

**Publisher**: [[Hunters Entertainment]]
**Designer**: Spenser Starke
**Year**: 2020
**System**: Story Game (Silent Discord Mystery)
**Genre**: Mystery, Modern Horror
**Status**: in-print

## Overview

Alice is Missing revolutionized story gaming by creating a completely silent, text-only mystery experience played through group chats, generating emotionally devastating narratives about small-town tragedy, teenage friendship, and the desperate search for a missing girl. Designed by Spenser Starke and published by Hunters Entertainment in 2020, the game asks 3-5 players to portray friends of Alice Briarwood, who vanished without a trace from the quiet town of Silent Falls. Over exactly 90 minutes in real-time, players communicate solely through text messages—no voice, no video—drawing cards that provide clues, prompts, and revelations while a curated playlist marks time's inexorable passage toward discovering what happened to Alice.

The game's audacious design choice—enforced silence during play—creates uniquely haunting experience. Players sit together (or connect remotely) but communicate only through group chat and private messages. This constraint generates profound effects: the weight of choosing words carefully, the vulnerability of revealing emotions through text, the isolation of being physically present but communicatively distant, the mounting dread as time runs out. The Spotify playlist that accompanies play adds emotional resonance, its 13 tracks each representing roughly 7 minutes, creating visible countdown to inevitable discovery.

Alice is Missing succeeded as landmark in emotional storytelling through games. The silent text-based structure creates space for reflection impossible in verbal improv. Players compose thoughtful responses rather than improvising immediately, leading to more deliberate, often more vulnerable sharing. The enforced 90-minute runtime creates genuine urgency—you must discover what happened before the final track ends. The card-based clue system ensures satisfying mystery structure while allowing interpretive freedom. The curated soundtrack enhances atmosphere without requiring GM narration.

The game released during COVID-19 pandemic lockdowns, finding perfect timing for remote play experiences. Discord-native design meant it worked flawlessly online when most games struggled with virtual adaptation. The emotional depth and innovative structure created viral word-of-mouth, with players sharing profound experiences across social media. The game won multiple awards, inspired numerous actual-play streams, and demonstrated that experimental structures could achieve both critical acclaim and commercial success.

Alice is Missing proved that radical formal constraints—silence, text-only, real-time countdown—could create emergence rather than limitation. It showed that games could achieve genuine emotional devastation through thoughtful design. It demonstrated that mystery structure could work in collaborative GM-less context. Most importantly, it expanded what story games could be, proving that formal innovation and emotional resonance could combine into experiences impossible in traditional RPG formats.

## Historical Context

Alice is Missing emerged from Spenser Starke's fascination with true crime podcasts, missing person cases, and the emotional dynamics of searching for lost loved ones. Starke, already active in indie RPG design circles, wanted to create something capturing the mounting dread, desperate hope, and inevitable tragedy of real missing person investigations while maintaining respectful distance through fictional framing.

The late 2010s saw increasing interest in horror and emotional storytelling in story games. Games like Ten Candles created tragic inevitability through mechanics. For the Queen demonstrated card-based prompts generating narratives. The Quiet Year showed how constraints (silence, specific procedures) could enhance rather than limit play. Starke synthesized these influences while adding distinctive innovations.

The silent text-based concept arose from recognizing how different text communication feels from verbal. Text creates space for thought, allows editing before sending, generates documentary record, and paradoxically enables both distance and intimacy. What if entire game happened through text? What if players sat together in silence, connected only through screens? This would create unsettling mirror of modern communication—physically proximate but digitally mediated—while serving mystery narrative perfectly.

The 90-minute real-time structure solved pacing problems elegantly. Mystery games often struggle with pacing—too fast and revelations feel rushed, too slow and tension dissipates. Locking to exact 90 minutes with soundtrack creating visible countdown generated inexorable pressure. The Spotify playlist (carefully curated by Starke) provided atmospheric enhancement and temporal structure without requiring rules or GM management.

Development involved extensive playtesting refining card prompts, clue revelations, and character relationships. Early versions had more explicit mechanics; final version streamlined to minimal procedures, trusting emergent player creativity within structured framework. The card timing (when different types appear), clue distribution (ensuring mystery components emerge appropriately), and character backstory hooks went through numerous iterations.

Hunters Entertainment, known for publishing innovative indie designs, provided publishing support. The game's unusual requirements—Spotify playlist, group chat requirement, card deck—presented production challenges, but Hunters recognized the design's brilliance and commercial potential.

Alice is Missing launched in 2020 during COVID-19 pandemic lockdowns. The timing proved serendipitous. As gaming shifted online, Alice is Missing's Discord-native design worked perfectly for remote play when other games struggled with adaptation. The emotional depth provided meaningful connection during isolation. The game found audience desperately seeking powerful shared experiences during lockdown.

Social media amplification followed. Players shared devastating experiences on Twitter, Reddit, and Discord. Actual-play streams and podcasts featured Alice is Missing, often warning viewers about emotional intensity. The game developed reputation for making players cry—a badge of honor demonstrating its emotional effectiveness.

Critical recognition followed rapidly. The game won 2021 ENNIE Awards including Game of the Year, Product of the Year, and Best Writing. Reviews praised its innovation, emotional depth, and formal experimentation. Academic game scholars examined it as example of procedural emotional design and mediated communication in play.

By 2021-2022, Alice is Missing had become phenomenon. It demonstrated several crucial principles: formal constraints create unique experiences, emotional devastation can be design goal, remote-native design has value beyond pandemic necessity, and experimental structures can achieve mainstream success. The game inspired derivatives and influenced thinking about digital-mediated play, emotional storytelling, and innovative formal structures.

A decade into the 2020s, Alice is Missing remains essential story game, regularly introducing players to emotionally intense collaborative narrative while proving that innovation and accessibility can combine into commercially successful critically acclaimed experiences.

## Core Structure and Mechanics

Alice is Missing creates emergent mystery narratives through carefully structured card-based procedures executed entirely through silent text communication.

**Setup and Requirements**:
- **Group Chat**: Discord, iMessage, WhatsApp, or any group text platform
- **Card Deck**: 72 cards including character, clue, suspect, location, and motive cards
- **Spotify Playlist**: Curated 90-minute soundtrack
- **Timer**: 90-minute countdown (playlist provides this)
- **Silence**: No verbal communication allowed during play

**Character Selection**: Each player selects one of five pre-generated characters, all friends of missing Alice Briarwood:
- **Charlie Barnes**: Alice's best friend since childhood
- **Dakota Travis**: The outsider who recently became close to Alice
- **Evan Rhodes**: Alice's ex-boyfriend trying to reconnect
- **Jack Briarwood**: Alice's younger sibling
- **Julia North**: The popular student who drifted from Alice

Each character card includes motivations, secrets, and relationship prompts establishing connections to Alice and other characters.

**The Opening**: Players introduce characters through establishing posts in group chat, setting scene and relationships. They draw Introduction cards providing prompts about their connection to Alice and each other. This 10-15 minute opening establishes baseline before mystery begins.

**The Card Structure**: The deck contains several card types:

**Clue Cards** (40 cards): Draw at specific times (marked on cards). Each contains:
- **Clue**: A piece of information about Alice's disappearance
- **Prompt**: A question or situation for your character to address
- **Example**: "Clue: Alice withdrew all her savings the day before she disappeared. Prompt: How do you react when you discover this?"

**Suspect Cards** (10 cards): Introduce potential suspects with prompts exploring their possible involvement.

**Location Cards** (10 cards): Reveal significant places related to Alice's disappearance with investigative prompts.

**Searching Cards** (various): Prompt active investigation, character interactions, and emotional responses to unfolding mystery.

**The 90-Minute Structure**: Play proceeds in exact real-time 90 minutes:

**Minutes 0-10** (Track 1-2): Introduction and setup. Characters establish presence, relationships, and initial responses to Alice's disappearance.

**Minutes 10-40** (Tracks 3-5): Investigation begins. Players draw clue cards at prescribed times, posting discoveries and reactions in group chat. Suspicion builds, theories develop, relationships strain under pressure.

**Minutes 40-70** (Tracks 6-9): Tension escalates. More disturbing clues emerge. Private messages intensify as characters share suspicions or confide fears. The web of connections tightens.

**Minutes 70-85** (Tracks 10-12): Climax approaches. Final clues reveal critical information. Characters make choices about what to share, what to hide, who to trust. The truth becomes clear.

**Minutes 85-90** (Track 13): Resolution. Players draw their finale cards revealing what happened to Alice (different cards provide different outcomes—some tragic, some hopeful, all emotionally resonant). Characters respond to discovery with final posts processing what they've learned.

**Communication Rules**:

**Group Chat**: Main communication channel where all players see posts. Used for:
- Sharing discovered clues
- Proposing theories about Alice
- Character interactions and dialogue
- Emotional reactions and processing
- Coordinating investigation

**Private Messages**: One-on-one messages between characters (and potentially NPCs). Used for:
- Sharing secrets or suspicions
- Confiding fears or guilt
- Developing specific relationships
- Revealing information selectively
- Creating dramatic irony (others don't know what you've shared privately)

**No Voice**: Complete silence maintained throughout play. Physical presence creates tension—you see friends typing, see their reactions, but can only communicate through text. This constraint creates unique atmosphere of isolation-within-proximity.

**Card Timing**: Cards specify when to draw them—"Draw at 10:00," "Draw at 30:00," etc. This ensures proper pacing and revelation timing. Players draw cards individually at prescribed times, introducing their clues through in-character posts.

**The Playlist**: Curated Spotify soundtrack provides:
- **Atmospheric Enhancement**: Mood-appropriate music creating emotional tone
- **Time Tracking**: 13 tracks, each ~7 minutes, creating visible progress
- **Pacing Structure**: Different tracks for different phases (investigation, escalation, climax, resolution)
- **Emotional Resonance**: Carefully selected songs enhancing narrative moments

Track transitions become significant moments—you know time is passing, Alice remains missing, and answers approach inevitably.

**Clue Integration**: Unlike traditional mystery games, players don't solve predetermined mystery. Instead:
- Clues provide factual elements (Alice withdrew money, someone saw her at location, etc.)
- Players interpret clues collaboratively
- The mystery's shape emerges from how players connect clues
- Multiple interpretations remain valid until finale cards resolve

This interpretive flexibility allows creative engagement while maintaining mystery structure.

**Finale Cards**: At game's end, one player draws a finale card revealing what happened to Alice. Options include:
- Tragic ends (death, abduction, suicide)
- Ambiguous conclusions (missing without resolution)
- Hopeful outcomes (found safe, chose to leave)

The finale card players draw determines their game's specific resolution, ensuring each play has concrete ending while maintaining uncertainty throughout.

**Safety and Boundaries**:
- **X-Card Available**: Can be used in text ("X-Card: let's rewind from that")
- **Lines and Veils**: Established pre-game through text discussion
- **Pause Mechanism**: Anyone can request pause for check-in
- **Content Warnings**: Game includes warnings about missing persons, potential death, teen drama

**Post-Game**: After the 90 minutes conclude, players often debrief verbally, processing the emotional experience and appreciating the collaborative story.

These mechanics create reliable framework for generating emotionally intense mystery narratives through constrained communication, time pressure, and interpretive collaborative investigation.

## Narrative Philosophy and Design Goals

Alice is Missing's narrative philosophy centers on emotional authenticity, formal constraint as generator, and respectful engagement with tragedy.

**Silence as Emotional Amplifier**: The enforced silence creates unique emotional dynamics. Verbal communication encourages immediate reaction and improv bravado. Text communication creates space for:
- **Reflection**: Composing responses thoughtfully rather than reacting immediately
- **Vulnerability**: Text sometimes makes emotional honesty easier than face-to-face
- **Permanence**: Text creates record, making statements feel more consequential
- **Isolation**: Physical silence creates loneliness mirroring characters' emotional isolation
- **Distance and Intimacy**: Text paradoxically enables both emotional distance and deep intimacy

This constraint transforms the play experience fundamentally, creating contemplative rather than performative engagement.

**Real-Time as Generator of Urgency**: The 90-minute countdown creates genuine pressure:
- Time passes whether you're ready or not
- Can't pause for discussion or planning
- Clues arrive on schedule regardless of preparedness
- Mounting tension as playlist progresses
- Inevitable conclusion approaching inexorably

This temporal constraint mirrors characters' experience—searching desperately while time runs out—creating mechanical-narrative alignment.

**Mystery as Collaborative Construction**: Rather than predetermined solution, the mystery emerges collaboratively:
- Clues provide facts but not interpretation
- Players create connections between elements
- Multiple theories remain valid until resolution
- The "truth" emerges from collective interpretation
- Finale cards resolve ambiguity but honor preceding narrative

This collaborative mystery construction creates investment—you're discovering AND creating simultaneously.

**Emotional Devastation as Design Goal**: Unlike games avoiding negative emotions, Alice is Missing explicitly aims for emotional intensity:
- Mounting dread and grief as search continues
- Vulnerability in sharing fears and suspicions
- Weight of secrets and lies
- Inevitable tragedy (most finale cards are dark)
- Cathartic processing through play

The game trusts players to engage with difficult emotions, creating space for genuine feeling rather than just entertainment.

**Respectful Distance from Reality**: While inspired by real missing person cases, the game maintains respectful distance:
- Explicitly fictional framing (Silent Falls is not real)
- Fantastical elements available (supernatural options in some prompts)
- Player control over tone and content
- Option to veil traumatic details
- Focus on emotional truth rather than procedural realism

This balance allows meaningful engagement with tragedy without exploitation.

**Text as Documentary**: The text-based format creates lasting artifact:
- Complete chat log documents entire story
- Players can revisit and reflect afterward
- Permanent record of collaborative creation
- Screenshot sharing (with permission) shows others what happened
- The log IS the story, not just record of it

**Mediated Communication Reflecting Reality**: The game mirrors modern communication patterns:
- Physical proximity but digital mediation
- Group versus private conversations
- What we share publicly versus privately
- Typing indicators creating anticipation
- The performance of text versus authentic feeling

This makes the game feel contemporarily relevant—this IS how people communicate now.

**Character Secrets as Drivers**: Each character has secrets creating internal conflict:
- What you know versus what you share
- Guilt about past actions
- Fears about being involved
- Protecting others or yourself
- The burden of knowledge

These secrets create dramatic tension and moral complexity.

**Playlist as Emotional Guide**: The Spotify integration serves multiple purposes:
- Creates atmospheric immersion
- Marks temporal progression visibly
- Provides emotional peaks and valleys
- Eliminates need for GM narration
- Shared experience enhancing connection

Music becomes unspoken narrator, guiding without dictating.

**The Missing Person as Absence**: Alice never appears directly—she exists only through others' memories, clues, and speculation. This absence creates:
- Space for imagination
- Each player's unique relationship to Alice
- Mystery and uncertainty
- Focus on those left behind
- The weight of loss

Alice becomes collaboratively constructed through shared mourning and investigation.

**Teenage Perspective**: Focusing on teen characters creates specific dynamics:
- High school relationships and drama
- Limited power and resources
- Intense emotions and loyalty
- Secrets from adults
- Coming-of-age intersecting with tragedy

This perspective adds layers of vulnerability and authenticity.

**Procedural vs. Emotional Investigation**: The game cares less about forensic accuracy than emotional truth:
- Not "realistic" detective work
- Focus on personal connection to clues
- Emotional reactions matter more than logical deduction
- Character relationships drive investigation
- Feeling like teenagers searching for friend, not professional detectives

This emotional focus creates authentic engagement with material.

These philosophical commitments create game explicitly designed for emotional impact through formal innovation, trusting players to engage seriously with difficult material while providing structures enabling meaningful collaborative storytelling about loss, loyalty, and desperate hope.

## Play Experience, Safety, and Best Practices

Alice is Missing creates intense emotional experience requiring careful safety practices and thoughtful facilitation.

**The Silence Experience**: The enforced silence creates distinct atmosphere:

**Positive Effects**:
- Contemplative thoughtful engagement
- Space for emotional processing
- Vulnerability through text often easier than voice
- Heightened awareness of others' reactions
- Isolation mirroring characters' emotional state
- Permanent record allowing revisiting

**Challenges**:
- Can feel alienating for some players
- Typing speed disparities create inequality
- Difficulty reading tone in text
- Potential for misunderstanding
- Less immediate connection than verbal play
- Screen fatigue over 90 minutes

**Managing Silence**:
- Pre-game discussion of expectations
- Agreement on silence's purpose
- Permission to break silence for safety
- Understanding it's experiment, not everyone's preference

**Emotional Intensity Management**:

Alice is Missing can be genuinely devastating. Responsible play requires:

**Pre-Game Safety Discussion**:
- **Content Warnings**: Missing persons, potential death, teen drama, secrets/lies
- **Lines and Veils**: Establish off-limits content and what to handle off-screen
- **Safety Tools**: X-Card, OK Check-In, Script Change
- **Emotional Check-In**: Is everyone in appropriate headspace?
- **Exit Strategy**: Permission to leave game if overwhelmed

**Common Lines/Veils**:
- Graphic violence or death (often veiled)
- Sexual content or assault (usually hard line)
- Specific trauma that mirrors players' experiences
- Drug/alcohol abuse details
- Self-harm or suicide explicit details
- Family abuse or domestic violence

**During Play Safety**:
- X-Card available in chat (type "X-Card: [content]")
- Permission to take breaks (pause timer)
- Check-in system via private messages
- Facilitator monitoring for distress
- Remember it's game, not reality

**Post-Game Processing**:

The emotional intensity requires post-game care:

**Debrief Practices**:
- **Decompress**: 10-15 minutes processing verbally
- **Share Feelings**: What hit hardest? What surprised you?
- **Appreciate Creation**: Celebrate collaborative story
- **Reality Check**: Reinforce fiction versus reality
- **Self-Care**: Remind people to care for themselves afterward

**Potential Emotional Aftermath**:
- Lingering sadness or grief
- Thinking about the story for days
- Real-world resonances with missing person cases
- Processing relationships to own friends/family
- Catharsis and emotional release

**Support Resources**:
- Check in with players days later
- Provide resources if game triggered difficult feelings
- Normalize seeking support if needed

**Technical Considerations**:

**Platform Selection**:
- **Discord**: Most common, voice channels can stay muted
- **iMessage/WhatsApp**: Good for mobile-focused groups
- **Slack/Teams**: Works but less common
- **Any group chat** that supports private messages

**Setup Requirements**:
- Reliable internet for all players
- Devices for chatting (phones/computers)
- Spotify access for playlist
- Timer/clock visible to all
- Card deck (physical or digital)

**Accessibility**:

**Strengths**:
- Excellent for deaf/hard of hearing players
- Good for players with speech anxiety/disabilities
- Allows processing time for cognitive differences
- Creates permanent record for review

**Challenges**:
- Requires typing ability
- Assumes literacy
- Screen reader compatibility needed for blind players
- Fine motor skills for typing
- Digital divide (assumes device access)

**Adaptations**:
- Voice-to-text for typing difficulties
- Screen readers for visual impairments
- Extended timer for slower typers
- Assistance from other players if needed

**Group Composition**:

**Ideal Groups**:
- Trust and psychological safety among players
- Comfort with emotional vulnerability
- Willingness to engage seriously with material
- Similar expectations about tone
- 3-5 players (4 optimal)

**Challenging Groups**:
- Players seeking comedy over drama
- Insufficient trust for vulnerability
- Vastly different typing speeds
- Players uncomfortable with silence
- Anyone not in appropriate emotional headspace

**Facilitation Tips**:

**For Organizers**:
- Screen players for appropriateness
- Extensive pre-game safety discussion
- Monitor emotional state during play
- Be prepared to pause or end if needed
- Plan post-game processing time
- Have support resources available

**Teaching New Players**:
- Explain silence purpose and effects
- Demonstrate safety tools clearly
- Set expectations about emotional intensity
- Give permission to pass or pause
- Model appropriate engagement

**Managing Difficult Situations**:

**Player Overwhelmed**:
- Pause timer immediately
- Check in privately
- Offer exit or break
- Validate feelings
- Resume only when ready

**Tone Mismatch**:
- If someone plays comedy when others want drama:
- Private message about tone
- Check if group tone needs recalibration
- Sometimes better to end early

**Technical Issues**:
- Have backup platforms ready
- Can pause timer for technical problems
- Discord outages have happened mid-game

**Content Safety Failures**:
- X-Card immediately
- Pause and discuss
- Rewind if needed
- Check if game should continue
- Prioritize people over story

**Solo and Small Group Variants**:

**Two-Player**:
- Works but less mystery complexity
- More intimate and intense
- Each player takes multiple characters possible

**Three-Player**:
- Minimum recommended
- Each takes one character
- Intimate but functional

**Five-Player**:
- Maximum standard
- Richer interactions
- More complex relationship webs

**Solo**:
- Not officially supported
- Some play all characters
- More journaling exercise than emergent mystery

**Remote vs. In-Person**:

**Remote Advantages**:
- Natural fit for digital platforms
- Easier to maintain silence
- Chat infrastructure built-in
- Can play across distance

**In-Person Advantages**:
- See physical reactions
- Shared physical space creates atmosphere
- Easier to support emotionally
- Post-game processing more natural

**Hybrid**:
- Some players local, some remote
- Technically possible but challenging
- Creates inequality of experience

**Best Practices Summary**:

1. **Robust Pre-Game Safety**: Extensive discussion, clear tools
2. **Appropriate Group Composition**: Trust, emotional readiness, aligned expectations
3. **Monitor During Play**: Watch for distress, be ready to pause
4. **Post-Game Processing**: Essential, not optional
5. **Follow-Up Care**: Check in with players afterward
6. **Respect Intensity**: Not appropriate for all groups/occasions
7. **Technical Preparation**: Test platforms, have backups
8. **Accessibility Consideration**: Accommodate diverse needs
9. **Exit Permissions**: Clear that leaving is okay
10. **Reality Boundaries**: Maintain fiction/reality separation

Alice is Missing can create profound meaningful experiences when handled with care, respect, and robust safety practices. The intensity is feature, not bug—but requires responsible facilitation and player care.
