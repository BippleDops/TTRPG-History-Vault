# Call of Cthulhu

**Publisher**: [[Chaosium]]
**Designer**: [[Sandy Petersen]]
**Year**: 1981
**System**: percentile
**Genre**: horror
**Status**: in-print

## Historical Context

Call of Cthulhu, designed by Sandy Petersen and published by Chaosium in 1981, adapted H.P. Lovecraft's cosmic horror fiction into tabletop roleplaying form. The game emerged during the explosion of RPG diversity in the early 1980s, when publishers were exploring genres beyond fantasy.

Building on Chaosium's Basic Roleplaying System (originally developed for RuneQuest), Call of Cthulhu made several crucial design decisions that distinguished it from D&D-style games:

- Characters were fragile investigators, not powerful heroes
- Sanity was a resource that degraded through exposure to the unknown
- Combat was deadly and best avoided
- Investigation and puzzle-solving were primary activities
- Character death and madness were expected outcomes

The game has remained continuously in print for over 40 years, an extraordinary achievement in the TTRPG industry, now in its 7th edition with Chaosium still publishing.

## Mechanical Innovations

Call of Cthulhu introduced several mechanical innovations that influenced horror and investigation games:

**Sanity System**: Characters possessed a Sanity stat that decreased upon witnessing horrific events. Loss of sanity caused temporary or permanent mental illness, mechanically representing cosmic horror's psychologically destructive nature. This became the defining mechanic, copied by countless horror games.

**Percentile Skill System**: Skills were rated from 0-100%, with players rolling d100 to determine success. This provided intuitive probability assessment and granular skill differentiation.

**Skill-Based Character Advancement**: Characters improved through use, with successful skill rolls enabling potential advancement. This moved away from experience points toward organic development.

**Investigative Focus**: The game mechanics supported clue-gathering, research, and deduction rather than combat and dungeon-crawling.

**Deadly Combat**: The system made combat extremely dangerous, with small weapons capable of killing in one or two hits. This mechanically reinforced the horror genre's vulnerability themes.

**Occupation-Based Characters**: Character creation centered on mundane professions (journalist, professor, private investigator) rather than heroic archetypes, grounding characters in reality before thrusting them into cosmic horror.

## Cultural Impact

Call of Cthulhu demonstrated that TTRPGs could successfully explore genres beyond fantasy and science fiction. The game:

- Legitimized horror as a TTRPG genre
- Proved investigation-focused gameplay could work in tabletop form
- Popularized Lovecraft's Cthulhu Mythos beyond weird fiction circles
- Established sanity mechanics as a horror gaming staple
- Showed that character vulnerability and failure could be engaging play experiences
- Created a community that valued atmosphere and storytelling over tactical combat

The game influenced video games (Eternal Darkness, Bloodborne), board games (Arkham Horror, Eldritch Horror), and other TTRPGs. It demonstrated that games didn't need power progression to be engaging.

## Design Philosophy

Call of Cthulhu's design philosophy centered on several core principles:

**Cosmic Insignificance**: Investigators are ordinary people facing incomprehensible cosmic forces. Victory often means survival, not triumph.

**Knowledge is Dangerous**: Learning the truth destroys the mind. The more you understand, the less sane you become.

**Investigation Over Combat**: The game rewards research, deduction, and clever thinking over fighting.

**Atmosphere and Dread**: Rules serve the creation of horror atmosphere. Mechanics should make players feel vulnerable and afraid.

**Player Agency with Consequences**: Players make meaningful choices, but those choices have severe consequences.

**Accessible Rules**: The percentile system is intuitive, allowing players to focus on the narrative rather than mechanical complexity.

## Setting and Themes

Call of Cthulhu typically sets adventures in the 1920s, the era of Lovecraft's fiction, though the game supports multiple time periods including modern day, Victorian era, and even Dreamlands fantasy.

The game explores themes central to Lovecraft's work:
- Cosmic horror and humanity's insignificance
- Forbidden knowledge that destroys the seeker
- Ancient evils awakening
- Reality's fragility
- Rationality versus the irrational

The setting is Earth, but one where malevolent entities and cults threaten to destroy civilization. Investigators uncover conspiracies, confront monsters, and attempt to prevent apocalyptic events—often at the cost of their sanity or lives.

## Reception and Legacy

Call of Cthulhu received immediate acclaim and has maintained popularity for over four decades. The game:

- Won the Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules of 1981
- Has been translated into dozens of languages
- Spawned numerous supplements covering different eras and locations
- Spawned Chaosium's own variant settings (Cthulhu Now, Cthulhu Dark Ages) and, under licence, third-party lines such as Pagan Publishing's Delta Green (1997; continued since 2016 by Arc Dream Publishing)
- Maintained continuous publication through seven editions
- Established Chaosium as a major RPG publisher

The game proved that TTRPGs could be more than power fantasies. Its influence appears in every subsequent investigation and horror game, from Trail of Cthulhu to Monster of the Week to Bluebeard's Bride.
