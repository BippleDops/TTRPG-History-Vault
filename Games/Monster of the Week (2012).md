# Monster of the Week

**Publisher**: [[Evil Hat Productions]]
**Designer**: [[Michael Sands]]
**Year**: 2012
**System**: Powered by the Apocalypse (PbtA)
**Genre**: horror, modern, mystery
**Status**: in-print

## Historical Context

Monster of the Week emerged as one of the most successful genre applications of Powered by the Apocalypse, bringing the episodic monster-hunting structure of shows like Buffy, Supernatural, and The X-Files to tabletop gaming through carefully crafted procedural frameworks.

When Apocalypse World released in 2010, it inspired designers to adapt PbtA principles to various genres. Michael Sands, a New Zealand-based designer, saw potential in applying the framework to monster-hunting television shows. These shows followed reliable formulas—team of hunters encounters mysterious threat, investigates clues, discovers monster's nature, confronts and defeats it. Could PbtA's conversation-based play and move structure support this procedural approach while maintaining the shows' character drama?

The design challenge was significant. Monster-hunting shows balanced mystery investigation (requiring careful clue management), horror (building tension despite familiar patterns), action (exciting confrontations), and character development (personal arcs amid monster-fighting). Previous RPG attempts often emphasized either investigation or combat at the expense of other elements. Sands needed mechanics supporting all these aspects equally.

Sands' crucial insight was recognizing that these shows operated episodically. Each episode brought a new monster following a similar structure: something's wrong, investigate, identify the monster, find its weakness, defeat it. Rather than fighting this formula, embrace it. Create explicit mystery structure providing procedural framework for investigations while leaving specific details flexible.

The playbook designs reflected this understanding. The Chosen (Buffy), The Professional (Winchesters), The Spooky (psychic teen), The Flake (conspiracy theorist), The Mundane (normal person), The Expert (Giles), and others each represented familiar archetypes from the genre. Each playbook had moves supporting their specific role in the team and story type.

Development occurred through extensive playtesting in indie gaming communities. Sands shared iterations on Story Games forums, gathering feedback on mystery structure, playbook balance, and move design. The clue-based investigation framework underwent particular refinement to ensure mysteries felt solvable without becoming obvious.

Originally self-published in 2012 through Generic Games, Monster of the Week found modest success in indie circles. The game worked well for fans of the source material, demonstrating PbtA could handle procedural mystery structures. However, broader awareness remained limited initially.

Evil Hat Productions saw the game's potential. Known for publishing innovative systems with professional production, Evil Hat partnered with Sands to produce a revised, expanded edition. This 2015 edition featured professional editing, layout, and art while expanding content with additional playbooks, GM guidance, and mystery frameworks.

The Evil Hat edition dramatically expanded Monster of the Week's reach. The publisher's distribution networks, marketing capabilities, and reputation brought the game to wider audiences. Reviews emphasized how perfectly the mechanics captured the genre's feel—investigating monsters felt like being on Supernatural, team dynamics echoed Buffy, weird conspiracy theories channeled The X-Files.

The game's accessibility proved crucial for its success. New players could grasp the core concept immediately—you hunt monsters like on TV. The episodic structure meant each session provided complete satisfying story. The mystery framework gave GMs clear preparation guidelines. These factors made Monster of the Week excellent for one-shots, convention games, and introducing newcomers to narrative gaming.

By the late 2010s, Monster of the Week had become one of the most popular PbtA games, regularly appearing in "best RPG" lists and recommended games for beginners. It demonstrated that PbtA principles could support procedural structures, that episodic play could be deeply satisfying, and that genre emulation worked best when mechanics captured the genre's core patterns rather than surface details.

The game spawned supplements expanding playbook options and mystery types. It influenced how designers thought about procedural play and mystery structures in narrative games. It proved that careful attention to genre patterns could create mechanics generating those patterns reliably.

Monster of the Week represents successful synthesis of PbtA innovation and genre conventions. It showed that understanding television's storytelling patterns could inform game design, that procedural frameworks enhanced rather than restricted creativity, and that accessible design could introduce thousands to narrative gaming through familiar genre touchstones.

## Mechanical Innovations

Monster of the Week adapted PbtA frameworks to mystery investigation while introducing innovations for procedural monster-hunting play.

**Mystery Structure**: The game provides explicit mystery preparation framework—threats (the monster), locations, bystanders, countdown (what happens if hunters don't intervene). This structure gives GMs clear preparation guidelines while maintaining flexibility. Mysteries follow television's pattern: establish mystery, investigate, identify monster, find weakness, confront.

**Investigate a Mystery**: When you investigate, you roll and ask questions based on results—"What happened here?", "What sort of creature is it?", "What can hurt it?", "Where did it go?" This move structures investigation through player-driven questions rather than GM information dumps. Players pursue what interests them while gathering necessary clues.

**The Countdown**: Each mystery has a countdown—stages showing what happens if hunters don't intervene. "Day 1: Monster arrives. Day 2: First victim. Day 3: Pattern established. Day 4: More victims. Day 5: Monster achieves goal." This creates urgency and provides GM framework for when hunters don't immediately solve mysteries.

**Hunter Playbooks**: The game features playbooks representing monster-hunting archetypes:
- **The Chosen** - Destined hero with special abilities (Buffy)
- **The Professional** - Trained monster hunter (Winchesters)
- **The Spooky** - Psychic with dark powers (Willow, Sam Winchester)
- **The Expert** - Researcher with vast knowledge (Giles, Bobby)
- **The Flake** - Conspiracy theorist seeing patterns (Lone Gunmen)
- **The Mundane** - Normal person who got involved (Xander)
- **The Initiate** - Part of ancient monster-fighting organization
- **The Monstrous** - Part monster yourself (Angel, Castiel)
- **The Divine** - Angel or divine agent
- **The Wronged** - Seeking revenge for what monsters took

Each playbook provides distinct play experience and team role.

**Luck**: Hunters have Luck points (usually 7) representing plot armor. Spend Luck to change outcomes, prevent death, or escape seemingly impossible situations. Once Luck depletes, you're vulnerable—maybe you die, maybe you're transformed, maybe you retire. This mechanizes the genre's combination of danger and heroic survival.

**Harm and Healing**: Damage uses simple harm track (0-7 with thresholds creating penalties). At 4+ harm you're seriously injured. At 8+ you're dying. Healing requires time or magic—no instant recovery. This makes combat dangerous without being instantly lethal, matching the shows' tone where heroes get hurt but rarely die.

**Use Magic**: When you use magic, describe what you're doing and roll. Results determine whether magic works, costs, or has unexpected consequences. This handles all magic generically—whether it's spells, psychic powers, or divine intervention—through single flexible move.

**Big Magic**: For major rituals or powerful effects, use Big Magic—a multi-stage process requiring time, components, and dealing with consequences. This handles season-finale-level magic that changes situations fundamentally.

**Manipulate Someone**: Social interaction uses Manipulate—leverage something they want, roll, and on success they do it if you meet their conditions. This keeps social situations grounded in fictional leverage rather than abstract persuasion checks.

**Kick Some Ass**: Combat uses single streamlined move. Roll+Tough, and results determine whether you hurt them, get hurt, create advantage, or suffer complication. This keeps fights fast and dangerous without blow-by-blow tactical detail.

**Act Under Pressure**: Generic move for risky actions—escaping, avoiding danger, quick action under stress. This catch-all handles edge cases without requiring specific moves for every situation.

**Read a Bad Situation**: When entering dangerous situations, you can read it—ask questions about threats, best escape route, biggest danger, etc. This tactical assessment move helps hunters prepare for confrontations.

**Help Out**: When helping teammates, you expose yourself to danger or consequences but grant them bonuses. This encourages teamwork while making assistance meaningful rather than automatic.

**Protect Someone**: When you protect someone from harm, roll to see how well you shield them. Success means you suffer the consequences instead. This mechanizes the heroic self-sacrifice common in the genre.

**Haven**: Your team has a base of operations with specific features—workshop, library, armory, etc. The Haven provides resources and serves as safe space for planning and recovery. This mechanizes the Scooby Gang's library, the Winchesters' bunker, or the X-Files office.

**Phenomenon and Phenomena**: For weird ongoing situations beyond single monsters, use Phenomenon structure—similar to mysteries but representing ongoing threats requiring multiple sessions to resolve. This handles story arcs beyond episodic mysteries.

**Improvements**: Advancement uses playbook improvements—get better at moves, increase stats, develop relationships, gain abilities. At certain thresholds, you can change playbooks or retire your character, representing major life changes.

**Team Bonds**: You have history with teammates granting mechanical benefits when working together. These bonds provide reasons for cooperation and mechanical encouragement for supporting each other.

## Design Philosophy

Monster of the Week's design philosophy centered on genre emulation through procedural structure.

**Television Structure**: The game explicitly emulates television's episodic format. Each session aims to tell complete mystery story while advancing ongoing character arcs. This structure matches source material while creating accessible play.

**Procedure Enables Creativity**: The mystery structure doesn't restrict—it enables. GMs know what to prepare (countdown, locations, monster). Players know what to do (investigate, identify, defeat). This clarity frees mental energy for creative execution.

**Genre Before Simulation**: The game prioritizes feeling like the source material over realistic simulation. Luck points prevent death because protagonists don't randomly die. Investigation uses questions because TV detectives always find clues. Combat stays fast because shows don't dwell on tactical details. Every choice serves genre emulation.

**Accessible Complexity**: The rules are simple enough for newcomers but deep enough for experienced players. Core moves are straightforward; complexity emerges from fictional positioning and player creativity rather than mechanical intricacy.

**Mystery as Collaboration**: While GM creates mysteries, players drive investigation through their questions and actions. The Investigate move ensures players ask about what interests them. This creates collaborative mystery-solving rather than players guessing what GM wants.

**Teamwork Emphasized**: The game assumes you're a team. Moves like Help Out and Protect Someone mechanically encourage cooperation. Haven provides shared resource. Individual success matters less than team success.

**Failure as Complication**: Failed rolls don't mean incompetence—they mean complications, costs, or escalation. You still get information from failed Investigate rolls; you just face consequences. This keeps play moving forward rather than stalling on failed rolls.

**Monsters Are Threats**: The game treats monsters as dangerous threats rather than balanced encounters. Hunters should feel challenged and at risk. Luck and teamwork are necessary for survival. This maintains tension despite familiarity with patterns.

**Character Growth**: Advancement represents hunters becoming more capable and experienced. Playbook changes represent major life transitions—maybe The Chosen becomes The Expert after learning enough, or The Professional becomes The Wronged after tragedy.

**Explicit Guidance**: Like all PbtA games, Monster of the Week provides extensive GM guidance—how to create mysteries, run countdowns, make GM moves, handle combat. This teaches GMing the game's specific approach rather than assuming knowledge.

**Countdown as Structure**: The countdown provides both urgency and pacing framework. GMs advance it when appropriate, creating pressure without requiring real-time tracking. This structure ensures mysteries escalate toward resolution.

**Flexible Canon**: The game doesn't define specific setting—whether monsters are public knowledge or secret, whether governments know about them, what types exist. Tables decide these details, creating their own monster-hunting reality.

## Setting and Themes

Monster of the Week deliberately avoids detailed default setting, instead providing framework for creating your own monster-hunting reality.

**Your Monster-Hunting World**: The game asks tables to establish basic facts: Are monsters secret or public? Do governments acknowledge them? Are there monster-hunting organizations? What types of monsters exist? These decisions shape the campaign's reality.

**Implicitly Modern**: The game assumes contemporary or near-contemporary setting—cell phones exist, internet research is possible, modern weapons are available. This matches the source material's modern settings.

**Television Inspirations**: The game explicitly draws from specific shows:
- **Buffy the Vampire Slayer** - Chosen One fighting supernatural evil with friends
- **Supernatural** - Brothers hunting monsters across America
- **The X-Files** - Investigating paranormal phenomena and conspiracies
- **Fringe** - Scientific approach to weird occurrences
- **Grimm** - Hidden supernatural world in modern city

Each provides different tone and approach while fitting the framework.

**Haven as Center**: Your team's base—whether library, bunker, office, or headquarters—serves as narrative and mechanical center. It's where you plan, research, recover, and develop relationships. The Haven grounds the campaign geographically and emotionally.

**Wandering or Stationary**: Some campaigns follow traveling hunters (Supernatural-style). Others focus on protecting specific location (Buffy-style). The framework supports both approaches.

**Monsters as Metaphor**: Like the source material, monsters can represent real-world issues—addiction, abuse, alienation, societal pressure. The game supports literal monster-hunting and metaphorical exploration equally.

**Themes**:

**Fighting the Darkness**: The central theme. Hunters face supernatural evil threatening innocents. Whether they're chosen, trained, or accidentally involved, they stand between monsters and victims.

**We're In This Together**: Teamwork is essential. No hunter survives alone. Your team provides backup, support, specialized knowledge, and emotional grounding. Relationships matter as much as monster-fighting skills.

**Knowledge is Power**: Understanding monsters—their nature, weaknesses, origins—provides advantage. Research matters. The Expert's knowledge, The Flake's conspiracy theories, The Spooky's intuition—all contribute to identifying and defeating threats.

**The Cost of Hunting**: Monster-hunting takes tolls—physical injuries, relationship strain, moral compromises, traumatic experiences. Hunters sacrifice normal lives to protect others. The game explores these costs through Harm, depleting Luck, and character arcs.

**Chosen vs. Choice**: Some hunters are chosen—destiny, divine selection, family legacy. Others chose this life—revenge, duty, accident. The game explores tensions between fate and free will through different playbooks.

**Ordinary Heroes**: Even The Chosen is fundamentally human. The Mundane has no powers at all. Heroes are ordinary people facing extraordinary evil. This makes heroism meaningful—they fight despite fear because someone must.

**Hidden World**: Typically, the supernatural exists hidden from public awareness. Hunters know what most don't. This secret knowledge isolates them while creating special bond with those who understand.

**Investigation Before Action**: You can't just shoot monsters—you need to understand them first. Wrong approaches fail or backfire. Investigation, research, and planning matter as much as combat capability.

**Escalating Threats**: While individual mysteries resolve episodically, hunters face escalating dangers. Each monster teaches lessons. Ongoing phenomena represent larger threats. Characters grow to face bigger challenges.

**Personal Stakes**: The best mysteries involve personal connections—protecting friends, avenging losses, confronting past mistakes. The game encourages GMs to tie mysteries to character backgrounds and relationships.

- Episodic structure didn't satisfy players wanting deeper continuity
- Mystery formula could feel repetitive
- Limited support for investigation-heavy play

- Required GM comfort creating mysteries
- Worked better for some monster-hunting styles than others

These criticisms generally reflected preference rather than flaws—the game optimized for specific experience not everyone wanted.

**Greatest Achievement**:
Monster of the Week proved that understanding a genre's narrative patterns—investigation, identification, confrontation—and creating mechanics supporting those patterns produces better genre emulation than simulating surface details. The game doesn't have detailed combat rules or monster stat blocks; it has investigation moves and mystery structures because that's what makes monster-hunting shows work.

By focusing on television's storytelling rhythm rather than realistic monster-hunting simulation, Sands created system that felt exactly like being on Buffy or Supernatural. This thematic focus over mechanical simulation influenced how designers approached licensed and genre-specific games.

**Legacy Established**:
Monster of the Week's lasting contributions include:

For many players, Monster of the Week was their introduction to PbtA through familiar genre touchstones. For designers, it demonstrated that television's narrative patterns could inform game structure productively. For the hobby, it proved that episodic play could create satisfying long-term campaigns while remaining accessible to newcomers.

Monster of the Week represents successful synthesis of indie game innovation and mainstream genre appeal. It showed that carefully understanding source material's patterns, then creating mechanics generating those patterns, produces authentic genre experiences. It proved that procedural structure enhanced rather than restricted creativity. It demonstrated that accessible design could serve both newcomers and experienced players through simple rules generating complex play.

In these respects, Monster of the Week achieved exactly what great licensed and genre games should: it made players feel like they were living in their favorite shows while providing mechanically interesting gameplay and opportunities for character development. This balance ensures its legacy as essential PbtA game and excellent introduction to narrative gaming.
