# Amber Diceless RPG

**Publisher**: [[Phage Press]]
**Designer**: [[Erick Wujcik]]
**Year**: 1991
**System**: diceless (attribute comparison)
**Genre**: multi-dimensional fantasy
**Status**: out-of-print

## Overview

Amber Diceless Roleplaying Game, designed by Erick Wujcik and published by Phage Press in 1991, represents one of RPG design's most audacious experiments—eliminating dice and all randomizers entirely, resolving conflicts through attribute comparison, player description, and game master adjudication. Based on Roger Zelazny's Chronicles of Amber fantasy series, the game cast players as members of the royal family of Amber, reality's one true city, whose princes and princesses walked between infinite Shadow worlds (parallel dimensions), wielded Pattern or Logrus (primal forces), and engaged in Byzantine family politics where immortal relatives schemed for power across eternities.

The game emerged at historical moment when narrative-focused design challenged dice-driven mechanics. While most RPGs relied on randomization to determine outcomes, Wujcik argued that dice undermined dramatic pacing, reduced player agency, and created absurd results where incompetent characters occasionally succeeded while masters failed. Amber eliminated randomization completely—conflict resolution compared character attributes directly, with higher scores guaranteeing victory. This radical approach fundamentally challenged assumptions about what RPGs required mechanically.

Wujcik's design philosophy emphasized character competence and player skill over random chance. Amber's princes and princesses were essentially demigods—vastly superior to normal humans, capable of reshaping reality through willpower, traveling dimensions, and living potentially forever. Dice couldn't capture such beings adequately; Zelazny's novels never suggested uncertainty about whether Corwin could defeat mortal swordsmen—only whether he could overcome his equally powerful siblings. Amber's diceless system reflected this—player characters succeeded at challenges unless facing worthy opposition (other player characters, major NPCs, or overwhelming circumstances).

The auction mechanic revolutionized character creation. Rather than rolling or point-buying attributes, players bid points for rank in four attributes (Psyche, Strength, Endurance, Warfare). The highest bidder ranked first (best in attribute across all player characters), second-highest ranked second, etc. This created ranked hierarchy where everyone knew who was strongest, smartest, toughest. Players strategized about where to invest, often accepting weakness in some attributes to dominate others. The auction created immediate character relationships and party dynamics—younger siblings might accept lower ranks while elders bid aggressively.

Powers distinguished Amber dramatically from traditional RPGs. Instead of spell lists or skill sets, characters purchased major powers: Pattern Imprint (allowing dimensional travel and reality manipulation), Logrus Mastery (chaos magic and summoning), Trump Artistry (creating magical communication tools), Shape Shifting (infinite transformations), and Sorcery (traditional magic). These weren't incremental improvements but fundamental capabilities defining how characters interacted with setting. A Pattern initiate could walk between dimensions, alter probability, and defend against magical attacks—vastly beyond normal human capacity.

Shadow worlds—infinite parallel realities—provided campaign setting. Amber existed as reality's metaphysical center, Chaos at the opposite extreme, with infinite Shadow worlds between representing every possibility. Characters traveled through Shadow seeking resources, allies, hiding places, or conquest. Game masters described Shadow worlds ranging from technological future-scapes to primitive wildernesses to fantastical impossibilities. This infinite canvas meant campaigns could explore any genre or theme.

The game achieved critical and commercial success despite niche appeal. It won Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules in 1992, validating diceless design's viability. Amber cultivated devoted fanbase appreciating its specific virtues: diceless resolution, character competence, political intrigue, dimensional exploration, and faithful adaptation of beloved literary source.

However, Amber also generated controversy. Critics argued that eliminating dice reduced gaming to arbitrary GM fiat, making outcomes dependent on game master favoritism. The ranked attribute system meant lower-ranked characters couldn't win direct confrontations, creating intra-party hierarchy some players found frustrating. The diceless approach required game masters with strong improvisational skills and fair adjudication—not universal traits.

Despite controversies, Amber's influence extended far beyond its player base. It demonstrated that diceless systems could work commercially, inspiring subsequent diceless games (Nobilis, Everway, Puppetland). It showed how mechanical systems could emerge from setting needs rather than generic frameworks. It proved that character competence and dramatic storytelling could outweigh tactical complexity for some audiences. It influenced narrative-focused design movements emphasizing description and characterization over mechanics.

## Historical Context

In 1991, most RPGs relied fundamentally on dice or randomizers. D&D used d20s, percentile rolls, and various polyhedral dice. Champions rolled 3d6. Call of Cthulhu used percentile dice. White Wolf's Vampire (1991, same year) rolled d10 pools. Randomization seemed essential—how else could outcomes remain uncertain and exciting?

Erick Wujcik, experienced game designer known for work on Palladium's Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles RPG (1985) and other properties, questioned this assumption. He argued that dice created problems: they slowed play through rolling and calculation, undermined dramatic pacing by resolving climactic moments through random chance, reduced player agency by making success or failure independent of player decisions, and created absurdities where masters occasionally failed at basic tasks while incompetents lucked into success.

Wujcik sought alternative resolution for game based on Roger Zelazny's Chronicles of Amber—ten-book fantasy series published 1970-1991 that was cult classic among fantasy readers. The series followed Corwin and his siblings, princes and princesses of Amber (reality's true city), as they schemed for throne, traveled infinite Shadow worlds, wielded Pattern (order magic) and Logrus (chaos magic), and engaged in complex family politics across dimensions and centuries.

Zelazny's narrative never suggested randomness in confrontations. When Corwin fought Gerard (strongest sibling), outcome was certain—Gerard would win physically. When Corwin outfoxed siblings through cunning, his Warfare skill determined tactical superiority. Character capability was absolute and relative simultaneously—Corwin was godlike compared to mortals but middle-ranked among siblings.

Wujcik recognized that dice couldn't capture this. Rolling dice might occasionally let weak character defeat strong one, contradicting source material's established hierarchy. The solution: eliminate dice entirely. Resolve conflicts through attribute comparison—higher rank wins. If Corwin (Warfare rank 2) fought Benedict (Warfare rank 1), Benedict won inevitably. This determinism matched novels perfectly.

The design crystallized around several principles:

**Diceless Resolution**: Conflicts compared attributes or approached outcomes through description and GM adjudication. Combat between characters with different Warfare ranks had predetermined outcomes. Characters with equal ranks required detailed description of tactics, with GM deciding based on player explanations and situational factors.

**Auction Character Creation**: Players bid character creation points for attribute ranks, creating competitive environment mirroring Amber family dynamics. Players strategized about specialization versus balance, knowing final ranks determined capabilities absolutely.

**Power-Based Customization**: Instead of skills and spells, players purchased fundamental powers (Pattern, Logrus, Trump, Shape Shifting, Sorcery) providing broad capabilities. These weren't incremental but transformative—Pattern initiate could travel dimensions and reshape reality.

**Character Competence**: All player characters were Amber royalty—essentially demigods compared to mortals. They automatically succeeded at mundane tasks. Challenges came from equally powerful opposition, overwhelming circumstances, or strategic dilemmas.

**Shadow Worlds**: Infinite parallel realities provided limitless campaign settings. GMs could explore any genre—fantasy kingdoms, science fiction futures, historical periods, impossible dreamscapes—all existing as Shadow worlds accessible through Pattern walking.

Phage Press, small publisher run by Wujcik himself, released Amber in 1991. The 256-page hardcover featured striking cover art and comprehensive setting information drawn from Zelazny's novels. The book explained diceless resolution, detailed character creation, explored setting cosmology, described powers, provided campaign frameworks, and offered GM guidance on adjudication.

Critical reception was polarized but respectful. Many reviewers praised the innovative design and faithful literary adaptation. Others questioned whether diceless resolution truly constituted "roleplaying game" or was instead collaborative storytelling with different name. Commercial reception was modest but positive—the game sold well enough to sustain Phage Press and justify supplements.

Supplements expanded Amber: Shadow Knight (expansion rulebook), Rebma (underwater kingdom sourcebook), various scenario books. These demonstrated commercial viability, though Amber never achieved mainstream success comparable to D&D or Vampire. The game remained niche property appreciated by devoted fans.

The 1991 timing proved significant. Vampire: The Masquerade also released in 1991, emphasizing storytelling over tactical combat. Story game movement was emerging, with designers exploring narrative focus over mechanical simulation. Amber stood at this transition's forefront, demonstrating extreme narrative prioritization by eliminating randomization entirely.

Amber influenced subsequent designs significantly. Nobilis (1999) adopted diceless resolution for playing incarnate concepts. Everway (1995) used minimal randomization with fortune-telling-based resolution. Various indie games explored diceless mechanics. Narrative-focused games generally acknowledged Amber as demonstrating alternatives to dice-driven mechanics.

The game's dependence on Zelazny's literary property created challenges. When Zelazny died in 1995, further Amber novels ceased. The game's limited license restricted commercial exploitation. Phage Press remained small operation unable to compete with larger publishers' marketing. These factors limited growth while fostering dedicated community.

By 2000s, Amber was technically out of print, though used copies circulated and PDF versions became available. The game achieved cult classic status—influential among designers and beloved by fans despite small active player base. When Rite Publishing released Lords of Gossamer and Shadow (2012), essentially Amber successor using similar mechanics without Zelazny's setting, it demonstrated sustained interest in diceless Amber-style play.

## Core Mechanics

Amber's mechanics were revolutionary in their simplicity and radicalism:

**Character Creation Auction**: Players received 100 points to bid for attribute ranks and purchase powers. The auction proceeded:

1. Players simultaneously bid points for ranking in four attributes:
   - **Psyche**: Mental strength, magic resistance, psychic power
   - **Strength**: Physical power
   - **Endurance**: Stamina, damage resistance, healing
   - **Warfare**: Combat skill, tactics, strategic thinking

2. Highest bidder in each attribute ranked first (best among player characters), second-highest ranked second, etc. Ties resolved through additional bidding or negotiation.

3. Rankings were absolute—first rank guaranteed victory over second rank in direct comparisons, second over third, etc.

4. "Amber rank" (baseline competency matching original novels' unnamed NPCs) could be purchased for 10 points without competing, placing character above mortals but below ranked player characters.

The auction created immediate character dynamics—everyone knew relative strengths. Players strategized about specialization (dominating one attribute) versus balance (being competent across multiple attributes).

**Powers**: Players purchased major capabilities:

- **Pattern Imprint** (50 points): Allowed walking the Pattern (mystical labyrinth in Amber), granting:
  - Shadow walking (traveling between parallel dimensions)
  - Probability manipulation (shifting to Shadow worlds favoring desired outcomes)
  - Magical defense (psychic immunity, resistance to sorcery)
  - Regeneration (healing in Shadow)
  - Pattern ghosts (creating temporal echoes)

- **Logrus Mastery** (45 points): Chaos analogue to Pattern, granting:
  - Logrus tendrils (manifesting chaos appendages to manipulate objects remotely)
  - Summoning (pulling objects/creatures from Shadow)
  - Spell storage (holding sorceries in Logrus)
  - Chaotic defense and attack capabilities

- **Trump Artistry** (40 points): Creating magical paintings/cards allowing:
  - Instantaneous communication across dimensions
  - Teleportation through Trump contact
  - Scrying and observation
  - Trap creation and defensive Trump

- **Shape Shifting** (35 points): Assuming any physical form, granting:
  - Animal transformations
  - Appearance alteration
  - Physical adaptation (gills, wings, armor)
  - Accelerated healing through shapeshifting

- **Sorcery** (15 points): Traditional magic system:
  - Spell construction through symbolic manipulation
  - Slower but versatile effects
  - Requiring Psyche contests against targets
  - Limited in Amber itself (technology suppresses magic)

Powers were expensive—Pattern alone cost half the starting points. Characters typically purchased one or two major powers, creating specialization.

**Diceless Resolution**: All conflicts resolved without randomization:

**Attribute Comparison**: When characters with different attribute ranks competed directly, higher rank won automatically. First rank Warfare defeated second rank Warfare in combat inevitably. This determinism matched setting—Benedict (best fighter) defeated any sibling in honest combat.

**Equal Ranks**: When characters with equal ranks competed, resolution depended on:
- Tactical description—players explained approaches, GM adjudicated based on creativity, planning, and situational advantages
- Relevant advantages—equipment, environmental factors, preparation
- GM interpretation of relative positions

This rewarded player skill—better tactical explanations improved outcomes.

**Amber NPC Ranks**: Named NPCs from novels had fixed ranks:
- Benedict (First Warfare across all existence—unbeatable in combat)
- Gerard (First Strength—physically strongest)
- Fiona/Bleys (First Psyche—most powerful mystics)
- Corwin (Second Warfare, high Endurance—formidable fighter and survivor)

Players ranking above novel characters effectively retconned setting hierarchy, becoming more capable than source material's established powers. This created interesting dynamics.

**Overwhelming Circumstances**: Even first-rank characters could be defeated by overwhelming numbers, environmental hazards, or sustained attrition. The system wasn't entirely deterministic—context mattered.

**Good Stuff/Bad Stuff**: Characters could purchase "Good Stuff" (positive karma) or accept "Bad Stuff" (negative karma) for extra points:
- **Good Stuff**: Luck favored character—coincidences worked out positively, encounters went well, random events benefited character
- **Bad Stuff**: Misfortune plagued character—plans went awry, situations deteriorated, random events harmed character
- **Zero Stuff**: Neutral karma—fate neither helped nor hindered

This mechanical element added uncertainty without dice. GM interpreted "stuff" through narrative developments and situational outcomes rather than rolls.

**Shadow Walking**: Pattern initiates moved through Shadow by concentrating on desired destination characteristics. This narrative travel system allowed characters to find specific Shadow worlds:
- "Find high-tech world with advanced computers"
- "Find medieval world where I'm worshipped as god"
- "Find Shadow where Benedict doesn't exist"

GM described worlds encountered during travel based on character's Pattern Imprint strength and journey description.

**Trump Communication**: Characters with Trumps (magical art pieces) could activate them for instantaneous psychic contact across dimensions. Target felt contact and could choose to answer, ignore, or deflect. Trump usage facilitated party coordination despite dimensional separation.

**Combat**: Resolved through Warfare comparisons and tactical descriptions. Higher Warfare controlled engagement—deciding range, choosing terrain, dictating tactical flow. Equal Warfare fighters required detailed descriptions, with GM adjudicating based on tactics, equipment, and circumstances.

Strength determined damage capability. Endurance determined damage resistance and healing speed. Combat rarely killed player characters outright—siblings were resilient. Death required overwhelming force, extended helplessness, or sacrificial situations.

**Magic and Powers**: Psyche comparisons determined magical contests. Higher Psyche overpowered lower Psyche in psychic combat, spell resistance, or magical manipulation. Pattern/Logrus provided inherent magical defenses.

The mechanical philosophy prioritized narrative description, strategic planning, and attribute hierarchies over randomized resolution. This created predictable yet strategically complex gameplay.

## Setting and Themes

**The Chronicles of Amber**: Zelazny's ten-novel series provided comprehensive setting:

**First Chronicles** (Corwin's story, books 1-5):
- Corwin awakens on Earth with amnesia
- Rediscovers identity as Amber prince
- Returns to Amber during succession crisis
- Competes with siblings for throne
- Discovers Pattern and Shadow nature
- Faces existential threats to reality

**Second Chronicles** (Merlin's story, books 6-10):
- Merlin (Corwin's son) navigates Chaos/Amber politics
- Investigates assassination attempts
- Explores Logrus and chaos magic
- Manages Pattern/Logrus conflict
- Resolves cosmic implications of earlier events

**Amber**: Reality's one true city, existing at metaphysical center of all existence. Physical, eternal, perfect. All other worlds were Shadows—less real reflections. Amber's royal family wielded Pattern—primal force of order granting dimensional powers.

**The Pattern**: Mystical labyrinth beneath Amber's castle. Walking Pattern was initiation rite granting:
- Dimensional travel capabilities
- Reality manipulation
- Connection to order principle
- Qualification for throne succession
- Immortality and enhanced resilience

**Chaos Courts**: Reality's opposite pole—realm of entropy, change, flux. Ruled by chaos lords including Amber royals' relatives. Logrus was Chaos equivalent of Pattern.

**The Logrus**: Chaotic equivalent of Pattern—shifting maze granting:
- Chaos magic and summoning
- Reality manipulation through entropy
- Connection to chaos principle
- Enhanced capabilities in flux

**Shadow Worlds**: Infinite parallel realities between Amber and Chaos. Every possibility existed as Shadow—worlds with magic, technology, impossibility, or normalcy. Pattern initiates walked through Shadow, traveling dimensions by focusing on desired characteristics.

**Key Locations**:
- **Amber**: True city, center of reality
- **Rebma**: Underwater reflection of Amber
- **Chaos Courts**: Far realm of entropy
- **Arden Forest**: Vast wilderness surrounding Amber
- **Shadows**: Infinite—GMs created worlds as needed

**Royal Family**: The game cast players as previously unknown members of Amber's royal family—siblings, cousins, or descendants of Oberon (late king) or other royals. This immediately established:
- Immense personal power (demigod-level)
- Complex family relationships
- Political intrigue and succession issues
- Dimensional travel capabilities
- Potential immortality

The family dynamics were Byzantine—relatives schemed, allied, betrayed, reconciled, and competed across centuries. No family member was entirely trustworthy; even allies pursued personal agendas.

**Themes**:

- **Family Politics and Betrayal**: Immortal relatives schemed for power, creating intrigue where anyone might be ally or enemy depending on circumstances.

- **Identity and Memory**: Multiple novels featured amnesia, false memories, or identity crises. Characters questioned their nature and loyalties.

- **Order vs. Chaos**: Pattern and Logrus represented fundamental cosmic principles in eternal tension. Characters navigated between extremes.

- **Infinite Possibility**: Shadow worlds meant anything imaginable existed somewhere. This creative freedom enabled diverse campaigns.

- **Power and Responsibility**: Royal power brought obligation to protect Amber and maintain reality's structure.

- **Competence and Capability**: Characters were essentially godlike—challenges came from equally powerful opposition, not mundane obstacles.

- **Manipulation and Deception**: Nothing was as it seemed. Every character potentially concealed secrets, agendas, or capabilities.

## Cultural Impact

Amber's influence extended far beyond its commercial success:

**Diceless Design Validation**: Demonstrated that games could eliminate randomization entirely while remaining mechanically functional and commercially viable. This opened design space for subsequent diceless games.

**Literary Adaptation Model**: Showed how to faithfully adapt complex literary properties while creating playable game. Amber captured Zelazny's setting and themes without compromising either.

**Attribute Comparison Systems**: Influenced games using direct attribute comparisons rather than randomized resolution. Various narrative-focused games adopted similar approaches.

**Auction Mechanics**: The bidding system for character creation influenced later games using auctions or competitive point allocation.

**Character Competence Focus**: Demonstrated appeal of playing extremely powerful characters where challenge came from worthy opposition rather than incompetence risk.

**Narrative Over Mechanics**: Amber prioritized description and adjudication over mechanical detail, influencing story game movement toward narrative emphasis.

**Infinite Setting Framework**: The Shadow worlds concept—infinite parallel realities—influenced multiverse gaming and dimensional travel mechanics.

**Designer Influence**: Directly inspired:
- **Nobilis** (1999): Diceless game about playing concepts
- **Everway** (1995): Minimal-randomization narrative game
- **Lords of Gossamer & Shadow** (2012): Spiritual successor
- Various indie diceless experiments

**Community Culture**: Cultivated devoted fanbase engaged with deep character development, political intrigue, and sophisticated narrative play. Amber conventions featured elaborate multi-table campaigns with dozens of players.

**GM Adjudication**: Demonstrated both potential and challenges of GM-dependent resolution. Amber required fair, consistent, creative adjudication—raising discussions about GM authority and arbitrariness.

**Academic Interest**: Game studies examined Amber as example of diceless design, literary adaptation, and narrative-focused gaming.

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Revolutionary diceless design
- Faithful literary adaptation
- Emphasis on narrative and description
- Character competence and capability
- Infinite campaign possibilities through Shadow worlds
- Political intrigue depth
- Elegant mechanical simplicity

**Criticism**:
- GM-dependent resolution vulnerable to favoritism
- Ranked attributes created intra-party hierarchy frustrating lower-ranked players
- Diceless approach felt arbitrary without randomization's objectivity
- Required sophisticated GMs capable of consistent fair adjudication
- Limited mechanical complexity disappointed tactical gamers
- Niche appeal limited audience growth
- Dependent on Zelazny's specific literary property

**Community Reception**:

Amber cultivated intensely devoted but small community:
- **Enthusiasts**: Loved narrative focus and diceless resolution
- **Skeptics**: Questioned whether system was truly "game" without mechanical objectivity
- **Literary Fans**: Appreciated faithful adaptation
- **Political Players**: Enjoyed intrigue and family dynamics

**Legacy Assessment**:

Amber succeeded as:
- Proof-of-concept for diceless gaming
- Influential design experiment
- Faithful literary adaptation
- Cult classic with devoted following

It struggled with:
- Limited mainstream appeal
- GM dependency concerns
- Market scalability
- Property license restrictions

Despite limitations, Amber demonstrated that radically different mechanical approaches could succeed, influencing game design philosophy significantly.

## Legacy and Influence

Amber's legacy encompasses:

**Design Innovations**:
- Diceless conflict resolution
- Auction-based character creation
- Attribute comparison systems
- Good Stuff/Bad Stuff karma mechanic
- Power-based customization

**Cultural Contributions**:
- Proved diceless gaming viability
- Inspired subsequent diceless games
- Demonstrated literary adaptation potential

**Ongoing Relevance**:

While out of print, Amber remains influential and available through secondary market and PDFs. For players seeking diceless narrative-focused play emphasizing character competence and political intrigue, Amber remains touchstone and inspiration.
