# Star Wars RPG (West End Games)

**Publisher**: [[West End Games]]
**Designer**: [[Greg Costikyan]]
**Year**: 1987
**System**: d6 System
**Genre**: science fiction, space opera, cinematic adventure
**Status**: out-of-print (current edition by Fantasy Flight Games)

## Overview

The Star Wars Roleplaying Game, designed by Greg Costikyan and published by West End Games in 1987, stands as one of the most influential licensed RPGs ever created—a game that not only captured the cinematic feel of Star Wars better than any adaptation before or since, but also revolutionized how designers approached fast-paced, narrative-focused gameplay. Released during the franchise's "dark years" between Return of the Jedi (1983) and the Special Edition re-releases (1997), the game kept Star Wars alive in popular culture while simultaneously developing an Expanded Universe of characters, planets, and stories that would shape the franchise for decades.

The d6 System's elegance—rolling pools of six-sided dice against target difficulties, with wild dice creating dramatic swings—created exactly the feel of Star Wars action: heroes accomplishing incredible feats while occasionally suffering spectacular setbacks. Combat resolved quickly without sacrificing tactical interest. Force powers felt mysterious and powerful without overwhelming gameplay. Character creation was fast, allowing players to jump immediately into adventure. The entire system prioritized getting out of the rules' way so players could experience Star Wars stories.

What made West End's Star Wars RPG historically crucial was its dual legacy. First, it proved licensed games could achieve mechanical excellence and critical acclaim rather than serving as mere brand cash-ins. The game won numerous industry awards and maintained passionate following throughout its run. Second, and perhaps more importantly, it became the de facto keeper of Star Wars continuity during George Lucas's absence from active franchise management. West End's sourcebooks—detailing planets, species, technology, and history—provided the foundation for the Expanded Universe that would dominate Star Wars media until Disney's 2014 continuity reset.

The game's influence on RPG design cannot be overstated. The d6 System demonstrated that dice pool mechanics could be intuitive and fast-paced. Its emphasis on cinematic description over tactical simulation influenced a generation of narrative-focused games. The wild die mechanic—one die in each pool that could create critical successes or failures—became template for dramatic swing mechanics. The game showed that licensed properties could inspire mechanically innovative games rather than constraining them to conventional systems.

For many fans, West End's Star Wars RPG defined their experience of the franchise more than the films themselves. The game ran from 1987 to 1999, spanning twelve years and producing over 140 sourcebooks, adventures, and supplements. It survived West End Games' financial troubles, edition revisions, and eventually the company's bankruptcy. When Fantasy Flight Games acquired Star Wars RPG rights in 2011, they created entirely new system, but West End's d6 System remained beloved by devoted community who continued playing decades after official support ended.

## Historical Context

In 1987, Star Wars existed in peculiar cultural limbo. The original trilogy had concluded with Return of the Jedi four years earlier. No new films were planned. The franchise seemed dormant—beloved but past its cultural peak. George Lucas focused on other projects, particularly developing his digital effects company Industrial Light & Magic. For Star Wars fans, the mid-to-late 1980s felt like wilderness years—the story seemed finished, with no new content on the horizon.

West End Games, a company primarily known for wargames and their darkly comedic Paranoia RPG, saw opportunity in this apparent dormancy. They secured Star Wars RPG license and assigned the project to Greg Costikyan, an experienced designer who had co-created Paranoia and worked on numerous other games. The challenge was significant: create an RPG capturing Star Wars' cinematic action, accessible enough for mainstream audiences attracted by the license, yet mechanically interesting enough for experienced gamers.

Costikyan built on work done for West End's Ghostbusters RPG (1986), which had pioneered elegant dice pool mechanics using d6es. That game demonstrated that simple systems could create cinematic feel—roll dice pools against difficulties, describe results narratively, move quickly through scenes. For Star Wars, Costikyan refined and expanded these mechanics, creating the d6 System that would become West End's signature.

The timing proved fortuitous. TSR dominated RPG market with AD&D, but licensed games remained niche territory with mixed quality. Previous licensed RPGs often felt like thinly-reskinned D&D or used clunky custom systems that failed to capture source material's feel. West End's approach—building mechanics specifically to generate Star Wars experiences—represented philosophical shift in licensed game design.

The first edition rulebook, published in 1987, featured distinctive blue cover with Ralph McQuarrie artwork. The book was remarkably accessible—clearly written, well-organized, filled with examples from the films. Character creation was fast: choose a template (providing pre-assigned attributes and skills) or build custom character with dice to allocate. Players could create characters and start playing within minutes, crucial for attracting Star Wars fans who weren't experienced gamers.

The game's initial success surprised many in the industry. It won numerous awards, including Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules. Sales were strong enough to justify extensive supplement line. More importantly, it demonstrated that licensed RPGs could achieve critical acclaim and commercial success simultaneously—they didn't have to be compromised products trading on brand recognition alone.

West End's relationship with Lucasfilm evolved into something unprecedented in licensed gaming. Lucasfilm granted West End significant creative freedom while requiring everything published align with their vision of the Star Wars universe. This led to West End maintaining extensive continuity documents—tracking every planet, species, vehicle, and character mentioned across all supplements. These documents became the foundation for Lucasfilm's own continuity management and the Expanded Universe that would flourish through novels, comics, and games in the 1990s.

The game's sourcebooks became primary Star Wars reference materials. The Imperial Sourcebook detailed the Empire's military structure. The Rebel Alliance Sourcebook explained the rebellion's organization. Galaxy Guides explored specific planets and sectors. These books weren't just game materials—they were definitive Star Wars references, used by authors, comic creators, and even Lucasfilm itself.

Second Edition, released in 1992, refined the system with nine years of accumulated experience. It streamlined some rules, reorganized content for clarity, and incorporated Expanded Universe material that had developed since 1987. This edition, with its black cover featuring Brian Stelfreeze art, became the definitive version for many players.

The game flourished throughout the early-to-mid 1990s. West End published galaxy guides, adventure modules, campaign sourcebooks, equipment guides, and more. The sheer volume of material was staggering—over 140 products released over twelve years. This extensive support built loyal community and created comprehensive Star Wars gaming resource.

However, West End Games faced financial difficulties in the late 1990s. Company management issues, market changes, and business decisions unrelated to Star Wars RPG created mounting problems. When Lucasfilm learned of these troubles and concerns about proper license management, they declined to renew the license. West End's Star Wars RPG officially ended in 1999, though the company struggled on until final bankruptcy in 2004.

Wizards of the Coast acquired Star Wars RPG license in 2000, creating d20 System version that ran until 2010. Fantasy Flight Games took over in 2011 with entirely new narrative dice system. But for many fans, West End's d6 version remained definitive—the system that captured Star Wars' feel perfectly, the game that expanded the universe, the rulebook that introduced them to Star Wars roleplaying.

The game's legacy extended beyond its mechanical innovations. West End's Expanded Universe material directly influenced Timothy Zahn's Thrall Trilogy novels (1991-1993), which revitalized Star Wars fiction. The game kept Star Wars alive in popular culture during the franchise's dormant period, maintaining fan enthusiasm that would explode when Lucas announced the Prequel Trilogy in the mid-1990s.

## Core Mechanics

The d6 System's brilliance lay in its elegant simplicity combined with surprising tactical depth:

**Dice Pool Foundation**: Characters had attributes (Dexterity, Knowledge, Mechanical, Perception, Strength, Technical) rated in dice—typically 2D to 4D for humans. Skills added additional dice to relevant attribute. When attempting action, you rolled your dice pool and summed the results against target difficulty number.

For example, a character with Dexterity 3D and Blaster 2D rolled 5D when shooting, summing the results. If they rolled 18 total against difficulty 15, they succeeded. This created intuitive resolution—more dice meant better capability, higher rolls meant better success.

**The Wild Die**: One die in every pool was a different color—the wild die. Rolling 6 on the wild die meant roll again and add both results, potentially creating spectacular successes. Rolling 1 on the wild die meant potential complication—if the total still succeeded, perhaps minor issue occurred; if it failed, perhaps spectacular failure resulted. This single mechanic created Star Wars' signature dramatic swings—heroes pulling off incredible shots, critical failures creating disasters.

**Difficulty Numbers**: Tasks had difficulty numbers: Very Easy (1-5), Easy (6-10), Moderate (11-15), Difficult (16-20), Very Difficult (21-30), Heroic (31+). GM set difficulties based on circumstances. This provided clear framework while allowing situational adjustment.

**Fast Character Creation**: Templates provided pre-generated character concepts—Smuggler, Bounty Hunter, Jedi, Rebel Soldier, Diplomat, etc. Players chose template, customized with background details, added starting equipment, and began playing. Custom creation allowed allocating dice to attributes and skills with starting pools. Either way, character creation took 10-15 minutes maximum.

**Character Points**: Players earned Character Points for good roleplaying, clever plans, heroic actions. They could spend Character Points before rolling to add dice to pools, or after rolling to improve results. This rewarded engaging play while providing narrative control—spend points when success really mattered.

**Force Points**: Rare and precious, Force Points doubled all dice in a pool for one roll. Using Force Point for dark purposes (killing innocents, selfish acts) turned it to Dark Side Point. Accumulating too many Dark Side Points meant losing the character to darkness. Force Points mechanized Star Wars' moral framework—great power available, but at potential spiritual cost.

**Force Powers**: Jedi characters could learn Force powers (telekinesis, mind tricks, precognition, etc.). Powers required Control, Sense, or Alter skill rolls against difficulties. Powers felt powerful but costly—using them required concentration and could fail at crucial moments. The system made Force users powerful without overwhelming non-Force characters.

**Combat**: Initiative determined by Perception. Each round, characters declared actions, then resolved in initiative order. Attacks rolled dice pool against defense difficulty. Damage over armor/toughness caused wounds. Combat resolved quickly—typical fight took 15-30 minutes regardless of participants.

**Wound Levels**: Characters had wound levels: Unwounded, Stunned, Wounded, Incapacitated, Mortally Wounded, Dead. Each wound level imposed dice penalties on actions. This created cinematic damage—heroes fought through injuries (accepting penalties) or were taken out dramatically.

**Vehicle Combat**: Vehicles used same basic system with larger scale. Starfighter dogfights, speeder chases, and capital ship battles all resolved with dice pools against difficulties. Scale modifiers made starfighter attacking Death Star appropriately difficult. The system handled everything from personal combat to space battles with unified mechanics.

**Skill Advancement**: Characters improved through Character Point expenditure. Spending points equal to dice to next level improved skills or attributes. This created gradual advancement—characters grew more capable over campaigns without level-based power jumps.

**Narrative Focus**: Rules explicitly encouraged narrative description over mechanical detail. GMs were told to set difficulties based on dramatic needs, adjust for circumstances, and prioritize story flow over rules literalism. This philosophy pervaded the system—mechanics served story rather than constraining it.

**Fast Resolution**: Most actions resolved with single dice roll. The system avoided complex subsystems and extensive modifiers. This kept play moving at cinematic pace—scenes resolved quickly, combat was exciting but brief, adventures maintained momentum.

**Tactical Depth Despite Simplicity**: While mechanically simple, the system offered tactical decisions. Spend Character Points now or save for critical moment? Accept wound penalties or withdraw? Use Force Point for guaranteed success? These choices created engagement without complexity.

**Second Edition Refinements**: The 1992 Second Edition streamlined some rules, clarified ambiguities, and incorporated accumulated errata. It added "dice codes" notation (4D+2 instead of 4D and 2 pips), simplified Force Point mechanics slightly, and improved organization. Core system remained unchanged.

## Setting and Themes

West End's Star Wars RPG was set primarily in the Original Trilogy era—between A New Hope and Return of the Jedi, though materials covered from Old Republic to post-Endor. The game assumed players were Rebel heroes fighting the Empire, though other campaigns (smugglers, bounty hunters, Imperial agents) were supported.

**The Rebellion**: Default assumption was players fought for the Rebel Alliance—heroes opposing tyranny, protecting innocents, striking against overwhelming Imperial forces. Adventures involved espionage, military raids, rescue missions, and guerrilla warfare. The asymmetric conflict (small rebellion versus galactic empire) created inherent drama.

**The Empire**: The antagonist—oppressive military dictatorship controlling most of the galaxy. The Empire represented order through force, crushing dissent, exploiting populations. Imperial forces were numerous and powerful but bureaucratic and overconfident. Adventures often involved outmaneuvering Imperial might through cunning and heroism.

**The Force**: Central mystery and power source. Jedi were nearly extinct, making Force-sensitive characters rare and special. The Light/Dark Side dichotomy created moral framework—would characters use power responsibly or succumb to darkness? The game emphasized this struggle through Force Point mechanics and Dark Side Point accumulation.

**Galaxy Far, Far Away**: The setting encompassed thousands of planets, countless species, and ancient history. West End's sourcebooks expanded this dramatically—detailing planets, species, governments, corporations, criminal organizations, and more. This vast universe provided endless adventure possibilities.

**Expanded Universe Development**: West End's sourcebooks created much of what became the Star Wars Expanded Universe:
- **Grand Admiral Thrawn** (mentioned in sourcebooks before appearing in novels)
- **Coruscant** as the Imperial capital (the planet wasn't named in the films)
- Extensive Wookiee culture and Kashyyyk details
- Imperial military organization and ranks
- Countless planets and species
- Technological specifications for vehicles and equipment
- Historical events and timelines

These creations became official Star Wars canon (pre-2014), influencing novels, comics, video games, and eventually the Prequel Trilogy.

**Themes**:

- **Rebellion Against Tyranny**: Core theme—small group of dedicated heroes can oppose overwhelming evil through courage and sacrifice
- **Moral Choice**: The Light/Dark Side struggle represented moral decisions—would characters act selflessly or selfishly?
- **Found Family**: Typical groups bonded through shared struggles, creating surrogate families like the original trilogy's heroes
- **Redemption**: Even those who fall can return to light, echoing Vader's redemption
- **Adventure and Discovery**: Galaxy full of mysteries, ancient civilizations, unknown threats
- **Technology vs. Faith**: Empire relied on technological might while rebels included Force users representing spiritual power
- **Underdog Heroes**: Small rebellion versus galactic empire meant heroes were always outgunned, requiring cleverness and determination
- **Legacy and Destiny**: Characters might discover Force sensitivity, family connections, or destinies larger than themselves

## Cultural Impact

West End's Star Wars RPG achieved cultural impact far beyond typical licensed games:

**Keeping Star Wars Alive**: During the franchise's dormant period (1983-1991), the RPG was primary source of new Star Wars content. It maintained fan enthusiasm, introduced new generations to the universe, and kept the franchise visible until novels revitalized it.

**Creating the Expanded Universe**: West End's sourcebooks provided foundation for the Expanded Universe—the shared continuity across novels, comics, and games. Authors like Timothy Zahn, Kevin J. Anderson, and Michael A. Stackpole used West End materials as reference. The game's continuity documents became Lucasfilm's official reference.

**Establishing Continuity Management**: West End's approach to maintaining consistent continuity across products influenced how Lucasfilm managed the broader franchise. The detailed tracking of planets, species, and history became model for transmedia storytelling.

**Proving Licensed Games Could Excel**: Before West End's Star Wars, licensed RPGs were generally considered inferior products. West End demonstrated that licensed games could achieve mechanical innovation and critical acclaim, changing industry perception of what licensed properties could be.

**Influencing Cinematic RPG Design**: The d6 System's emphasis on fast resolution, narrative description, and dramatic swings influenced subsequent "cinematic" RPGs. Games like Feng Shui, Wushu, and others drew on similar principles—mechanics supporting exciting description rather than detailed simulation.

**Dice Pool Mechanics**: While not the first dice pool system, Star Wars RPG popularized the approach and demonstrated its viability for mainstream games. Many subsequent games adopted dice pool resolution.

**Wild Die Concept**: The wild die mechanic—one die creating dramatic swings—appeared in various forms across numerous later games. It demonstrated how single mechanical element could create genre-appropriate feel.

**Template-Based Creation**: Quick character creation using templates influenced many games seeking accessibility. The approach showed that detailed customization could be optional rather than required.

**Commercial Success**: The game sold well throughout its run, proving licensed RPGs could be commercially viable long-term. Over 140 products demonstrated market appetite for extensive support.

**Community Longevity**: Even after official support ended in 1999, devoted community continued playing. Fan sites hosted resources, adventures, and errata. Online communities kept the game alive, with new players discovering it decades after publication.

**Awards and Recognition**:
- Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules (1988)
- Multiple Origins nominations for supplements
- Inducted into Origins Hall of Fame
- Regularly cited as one of gaming's best licensed RPGs
- Featured prominently in RPG history discussions

**Influence on Star Wars Media**: Characters, planets, and concepts created for the RPG appeared in novels, comics, video games, and eventually animated series. The game directly shaped Star Wars transmedia storytelling.

**Educational Impact**: The game introduced many young players to roleplaying, attracted by Star Wars license. It served as gateway game, teaching RPG fundamentals through familiar franchise.

**International Reach**: Translated into multiple languages, the game found audiences worldwide, spreading both the RPG hobby and Star Wars fandom internationally.

## Reception and Criticism

**Critical Acclaim**:
- Won Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Rules
- Praised for capturing Star Wars' cinematic feel perfectly
- Recognized for elegant, accessible mechanics
- Lauded for extensive, high-quality sourcebook line
- Appreciated for respecting source material while expanding universe
- Noted for excellent production values and artwork
- Considered gold standard for licensed RPGs

**Player Reception**:
- Embraced by Star Wars fans as definitive RPG version
- Appreciated by experienced gamers for mechanical elegance
- Praised for fast character creation and gameplay
- Valued for extensive setting material
- Enjoyed for supporting various play styles (military, smuggling, Jedi, etc.)
- Community remained devoted decades after official support ended

**Criticisms**:
- **Force User Balance**: Some felt Jedi characters overshadowed non-Force users, though rules limited Jedi availability
- **Template Limitations**: While templates accelerated creation, some players wanted more customization guidance
- **Setting Constraint**: Being locked to specific era (Original Trilogy) limited some campaigns
- **Power Scaling**: Long campaigns sometimes struggled with power progression—characters became extremely capable
- **Simple Combat**: Some players wanted more tactical depth in combat
- **Force Powers**: Some found Force power system too open-ended or too restrictive depending on interpretation

**Edition Debates**:
- First Edition vs. Second Edition sparked preferences—Second was cleaner, but First had nostalgic charm
- Later "Revised and Expanded" edition received mixed reception for rules changes

**Commercial Challenges**:
- West End Games' financial troubles tarnished the game's final years
- License loss in 1999 felt premature to devoted fans
- Subsequent d20 version divided community—some appreciated modern system, others preferred d6

**Historical Perspective**:
Looking back, West End's Star Wars RPG is recognized as:
- One of the best licensed RPGs ever created
- Mechanically innovative for its era
- Culturally significant for maintaining Star Wars franchise
- Foundation of Expanded Universe continuity
- Influential on cinematic RPG design
- Example of excellent game design regardless of licensed property

The game's reputation has only improved over time. Modern designers and players recognize its achievements—capturing cinematic feel through elegant mechanics, creating vast setting resources, proving licensed games could excel, and maintaining franchise during dormant period.

## Legacy and Influence

West End's Star Wars RPG left indelible mark on gaming and the Star Wars franchise:

**Mechanical Legacy**:
- Demonstrated dice pool viability for mainstream games
- Wild die concept influenced dramatic swing mechanics across games
- Template-based character creation became common accessibility tool
- Cinematic resolution priority influenced narrative game design
- Unified mechanics handling personal and vehicle scale

**Design Philosophy Legacy**:
- Proved licensed games could innovate mechanically
- Showed fast resolution could coexist with tactical depth
- Demonstrated importance of mechanical support for genre feel
- Influenced thinking about accessibility versus customization
- Validated narrative description priority over simulation detail

**Star Wars Legacy**:
- Created foundation of Expanded Universe continuity
- Established Coruscant as capital (later used in Prequels)
- Developed countless planets, species, and characters used across media
- Maintained franchise visibility during dormant period
- Influenced Timothy Zahn's Thrawn Trilogy and subsequent novels
- Provided reference materials used by Lucasfilm and creators

**Industry Legacy**:
- Proved licensed RPGs could achieve critical acclaim
- Demonstrated extensive support line viability
- Showed importance of maintaining continuity across products
- Influenced licensed RPG design approach
- Validated cinematic play style as distinct design goal

**D6 System Legacy**:
- System was extracted and published as standalone OpenD6
- Used for various West End Games products (D6 Space, D6 Adventure, D6 Fantasy)
- Community continues developing OpenD6 materials
- Influenced subsequent dice pool systems

**Ongoing Community**:
- Active fan sites maintain resources, adventures, conversions

- Retro-clones and spiritual successors developed by fans
- OpenD6 community keeps system alive

**Influence on Subsequent Star Wars RPGs**:
- Wizards' d20 version (2000-2010) used different mechanics but kept West End's setting material
- Fantasy Flight's narrative dice system (2011-present) draws on West End's cinematic emphasis
- All subsequent Star Wars RPGs exist in West End's shadow—measured against its achievement

**Cultural Touchstone**:
