# Dogs in the Vineyard

**Publisher**: [[Lumpley Games]]
**Designer**: [[D. Vincent Baker]]
**Year**: 2004
**System**: Dogs in the Vineyard System
**Genre**: western, religious-drama, moral-conflict
**Status**: out-of-print (designer-initiated)

## Overview

Dogs in the Vineyard, designed by Vincent Baker and published in 2004, stands as one of the most influential and controversial indie RPGs ever created—a game about young religious lawkeepers in alternate-history American West who travel from town to town carrying out God's will, facing moral dilemmas where their judgment determines lives, souls, and communities' fates. Players portray "Dogs"—divinely-ordained authority figures empowered to pronounce judgment on sin, cure spiritual corruption, and execute God's justice as they understand it—creating gameplay where moral certainty collides with human complexity in towns sliding toward damnation.

The game's genius lies in its mechanical elegance serving thematic purpose. The conflict system uses dice pool escalation mechanics where you can always win if willing to escalate—talking becomes physical, fists become weapons, bullets become potential death. This creates agonizing choices: how far will you go to impose your will? Will you shoot someone over theological dispute? The mechanics make violence simultaneously tempting (guarantees success) and costly (endangers everyone), perfectly capturing moral weight of authoritarian violence.

The setting—loosely inspired by early Mormon history—places Dogs in isolated frontier communities beset by supernatural evil arising from human sin. Pride, lust, greed don't just corrupt individuals; they literally summon demons and manifest curses. The Dogs must diagnose spiritual sickness (what sin caused this?), judge sinners (what does God demand?), and prescribe remedy (repentance? Exile? Execution?). The game never provides right answers—players must make difficult judgments with imperfect information, then face consequences.

Critically, the game avoids endorsing the theocratic authority it depicts. Players aren't encouraged to agree with their characters' religious certainty but to explore how conviction empowers and corrupts, how authority functions and fails, how good intentions produce catastrophic harm. This meta-textual complexity—playing characters acting with moral certainty while you question that certainty—creates profound gameplay experiences examining power, faith, violence, and judgment.

However, this thematic richness also made the game controversial. Playing religious authority figures empowered to execute judgment made some players uncomfortable. The Mormon-inspired setting, while fictionalized, touched on sensitive religious history. Baker's stated design goals—exploring how people use religious authority to hurt others—confronted players with complicity in systems they might oppose. These tensions contributed to Baker discontinuing sales in 2019, citing concerns about play culture misaligned with design intent.

Despite commercial discontinuation, Dogs in the Vineyard's influence pervades indie gaming. The escalation mechanics directly inspired Apocalypse World's harm system. The conflict resolution framework influenced countless narrative games. The thematic ambition—using mechanics to examine difficult questions rather than provide escapism—became standard for story games movement. The game demonstrated RPGs could be morally complex art examining human systems, not just entertainment.

## Historical Context

Dogs in the Vineyard emerged from The Forge—online community where designers discussed Ron Edwards's GNS Theory (Gamist/Narrativist/Simulationist) and pursued "story games" prioritizing narrative over simulation or challenge. Vincent Baker, active Forge participant, designed Dogs explicitly as narrativist game: mechanics exist to generate dramatic story, not simulate reality or provide tactical challenge.

Baker drew inspiration from multiple sources:
- Early Mormon history, particularly pioneer-era communities and theological concepts
- Western fiction traditions, especially moral westerns examining justice and violence
- Moral philosophy questions about authority, judgment, and violence
- The Forge's design theories about conflict resolution and player authority

The game's development occurred publicly on The Forge, with playtest feedback shaping mechanics. Baker wanted system where:
1. Violence is always option but never mandatory
2. Escalation creates mechanical advantage and moral cost
3. Players can win any conflict if willing to pay price
4. Mechanics force difficult choices mirroring thematic concerns

The 2004 release positioned Dogs at intersection of indie RPG's experimental phase and emerging narrative design sophistication. Games like Sorcerer and My Life with Master had established indie credentials; Dogs demonstrated how mechanical innovation could serve thematic depth.

The setting's Mormon inspiration was intentional but fictionalized. Baker created "Faith" rather than explicitly depicting Mormonism, though parallels were clear: frontier communities, ongoing revelation, authoritarian priesthood structure, peculiar theology. This created both richness (grounded in historical reality) and controversy (potentially appropriating/criticizing religious history).

Critical reception was rapturous within indie gaming circles. The game won multiple awards including Origins Award for Best RPG 2005. Reviewers praised the mechanical elegance, thematic boldness, and how systems supported intended experience. The Forge community celebrated Dogs as exemplar of narrativist design achieving its goals.

However, broader reception varied. Traditional gamers sometimes found the moral complexity uncomfortable. The religious themes troubled some players. The escalation mechanics, while brilliant structurally, created tense gameplay where violence loomed constantly. Some groups struggled with the game's fundamental question: "What gives you the right to judge?"

The game's influence grew through designer osmosis. Vincent Baker's subsequent masterwork, Apocalypse World (2010), evolved directly from Dogs's conflict mechanics. Numerous designers cited Dogs as formative influence. The game became case study in design courses and indie development discussions.

In 2019, Baker discontinued Dogs in the Vineyard sales, citing concerns that play culture had drifted from design intent. He worried players were role-playing authoritarian violence uncritically rather than examining it. While the game remains legally available through earlier purchases, Baker no longer supports or promotes it, recommending instead that designers create new games building on its innovations without its problematic elements.

This discontinuation sparked extensive discussion about designer responsibility, thematic content handling, and when games become liabilities rather than assets. However, it didn't diminish Dogs's historical significance—the innovations remain influential, the design lessons still valuable, even as the specific implementation became uncomfortable for its creator.

## Core Mechanics

Dogs uses elegant dice pool system with escalation mechanics creating moral tension:

### Character Creation: Defining Authority

Characters are "Dogs"—recent graduates of Divine Service academy sent to frontier communities as wandering lawkeepers. Creation involves:

**Stats**: Acuity (noticing things), Body (physical action), Heart (emotional/social interaction), Will (determination)—rated 2d6 to 4d6

**Traits**: Descriptive qualities ("I'm a Dead Shot" 2d6, "I Never Back Down" 3d10) providing bonus dice when relevant

**Relationships**: Connections to NPCs providing dice when stakes involve them

**Belongings**: Significant items (favorite gun, inherited coat, religious texts) providing dice

**History**: "Complicated" background (witnessing violence, family shame, doubts about Faith) or "Strong" background (faithful upbringing, natural conviction) determining trait dice size

This creates characters mechanically defined by skills, convictions, relationships, and possessions—everything matters for conflicts.

### Conflict Resolution: Escalation Mechanics

When characters have opposed goals, initiate conflict:

1. **Set Stakes**: "Do I convince the town elder to confess?" Not "Can I talk to them?" but specific outcome desired

2. **Gather Dice**: Take dice for relevant stat (talking = Acuity + Heart), applicable traits, relationships, belongings

3. **First Volley**: 
   - Active party "Raises" (makes bid using 2+ dice, stating action)
   - Opponent "Sees" (matches raise using dice, narrating response) or "Takes the Blow" (accepts consequence, exits conflict)

4. **Continue Volleys**: Exchange raises and sees until someone can't/won't see, accepting stakes resolution

**The Escalation Hook**: Different action types use different dice:
- **Just Talking**: Only stat dice
- **Physical but Not Fighting**: Add trait/belongings dice for intimidation, physical acts
- **Fighting (fists/improvised weapons)**: Add fighting-trait dice, get fallout (potential harm)
- **Fighting (guns/weapons)**: Add weapon dice (d6-d10), risk serious fallout
- **Supernatural/Divine Intervention**: Add ritual/faith dice, powerful but expensive

Each escalation level adds dice, making you likelier to win BUT increases potential harm (fallout). You can always escalate to add more dice—but should you?

**Example**: 
- Dog argues with corrupt elder (using talking dice: 4d6)
- Elder won't back down, Dog escalates to grabbing shirt (adds physical dice: +3d6)
- Elder still resisting, Dog draws gun (adds weapon dice: +2d8 gun)
- Dog has many more dice now but risks killing someone over argument

### Fallout: Consequences of Violence

When conflict involves physical/gun violence, loser takes fallout:
- Roll fallout dice (d4 for physical, d10 for guns)
- High result determines severity:
  - 1-7: Exhaustion/bruising
  - 8-12: Injury requiring medical attention
  - 13-16: Severe injury, permanent consequences
  - 17+: Death (unless someone intervenes)

Fallout creates lasting traits—"Shot in the Gut" becomes permanent character descriptor affecting future conflicts.

This makes violence mechanically advantageous (more dice = likelier to win) but narratively costly (permanent harm, guilt, changed character definition).

### Reflection and Fallout Recovery

Between towns, Dogs "reflect" on experiences:
- Discuss conflicts and decisions
- Resolve fallout (healing, therapy, spiritual counsel)
- Develop new traits from experiences ("I've Seen Too Much Violence" 2d6)

This mechanical recovery time also provides narrative space for processing difficult choices.

### Town Creation: Generative Sin

Game masters create towns using "ladder of sin" template:
1. **Pride**: Someone's pride creates opening for corruption
2. **Injustice**: Pride manifests as unfair treatment
3. **Sin**: Injustice enables actual wrongdoing
4. **False Doctrine**: Sin justifies itself through theological distortion
5. **Corrupt Worship**: Community embraces false teaching
6. **False Priesthood**: Leaders arise promoting corruption
7. **Hate and Murder**: Violence erupts
8. **What Do The Demons Want?**: Supernatural evil manifests pursuing goal

This template ensures towns have:
- Complex situation arising from human failing
- Multiple "guilty" parties
- No obvious right answer
- Supernatural manifestation (demons, curses, possessions)

**Example Town**: 
- Pride: Steward wants to control water rights
- Injustice: Denies water to farmer he dislikes
- Sin: Farmer's daughter seduces steward for water access
- False Doctrine: "God favors the strong; weakness deserves suffering"
- Corrupt Worship: Sermons preach prosperity gospel
- False Priesthood: Steward's brother becomes steward of souls
- Hate and Murder: Farmer plans to murder steward
- Demons: Want community to abandon Faith entirely

Dogs arrive at any point on ladder and must diagnose situation, judge involved parties, and prescribe remedy—but what's just? What's merciful? What does God want?

### GM Role: Provocateur Not Adversary

GM creates challenging situations but doesn't oppose players:
- Towns present dilemmas without predetermined solutions
- NPCs have motivations, not plot armor
- Consequences flow from player choices
- GM pushes Dogs to make difficult judgment calls
- Never punish "wrong" choices—no right answers exist

This creates collaborative tension where GM provides moral complexity and players must navigate it.

### Group Dynamics: No Party Unity Required

Dogs don't have to agree. One Dog might want mercy, another justice, a third prevention through exile. Internal conflicts are legitimate gameplay—Dogs can conflict with each other using same mechanics.

This reflects game's thematic interest in authority: when authorities disagree, whose judgment prevails?

## Setting and Themes

### The Faith: Alternate Mormon-Inspired Religion

The game's setting features "the Faith"—frontier religion resembling early Mormonism:
- Ongoing divine revelation
- Priesthood authority structure
- Peculiar theology (God speaking through anointed)
- Pioneer communities isolated in mountain valleys
- Hierarchical but decentralized organization

The Faith is explicitly fictional, though Mormon parallels create historical resonance. Baker encouraged hacking setting for other contexts (Puritan witch-hunters, Inquisitors, etc.).

### The Dogs: Divine Authority

Player characters are "Watchdogs of the Lord"—young people (typically late teens/early twenties) given:
- Religious authority to pronounce judgment
- Physical authority to enforce decisions
- Social authority as God's representatives
- Guns and backing of the Faith

They travel circuit, visiting isolated communities, addressing problems. Their judgment is final—no appeals, no oversight, just their understanding of God's will.

### Sin and Demons: Spiritual Reality

In setting, sin literally summons demons. Pride opens door; subsequent sins invite evil in; demons manifest corrupting communities:
- Possessing individuals
- Cursing land (crops fail, water poisons)
- Manifesting as monsters
- Corrupting hearts toward hatred

This makes moral choices cosmically significant—your judgment affects not just humans but spiritual realm.

### Towns: Microcosms of Corruption

Each town is isolated community with specific social dynamics, power structures, and brewing conflict. Dogs arrive as outsiders with authority to reorganize social order—but should they?

### Violence and Authority: Central Themes

The game explores:
- **Authoritarian Power**: What happens when individuals have absolute authority?
- **Conviction vs Uncertainty**: How do you judge with moral certainty when situations are complex?
- **Violence as Tool**: When (if ever) is violence justified?
- **Systemic Harm**: How do good intentions create suffering?
- **Complicity**: What responsibility do authority figures bear for outcomes?

### Moral Ambiguity: No Right Answers

Critically, the game provides no moral guidance. You must decide:
- Is adultery worth killing over?
- Should false teaching be punished if believers are happy?
- Is mercy or justice more godly?
- Who deserves redemption?

Different Dogs will answer differently. The game doesn't judge your choices—but it makes you face consequences.

## Cultural Impact

**Escalation Mechanics Innovation**: The dice pool escalation system directly influenced Apocalypse World's harm mechanic and numerous subsequent narrative games.

**Conflict Resolution Framework**: Demonstrated how mechanical conflict systems could create dramatic tension while distributing narrative authority.

**Moral Complexity in Gaming**: Proved RPGs could examine difficult ethical questions without providing comfortable answers.

**Religious Themes**: Showed religious content could provide rich thematic material when handled thoughtfully (and controversially when not).

**Narrativist Design Success**: Validated The Forge's design theories, demonstrating narrativist games could achieve mechanical elegance and critical acclaim.

**Indie Design Influence**: Inspired generation of designers to pursue thematically ambitious, mechanically innovative projects.

**Origins Award 2005**: Won Best RPG, giving indie narrativist design mainstream recognition.

**Designer Evolution**: Baker's development from Dogs to Apocalypse World demonstrated how designers could iterate on innovations.

**Critical Discourse**: Generated extensive discussion about game design, thematic content, and designer responsibility.

**Discontinuation Precedent**: Baker's 2019 decision to stop selling the game created important conversation about when creators should retire problematic works.

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Brilliant escalation mechanics creating genuine moral tension
- Elegant system serving thematic purpose
- Thought-provoking gameplay forcing difficult choices
- Innovative conflict resolution influencing genre
- Successfully examining power and authority
- Beautiful integration of mechanics and theme
- Accessible rules complexity
- Generates unforgettable play experiences

**Criticisms**:
- **Religious Appropriation**: Mormon-inspired setting troubled some, feeling exploitative
- **Uncomfortable Themes**: Playing authoritarian violence disturbed players
- **Potential Misuse**: Could enable uncritical power fantasy if played without reflection
- **Limited Replayability**: Core tensions resolve through repeated play
- **Narrow Setting**: Western religious context doesn't appeal universally
- **Designer Discontinuation**: Creator's discomfort with play culture validated concerns

**Community Response**:
- Devoted following within indie gaming
- Extensive actual-play documentation
- Academic attention for thematic complexity
- Heated debates about appropriateness
- Continued influence despite discontinuation

**Baker's Perspective**:
In 2019, Baker explained discontinuation:
"I've come to believe that the game as written, even with safety tools, creates space for players to enact violence they wouldn't normally condone. I don't want to be responsible for that anymore."

This honesty about design consequences elevated discussion about creator responsibility.

## Legacy and Influence

**Apocalypse World Connection**: Baker's subsequent masterwork evolved directly from Dogs's conflict mechanics, creating Powered by the Apocalypse engine.

**Escalation Systems**: The escalation framework appears in numerous games exploring moral choices and violence.

**Indie Design Standard**: Established benchmark for integrating theme and mechanics, studied by aspiring designers.

**Moral Gaming Movement**: Inspired games examining ethics, authority, and difficult choices through gameplay.

**Conflict Resolution Innovation**: The raise/see mechanic influenced narrative conflict systems broadly.

**Setting Hacking Culture**: Encouraged designers to hack mechanical frameworks for different settings/themes.

**Critical Play**: Demonstrated games could critique systems (authoritarianism, religious violence) through play itself.

**Designer Responsibility Discourse**: The discontinuation sparked important conversations about when creators should retire works.

**Educational Tool**: Used in game design courses studying mechanics-theme integration.

**Continued Reference**: Remains touchstone for discussing narrative mechanics and moral complexity in gaming.
