# Primetime Adventures

**Publisher**: Dog Eared Designs
**Designer**: Matt Wilson
**Year**: 2004
**System**: Primetime Adventures System
**Genre**: television-simulation, narrative-drama
**Status**: in-print

## Overview

Primetime Adventures, designed by Matt Wilson and published in 2004, revolutionized narrative gaming by asking "what if we created RPG sessions structured like television seasons?"—producing a game where players collaboratively create TV series, assign screen time, resolve conflicts through narrative stakes rather than combat outcomes, and use Fan Mail as currency rewarding entertaining play. Rather than portraying adventurers exploring dungeons, players create protagonists in television series (cop dramas, medical shows, sci-fi, westerns, anything) with each session functioning as episode, each multi-session arc as season, and mechanical systems ensuring character development, dramatic pacing, and ensemble cast dynamics matching television storytelling.

The game's genius lies in how mechanical elements mirror television production: Screen Presence (rated 1-3 per episode) determines which characters get spotlight focus that session, rotating so every protagonist has featured episodes and supporting episodes throughout the season. Issue (character's central dramatic problem) provides thematic through-line similar to television protagonists' recurring struggles. Fan Mail (tokens earned by entertaining play) serves as resource for narrative control and conflict resolution, mechanically incentivizing playing to the audience (your fellow players).

The collaborative series creation process defines show concept before play begins: group decides genre, tone, setting, and crucially the show's Premise—what makes this series interesting? Then players create protagonists who would star in such a series, establishing connections ensuring ensemble cast rather than isolated individuals. The Producer (GM equivalent) creates adversity and supporting cast but the players drive story through protagonist choices and rotating spotlight focus.

Conflict resolution uses card-based Stakes setting rather than traditional success/failure: before conflict, players narrate what they want (the stakes), draw cards (modified by traits and Fan Mail), and high card wins. Crucially, winning doesn't mean "you succeed"—it means you narrate the outcome. You might win conflict and narrate your character's failure because that's more dramatically interesting. This divorces mechanical victory from fictional success, creating space for dramatically appropriate outcomes regardless of dice results.

The game achieved significant influence despite modest commercial success. It demonstrated television structure could organize RPG campaigns effectively. It validated collaborative series creation over GM-provided settings. It proved mechanical systems could incentivize entertaining play explicitly. And it showed narrative control could be distributed through stakes-setting and winner-narrates-outcome mechanics. While fewer people played Primetime Adventures than studied it, the design influenced how indie gamers thought about campaign structure, spotlight management, and collaborative creation.

## Historical Context

Primetime Adventures emerged from early 2000s indie RPG experimentation with narrative authority distribution and collaborative story creation. Matt Wilson, influenced by The Forge's narrativist discussions, wanted game explicitly modeling television storytelling rather than adapting traditional RPG structures to approximate TV-like play.

Development coincided with television's creative renaissance. Shows like The Sopranos, The Wire, Buffy the Vampire Slayer, and Lost demonstrated television could tell complex serialized narratives with character development rivaling novels. Wilson recognized television's narrative structures—ensemble casts, rotating character focus, seasonal arcs, episodic structure within ongoing stories—could organize RPG play more effectively than dungeon-crawling or mission-based structures.

The game drew inspiration from:
- **Television narrative conventions**: Serialized storytelling and character development
- **The Forge narrativism**: Player-driven story over pre-plotted scenarios
- **Collaborative story games**: Distributed authority and shared creation
- **Sorcerer**: Issue-based character development and Kicker-like premises

Initial publication through Dog Eared Designs (Wilson's small press) limited distribution but built passionate community through PDF sales and con demonstrations. The game found particular resonance among players interested in character drama over tactical combat and groups seeking alternatives to traditional GM-dominated play.

Critical reception praised the innovation and thematic coherence. The game won the 2005 Diana Jones Award for Excellence in Gaming (shared with Dogs in the Vineyard and other nominees), validating its design significance. Reviewers noted how elegantly mechanics supported television-style storytelling and how the collaborative creation process generated immediate investment.

However, commercial success remained limited. The television-focused framing created cognitive hurdle for traditional gamers. The winner-narrates-outcome conflict resolution felt alien to players expecting character success/failure to match mechanical results. The episodic structure didn't suit groups preferring open-ended sandbox campaigns. These factors meant Primetime Adventures influenced more designers than it attracted players.

The second edition (2005) refined mechanics and presentation but maintained core concepts. Subsequent years saw the game's influence spread through designer osmosis—numerous games adopted season structures, screen presence concepts, collaborative series creation, and Fan Mail-style currencies rewarding entertaining play.

## Core Mechanics

Primetime Adventures uses elegant card-based system modeling television production:

### Series Creation: Collaborative Pitch Session

Groups collaboratively create television series through structured process:

1. **Choose Genre**: Cop drama, medical show, sci-fi, western, supernatural thriller, etc.
2. **Define Premise**: What makes this series interesting? "Supernatural cops investigate paranormal crimes," "Doctors without borders provide emergency medicine in war zones," "Thieves pull heists while navigating relationships"
3. **Establish Tone**: Serious/comedic, dark/light, realistic/fantastic
4. **Set Type**: Series (ongoing characters) or Anthology (changing cast)
5. **Determine Season Length**: Typically 5-9 episodes

This collaborative creation ensures everyone invests in shared vision.

### Character Creation: Casting the Ensemble

Players create protagonists who would star in established series:

**Core Elements**:
- **Name and Concept**: Who are they in show's premise?
- **Connections**: Relationships to other protagonists ensuring ensemble cohesion
- **Issue**: Central personal problem driving character development (changes per season)
- **Personal Sets**: Locations associated with character
- **Edges**: Traits providing narrative justification and mechanical bonuses
- **Screen Presence**: Spotlight focus per episode (totaling 9 across 5-episode season)

**Example** (Supernatural Cop Show):
- Name: Detective Sarah Chen
- Concept: Skeptical detective who sees ghosts
- Connections: Partner is John (another PC), ex-lover is Maria (another PC)
- Issue: Reconciling rational worldview with supernatural experiences
- Personal Sets: Precinct, her apartment, her father's grave
- Edges: Deduction, Ghost sight, Stubborn
- Screen Presence: 1, 2, 3, 2, 1 (spotlight episode 3)

**Screen Presence**: Each episode, characters have different Screen Presence (1-3):
- **3**: Featured episode, protagonist's Issue central to plot
- **2**: Important supporting role
- **1**: Background role

This rotates throughout season ensuring everyone gets spotlight while creating ensemble dynamics.

### The Producer: GM Role

The Producer (GM equivalent):
- Creates adversity and complications
- Plays supporting cast and antagonists
- Frames scenes and provides pacing
- Doesn't control plot—responds to player choices
- Has own Fan Mail budget and can spend it like players

Crucially, Producer serves series rather than opposing players.

### Scene Structure: Episodic Play

Each episode consists of scenes featuring characters pursuing goals. Scenes can be:

**Conflict Scenes**: Character actively pursuing something with opposition
**Non-Conflict Scenes**: Character development, relationships, downtime

Only conflicts use card mechanics.

### Conflict Resolution: Stakes and Cards

When conflict arises:

1. **Set Stakes**: Player narrates what they want from conflict ("I want to catch the ghost," "I want my partner to trust me," "I want to escape the building")

2. **Opposition Sets Counter-Stakes**: Producer or opposing player sets contrary outcome ("The ghost escapes," "Your partner doubts you," "You're trapped")

3. **Draw Cards**: 
   - Base: 1 card
   - +1 card if using relevant Edge
   - +1 card if conflict involves Issue
   - +1 card per Screen Presence
   - +cards for spent Fan Mail

4. **High Card Wins**: Player/Producer with highest card wins and **narrates the outcome**

5. **Winner Narrates**: Crucially, winning means you narrate—not that you necessarily succeed

**Example**:
- Stakes: "I catch the ghost"
- Counter: "The ghost escapes but leaves clue"
- Draw: Player has 4 cards (Screen Presence 3, Issue relevant, Edge applicable), Producer has 2 cards
- Result: Player draws highest, wins, **chooses to narrate**: "I almost catch the ghost but it slips through my fingers—however, I notice it's wearing my father's ring. This is personal now."

The winner-narrates mechanic allows dramatically appropriate outcomes regardless of who wins mechanically.

### Fan Mail: Audience Currency

Players earn Fan Mail (poker chips/tokens) when:
- Playing entertaining/compelling scenes
- Highlighting other characters effectively
- Contributing to series enjoyment
- Narrating compelling conflict outcomes

**Spending Fan Mail**:
- +1 card per Fan Mail spent in conflicts
- Buying scene editing authority
- Introducing elements supporting series

This mechanically rewards entertaining play and gives players audience appreciation currency.

### Budget: Producer Resources

Producer has Fan Mail budget representing production resources. Spending budget makes adversity more challenging. Budget regenerates between episodes.

This creates Producer resource management parallel to player Fan Mail.

### Issue Development: Character Arcs

Each character's Issue evolves throughout season:
- Early episodes introduce Issue
- Middle episodes complicate Issue
- Final episodes resolve/transform Issue

Between seasons, Issues change reflecting character development.

### Season Structure: Multi-Episode Arcs

Typical season:
- **Episode 1**: Introduce characters and premise
- **Episodes 2-4**: Develop characters and complications (rotating spotlight)
- **Episode 5**: Season finale resolving major threads

Screen Presence ensures everyone gets spotlight focus while maintaining ensemble feel.

## Setting and Themes

### Genre Flexibility: Any TV Show

The system supports any television genre:
- **Cop Drama**: Law & Order, True Detective
- **Medical**: ER, House, Grey's Anatomy
- **Sci-Fi**: Star Trek, Battlestar Galactica
- **Supernatural**: Buffy, X-Files, Supernatural
- **Western**: Deadwood, Firefly
- **Comedy**: Scrubs, Community
- **Period**: Mad Men, Downton Abbey

The mechanics work regardless of genre because they model television structure, not specific content.

### The Series Premise: Defining Concept

Every series needs clear premise answering "why would viewers watch this?" The premise focuses play and provides coherence.

**Example Premises**:
- "Supernatural cops investigate paranormal crimes threatening mundane world"
- "Thieves with hearts of gold steal from the corrupt"
- "Doctors provide emergency medicine in war-torn countries"
- "High school students discover they have superpowers"

### Issue-Driven Drama: Personal Stakes

Characters have Issues providing personal stakes beyond plot objectives. Issues create dramatic tension regardless of genre:

- Detective struggling with PTSD from shooting
- Doctor hiding terminal illness from colleagues
- Thief confronting family legacy of crime
- Superhero teen balancing powers with normal life

Issues ensure character development alongside plot advancement.

### Ensemble Dynamics: Connections Matter

Characters must connect to create ensemble cast rather than isolated protagonists. Connections might be:
- Professional relationships (partners, colleagues)
- Personal relationships (friends, lovers, rivals)
- Shared history (survivors, classmates, veterans)

These connections create scenes focusing on character interaction.

### Television Tropes: Genre Conventions

The game encourages using television conventions:
- Cliffhanger endings
- Bottle episodes (limited locations)
- Flashback episodes
- Crossovers (if running multiple series)
- Special episodes (holiday specials, musicals)

## Cultural Impact

**Television Structure in RPGs**: Validated organizing campaigns as TV seasons with episodic structure

**Screen Presence Mechanics**: Popularized rotating spotlight focus ensuring ensemble play

**Fan Mail Currency**: Demonstrated how to mechanically reward entertaining play

**Collaborative Series Creation**: Established template for group world-building before play

**Winner-Narrates Outcomes**: Influenced conflict resolution allowing dramatic flexibility

**Stakes-Setting**: Showed how explicitly negotiating conflict outcomes before resolution enhances drama

**Producer Role**: Redefined GM as facilitator rather than antagonist or plot-provider

**Issue-Based Development**: Character Issues as mechanical and thematic drivers influenced character creation

**Diana Jones Award 2005**: Shared award validated innovative design

**Designer Influence**: Inspired numerous games using television structures and spotlight mechanics

**Narrative Authority Distribution**: Contributed to indie gaming's exploration of shared authority

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Brilliant television structure modeling
- Screen Presence rotation ensuring spotlight equity
- Fan Mail elegantly incentivizing entertainment
- Winner-narrates creating dramatically appropriate outcomes
- Collaborative creation fostering investment
- Works across all genres equally well
- Accessible rules complexity

**Criticisms**:
- **Cognitive Disconnect**: "Roleplaying TV shows" feels meta/artificial to some
- **Winner-Narrates Confusion**: Players struggle with narrating outcomes counter to character goals
- **Limited Tactical Depth**: No mechanical combat or skill systems for those who enjoy them
- **Requires Buy-In**: All players must commit to television structure
- **Issue Definition**: Some struggle defining compelling Issues
- **Scene Framing**: Determining scene boundaries can be unclear

**Community Response**:
- Devoted following among narrative gamers
- Regular convention play demonstrating concept
- Designer community reverence
- Limited mainstream adoption
- Influenced more games than directly played
