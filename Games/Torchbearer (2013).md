# Torchbearer

**Publisher**: [[Burning Wheel Headquarters]]
**Designer**: [[Luke Crane]], Thor Olavsrud
**Year**: 2013
**System**: Burning Wheel-derived
**Genre**: Dark Fantasy Dungeon Crawling
**Status**: in-print

## Historical Context

Torchbearer, co-designed by Luke Crane and Thor Olavsrud and published in 2013, represents a fascinating design experiment: applying Burning Wheel's sophisticated belief-driven mechanics to old-school dungeon crawling, creating a game where resource management, survival horror, and the grinding misery of adventuring become the point rather than obstacles to heroic fantasy.

By 2013, the OSR (Old School Renaissance) had revitalized interest in early D&D's lethality, exploration focus, and resource management. Games like Lamentations of the Flame Princess and Dungeon Crawl Classics proved audiences wanted difficult, consequential dungeon crawling. Simultaneously, The Burning Wheel had established sophisticated mechanics for character-driven drama. Torchbearer asked: what if we combined these?

The premise was subversive. Rather than heroic adventurers conquering dungeons, Torchbearer portrayed desperate, hungry, scared delvers barely surviving hostile environments while pursuing greed and desperation-driven goals. The game made explicit what D&D implied—dungeons are terrible places, adventuring is miserable, most delvers die poor, and treasure-seeking is driven by desperation or avarice, not heroism.

Crane and Olavsrud drew inspiration from Norse mythology's grim fatalism, early D&D's lethality, and video games like Dark Souls that made suffering the experience. They simplified Burning Wheel's complexity while maintaining its philosophical core—characters had beliefs and goals driving them into dungeons, mechanical systems tracked grinding deterioration, and advancement came through surviving enough to learn from experience.

The Grind became Torchbearer's signature mechanic. Every action in dungeons increased the Grind—a relentless counter tracking exhaustion, hunger, light depletion, and despair. As the Grind advanced, conditions worsened—torches guttered, rations depleted, tempers frayed, wounds festered. This mechanized the resource management that traditional D&D handled through bookkeeping. The Grind made the dungeon itself antagonistic, constantly threatening regardless of monster presence.

Character creation emphasized fragility and desperation. Rather than heroic backgrounds, characters were desperate migrants, failed merchants, disgraced nobles, or criminals fleeing consequences. They delved dungeons because they had no better options. This anti-heroic framing distinguished Torchbearer from power-fantasy D&D.

The conflict resolution used simplified Burning Wheel mechanics—roll dice pools, count successes, complications arise from margins. But Torchbearer added specific dungeon-focused mechanics: kill conflict (life-or-death combat), drive-off conflict (escaping without killing), flee conflict (running away), and the dreaded twist/condition system where failures introduced complications or damaged character conditions.

Torchbearer's Kickstarter in 2013 funded successfully, demonstrating appetite for sophisticated OSR games. The campaign raised over $180,000 from 2,088 backers, proving Crane's design reputation and the concept's appeal. Backers wanted difficult games that made suffering meaningful and resource management tense.

Upon release, Torchbearer divided audiences. Some loved the grinding difficulty, meaningful resource decisions, and anti-heroic tone. Others found it punishingly difficult, the Grind too relentless, and the bleakness oppressive. This division was intentional—Torchbearer knew its audience and designed specifically for players seeking difficult, consequential dungeon crawling.

The game influenced the OSR by demonstrating that sophisticated narrative mechanics could serve old-school play. Beliefs, goals, and instincts drove characters into dungeons, creating story through desperate choices rather than predetermined plots. The systematic Grind showed resource management could be elegant rather than bookkeeping-heavy.

Second edition (2020) refined mechanics, clarified procedures, and incorporated years of feedback while maintaining the grinding core. This demonstrated sustained community and Crane's commitment to supporting the game long-term.

Torchbearer proved old-school sensibilities could combine with modern design, that suffering could be mechanically elegant, and that dungeon crawling could be both miserable and deeply engaging.

## Mechanical Innovations

Torchbearer innovated by applying Burning Wheel philosophy to dungeon crawling grind.

**The Grind**: Central mechanic tracking dungeon delving's toll. Every dungeon action (exploring, searching, fighting) advanced the Grind track. At specific intervals, Grind turns triggered—torches died, rations depleted, conditions worsened. This mechanized resource depletion elegantly while creating mounting tension. The Grind made time itself antagonistic.

**Conditions**: Rather than hit points, characters suffered conditions—Hungry, Thirsty, Exhausted, Angry, Afraid, Injured, Sick. Each condition imposed mechanical penalties. Accumulate too many and you were Dead or effectively removed from play. This created deterioration beyond simple health tracking.

**Town Phase**: After dungeon delving, characters returned to town for downtime. Town phase had its own mechanics—recovering, spending treasure, pursuing personal agendas. This created rhythm between desperate dungeon survival and town recuperation/development.

**Beliefs, Goals, Instincts**: Simplified from Burning Wheel but maintaining core concept. Beliefs drove why characters risked dungeons. Goals were session-specific objectives. Instincts were automatic reactions. These created character motivation mechanics.

**Persona and Fate**: Artha-style metacurrency rewarded pursuing beliefs and goals. Spending Persona/Fate provided rerolls, bonuses, or special actions. This maintained BW's reward-for-thematic-play while simplifying economy.

**Conflicts**: Formalized conflict resolution for combat (Kill), escaping (Drive Off), and fleeing (Flee). Each conflict type had specific procedures, creating tactical depth while streamlining resolution compared to Burning Wheel's Fight!

**Supply**: Abstract equipment tracking via Supply checks. Rather than listing every item, characters had supply rating. Need something? Test Supply. Failure depleted Supply. This elegantly handled equipment without inventory management tedium.

**Light and Darkness**: Torches provided light for limited turns. Darkness imposed severe penalties. Managing light sources became crucial survival consideration mechanized through Grind.

**Tests and Advancement**: Like Burning Wheel, skills improved through use. Tests that mattered (against meaningful obstacles) counted toward advancement. This created organic skill development.

**Seasons**: Campaign structure used seasons—each delve was episode in larger seasonal arc. Between seasons, characters advanced and pursued personal agendas. This created campaign framework.

**Circles for NPCs**: Simplified Burning Wheel's Circles for finding helpful NPCs in town. Success meant you knew someone useful; margin determined helpfulness.

**Resources for Wealth**: Abstract wealth through Resources attribute. Buying things required Resources tests. This eliminated coin-counting while maintaining economic stakes.

**Traits and Wises**: Personality traits provided mechanical benefits and complications. Wises (specialized knowledge) enabled clever problem-solving—"Dungeon-wise" might reveal trap patterns.

**Recovery Mechanics**: Specific mechanics for recovering from conditions during camp phase (in dungeon) and town phase. Recovery was difficult, requiring resources and time.

**Twists and Conditions**: Failed tests imposed twists (complications) or conditions (status penalties). This made failure dramatically interesting while mechanically consequential.

**Camp Phase**: Structured downtime in dungeons for recovering, planning, and arguing. Camp actions were limited and costly, creating strategic decisions about resting versus pressing forward.

**Level Drain**: Terrible magic could drain levels, devastating characters. This callback to classic D&D made certain threats existentially terrifying.

## Cultural Impact

Torchbearer's cultural impact operated at intersection of OSR and sophisticated indie design.

**OSR Narrative Synthesis**: Demonstrated that OSR sensibilities (lethality, resource management, dungeon focus) could coexist with sophisticated narrative mechanics (beliefs, goals, systematic resolution). This influenced subsequent games seeking middle ground.

**Grind Mechanic Influence**: The Grind as elegant resource depletion timer influenced various games' time-pressure mechanics. Making time and resources dramatically tense through single elegant counter proved influential.

**Suffering as Experience**: Torchbearer validated suffering as core game experience. Games could be about grinding difficulty and desperate survival, not just power fantasy. This influenced tone and design goals across indie space.

**Condition Systems**: Using conditions instead of hit points appeared increasingly in various games. Torchbearer demonstrated how narrative conditions could create mechanical deterioration.

**Town Phase Structure**: Separating dungeon and town phases with distinct mechanical procedures influenced how games handled downtime and recovery.

**Anti-Heroic Fantasy**: The game's embrace of desperate, unheroic delvers legitimized anti-heroic fantasy gaming beyond grimdark cynicism toward authentic portrayal of difficult lives.

**Burning Wheel Accessibility**: Torchbearer made Burning Wheel philosophy accessible to OSR players uncomfortable with BW's complexity. This introduced new audiences to Crane's design philosophy.

**Resource Management Elegance**: Demonstrated resource management could be elegant systematic mechanics rather than tedious bookkeeping. Supply and Resources attributes showed abstract tracking working well.

**Kickstarter Validation**: The campaign's success validated sophisticated indie games commanding premium prices through crowdfunding.

## Design Philosophy

Torchbearer's philosophy celebrated grinding difficulty and desperate choices.

**Dungeons Are Terrible**: The game assumed dungeons were hostile, miserable places where delvers suffered constantly. This wasn't bug but feature—the experience was about surviving terrible conditions while pursuing desperate goals.

**Grind Is The Point**: Rather than streamlining resource management away, Torchbearer made it central through elegant Grind mechanic. Tracking deterioration created tension and meaningful decisions.

**Anti-Heroic**: Characters were desperate people with few options, not heroes seeking glory. This grounded fantasy in uncomfortable realism where treasure-seeking was driven by need or greed.

**Beliefs Drive Desperation**: Even in grinding dungeon crawl, character beliefs mattered. Why risk death in terrible places? Your beliefs and goals provided answer and mechanical benefits.

**Fail Interesting**: Failed tests created complications or conditions, driving story through adversity. Failure was expected, meaningful, and dramatically engaging.

**Resource Decisions Matter**: Every choice—press forward or rest, use supplies or conserve, fight or flee—had meaningful consequences. No decision was trivial.

**Town Provides Relief**: The town phase created rhythm—desperate dungeon survival alternated with town recovery and development. This pacing prevented relentless bleakness from becoming overwhelming.

**Advancement Through Survival**: Characters improved by surviving enough to learn. Advancement reflected experience earned through suffering.

**Systematic Elegance**: Complex situations (resource depletion, equipment, wealth) received elegant abstract mechanics rather than detailed simulation. This maintained sophistication without overwhelming bookkeeping.

**Norse Fatalism**: Inspired by Norse mythology's grim worldview, the game accepted that most delvers would fail, suffer, and die poor. The few who succeeded earned their treasure through terrible experiences.

## Setting and Themes

Torchbearer was setting-agnostic but assumed dark fantasy with specific tonal elements.

**Desperate Medieval Fantasy**: The implied setting was grim medieval-ish fantasy where life was hard, death common, and delving dungeons was desperate occupation for desperate people.

**Dungeon Focus**: Unlike sandbox or political campaigns, Torchbearer focused on dungeon delving—exploring dangerous underground complexes, fighting monsters, looting treasure, and barely surviving.

**Dwarven Ruins**: Norse-influenced dwarven ruins, troll-haunted caves, forgotten crypts, and similar dark fantasy dungeons populated the game's examples.

**Town as Respite**: Towns provided safety, recovery, and civilization between delves. But towns had their own problems—expenses, obligations, social conflicts.

**Themes**:
- **Survival**: Staying alive in hostile environments
- **Desperation**: Risking death because alternatives are worse
- **Greed**: Treasure's pull versus danger's cost
- **Deterioration**: Constant grinding wear on body and spirit
- **Camaraderie**: Fellowship forged through shared suffering
- **Futility**: Most delvers fail; success is rare and costly
- **Resource Management**: Every supply, every torch, every ration matters
- **The Grind**: Relentless pressure of hostile environment

**Norse Influence**: Grim fatalism, emphasis on endurance, acceptance of death, and celebration of those who survive against odds.

**Anti-Tolkien**: Rather than epic quests for world-saving, characters pursued personal survival and enrichment in uncaring world.

## Reception and Legacy

Torchbearer achieved strong niche success while influencing OSR and indie design.

**Critical Acclaim**:
- ENnie Award Silver for Best Rules (2014)
- Golden Geek Best RPG nominee (2013)
- Extensive positive reviews in indie gaming media
- OSR community examined it seriously
- Recognized for sophisticated OSR design

**Commercial Success**:
- $180,000+ Kickstarter
- Strong retail sales
- Second edition (2020) demonstrated sustained community
- Consistent convention presence
- International following

**Community Response**:
- Passionate dedicated player base
- Active forums and support communities
- Strong actual-play presence
- Teaching resources developed by community
- Regular convention games and organized play

**Design Influence**:
- Grind-style time pressure mechanics appeared elsewhere
- Condition systems over HP gained traction
- Town phase structure influenced downtime mechanics
- Supply abstraction copied in various games
- OSR games increasingly adopted sophisticated narrative mechanics

**Second Edition**: 2020's second edition refined mechanics, clarified procedures, and incorporated years of feedback, demonstrating sustained support and community.

**Criticisms**:
- Grinding difficulty not for everyone
- Relentless bleakness could be oppressive
- Required specific player mindset and buy-in
- Learning curve steep for OSR players unfamiliar with BW philosophy
- Punishing mechanics frustrated some groups
- Anti-heroic tone clashed with heroic fantasy expectations

**Long-term Viability**: Maintained active community and in-print status, proving niche for difficult dungeon crawlers.

**Actual Play**: Featured in various actual-play shows demonstrating grinding gameplay and desperate survival to broader audiences.

**Educational Impact**: Game design discussions examined Torchbearer for:
- Integrating narrative mechanics into traditional play
- Elegant resource management systems
- Making suffering engaging gameplay
- Condition-based damage systems
- Structured phase-based play

**Legacy**: Torchbearer proved OSR and sophisticated narrative design could synthesize productively, that grinding difficulty could be elegant and engaging, and that anti-heroic fantasy could attract dedicated audiences. It demonstrated Luke Crane could simplify Burning Wheel for specific purposes while maintaining philosophical core.
