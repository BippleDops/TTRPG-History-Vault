# Sorcerer

**Publisher**: [[Adept Press]]
**Designer**: [[Ron Edwards]]
**Year**: 2001
**System**: Sorcerer System
**Genre**: horror, dark-fantasy, psychological-drama
**Status**: in-print

## Overview

Sorcerer, designed by Ron Edwards and first published in 2001, stands as the foundational text of narrativist RPG design and the indie games movement—a game about summoning and binding demons where mechanical power comes at cost of losing your humanity, creating agon

izing choices between effectiveness and soul. Players portray sorcerers who have crossed moral/mystical boundaries to gain demonic power, pursuing desperate goals (Kickers) while managing relationships with bound demons and preventing their Humanity stat from reaching zero (at which point they become NPCs, lost to inhumanity).

The game's revolutionary contribution wasn't primarily its premise but its design philosophy. Edwards wrote Sorcerer explicitly to test his GNS Theory (Gamist/Narrativist/Simulationist creative agenda taxonomy), creating game mechanics focused entirely on generating dramatic story rather than simulating reality or providing tactical challenge. Every mechanical element serves narrative purpose: Kickers (immediate crises) ensure story starts with momentum; Humanity tracking creates moral tension; demon relationship mechanics generate interpersonal drama; the dice system resolves conflicts based on fictional positioning rather than numerical optimization.

The publishing model proved equally revolutionary. Edwards self-published through Adept Press, selling primarily through PDF and print-on-demand rather than traditional distribution channels. This demonstrated indie designers could reach audiences directly, retaining creative control and larger profit margins. Sorcerer became template for indie publishing, proving viable path existed outside the industry's established gatekeepers.

The game's influence extended far beyond its direct play. Edwards founded The Forge (online community for indie designers) where Sorcerer served as proof-of-concept for narrativist theory. Designers studied Sorcerer's mechanics, adopted its innovations (Kickers became standard indie game feature), and used it as reference point for discussing story-focused design. The game's supplements—Sorcerer & Sword, Sex & Sorcery, The Sorcerer's Soul—expanded mechanical and thematic frameworks, demonstrating how core system could support diverse tones and settings.

However, Sorcerer's presentation created barriers. The 2001 edition's layout, while functional, lacked polish of mainstream RPG products. The writing assumed reader familiarity with narrativist theory, making the game somewhat opaque to traditional gamers. The mechanical simplicity (relative to D&D/White Wolf complexity) sometimes felt incomplete to players expecting comprehensive rules coverage. These factors meant Sorcerer's influence primarily touched designers and theory-focused gamers rather than achieving mass-market penetration.

Yet this niche influence proved profound. Sorcerer demonstrated narrativist design could work mechanically, philosophically, and commercially. It validated The Forge's theoretical discussions with practical application. It showed indie designers could succeed outside traditional publishing. And it established mechanical innovations (Kickers, Humanity, Currency dice, relationship-based conflicts) that became standard tools in narrative game design toolbox.

## Historical Context

Sorcerer emerged from Ron Edwards's dissatisfaction with 1990s mainstream RPG design, particularly AD&D 2E and White Wolf games. Edwards, trained in evolutionary biology and experienced game master, observed that most RPGs claimed to support storytelling but mechanically incentivized tactical optimization and power accumulation. He wanted game where mechanics actually generated dramatic narrative rather than merely accommodating it as happy accident.

This frustration led to GNS Theory development. Drawing on earlier game theory work (particularly Brian Gleichman's writings and Threefold Model), Edwards proposed that RPG play could be categorized by dominant creative agenda:
- **Gamism**: Challenge and competition
- **Narrativism**: Dramatic storytelling and thematic exploration
- **Simulationism**: Exploration and consistency

Edwards argued most RPGs tried serving multiple agendas simultaneously, creating incoherent designs where mechanics contradicted stated goals. He wanted to design explicitly narrativist game proving focused creative agenda could produce superior gameplay for players sharing that agenda.

Sorcerer development began in mid-1990s, influenced by:
- **Nephilim**: Provided some mechanical inspiration
- **Over the Edge**: Demonstrated rules-light design viability
- **Amber Diceless RPG**: Showed diceless/minimal-dice systems could work
- **Literary influences**: Moorcock's Elric, weird fiction, psychological horror

Edwards playtested extensively with his gaming group, refining mechanics to support narrative priorities. The key innovations emerged iteratively:
- **Kickers**: Ensuring player-driven story momentum
- **Humanity**: Creating moral stakes through mechanical tracking
- **Currency dice**: Allowing tactical positioning while maintaining narrative focus
- **Demon relationships**: Making antagonistic allies central rather than peripheral

The initial publication (1998) was extremely limited print run through Sorcerer Games. The 2001 Adept Press edition represented first widely available version, incorporating playtesting refinements and clearer theoretical grounding.

Publication timing coincided with indie RPG movement's emergence. The Forge (founded 2001) provided community infrastructure supporting indie designers. PDF distribution and print-on-demand technology enabled economically viable small-run publishing. Sorcerer rode this wave while simultaneously helping create it—Edwards's success encouraged others to pursue indie publication.

Critical reception split along predictable lines. Narrativist-sympathetic reviewers praised Sorcerer as breakthrough demonstrating theory in practice. Traditional gamers sometimes found it incomplete or confusing, expecting different design priorities. The Forge community lionized it as proof that focused design could achieve creative agenda coherence.

The supplements (2002-2003) expanded Sorcerer's reach. *Sorcerer & Sword* demonstrated how core mechanics could support sword-and-sorcery. *Sex & Sorcery* explored mature thematic content and relationship mechanics. *The Sorcerer's Soul* provided advanced GM techniques and deeper theoretical discussion. These supplements showed Sorcerer wasn't single narrow vision but flexible framework supporting diverse applications.

By 2004, Sorcerer's direct influence had peaked, but its impact continued through derivatives. Designers took Sorcerer's innovations and ran with them: Vincent Baker's *Dogs in the Vineyard* evolved from Sorcerer mechanics; Paul Czege's *My Life with Master* adapted Humanity-style tracking; Luke Crane's *Burning Wheel* incorporated belief-driven play. The game's theoretical foundation influenced entire generation of indie designers even as fewer people played Sorcerer itself.

The game remains in print through Adept Press, regularly revised and updated. While never achieving mainstream commercial success, Sorcerer occupies crucial position in RPG history as bridge between traditional and narrative design, proof-of-concept for indie publishing, and foundation of narrativist game theory application.

## Core Mechanics

Sorcerer uses elegant dice pool system serving narrative priorities:

### Core Resolution: Currency Dice

Roll d6 pools, with pool size determined by stats/abilities. Compare your highest die to opponent's highest die:
- Higher wins
- Ties go to initiator or defender (context-dependent)
- Margin of success (difference between high dice) determines impact magnitude

**Currency Dice Innovation**: Leftover dice from successful rolls carry forward as bonus dice in follow-up actions, creating tactical momentum. Successful actions build toward bigger successes; failures reset momentum.

**Example**: Binding demon requires opposed Will roll. You roll 6 dice (Will 5 + ritual preparation +1), demon rolls 4 dice (Power 4). You get 6,5,4,2,2,1; demon gets 5,3,2,2. Your 6 beats demon's 5. The margin (1) determines binding strength. Your five unused dice (5,4,2,2,1) become bonus dice for next action—maybe commanding newly-bound demon.

This creates natural narrative flow where successes build momentum and failures break it, encouraging tactical thinking within narrative frame.

### Stats and Scores

Characters have three stats rated 1-5:
- **Stamina**: Physical endurance and health
- **Will**: Mental/emotional strength
- **Lore**: Occult knowledge and summoning skill

Demons have:
- **Power**: Raw supernatural strength
- **Lore**: Demonic knowledge
- **Need**: What demon requires (feeding, worship, destruction)
- **Desire**: What demon wants (freedom, corruption, revenge)

Simple stat arrays focus attention on narrative elements rather than optimization.

### Humanity: The Central Mechanic

Humanity (rated 1-10) represents character's connection to human values, empathy, and moral grounding. As you use demons and commit transgressions, Humanity decreases. At Humanity 0, you become NPC—lost to inhumanity.

**Humanity Checks**: When performing potentially dehumanizing acts (binding demons, using demonic power to harm, witnessing atrocities), roll Humanity versus appropriate opposing dice pool. Failure reduces Humanity permanently.

**Humanity Gain**: Acting in accordance with human values (compassion, sacrifice, refusing demonic power when convenient) can restore Humanity through successful checks.

**Humanity Definition**: Each group defines what Humanity means in their game:
- Contemporary horror: Human empathy and compassion
- Sword & sorcery: Civilization versus barbarism
- Dark fantasy: Soul integrity versus corruption

This flexibility lets groups calibrate moral stakes to preferred themes.

**The Tension**: Demons provide power needed to pursue your Kicker, but using demons risks Humanity loss. The game creates agonizing choice: pursue goals effectively (risking soul) or preserve humanity (potentially failing objectives)?

### Kickers: Story Ignition

Each character begins with Kicker—immediate crisis demanding action:
- "My wife just left me for another man"
- "I discovered my daughter was kidnapped"
- "The police just arrested me for murder I didn't commit"
- "My demon revealed it knows my darkest secret"

Kickers must be:
- **Immediate**: Requiring urgent action
- **Player-driven**: What the player wants to explore
- **Dramatic**: Creating story momentum
- **Resolvable**: Having potential conclusion

The GM's role is facilitating Kicker resolution while creating complications. When Kicker resolves, the story arc completes—campaigns typically last 4-8 sessions until all Kickers resolve.

### Demons: Antagonistic Allies

Demons are bound supernatural entities with their own:
- **Type**: Possessor (inhabits host), Parasite (attached to body), Object (inhabits item), Passer (travels dimensions), Inconspicuous (appears normal)
- **Telltale**: Visible sign revealing demonic nature
- **Abilities**: Powers drawn from comprehensive list
- **Need**: Required sustenance (usually problematic)
- **Desire**: Goal demon pursues (usually contrary to master's interests)

**Binding and Relationship**: Sorcerers must bind demons through contested Will rolls. Binding strength determines control:
- Strong binding: Demon obeys reluctantly
- Weak binding: Demon negotiates, subverts, rebels
- No binding: Demon acts independently (usually badly for sorcerer)

Demons are never simple tools. They have agendas. They resent servitude. They tempt masters toward Humanity loss. Managing demon relationships creates ongoing dramatic tension.

**Demon Rebellion**: If sorcerer's Will drops below demon's Power, demon may rebel—refusing commands, bargaining for freedom, or attacking master.

### Conflict Resolution

All conflicts use same basic system but scale in scope:
- **Simple contests**: Single roll determines outcome
- **Extended contests**: Multiple exchanges with currency dice building
- **Combat**: Detailed exchange system with tactical positioning

The system intentionally blurs combat/social/supernatural conflicts—all use same mechanical framework, making social manipulation as mechanically engaging as swordfights.

### Banishing and Summoning

**Summoning**: Contested Lore roll, with preparation and rituals adding bonus dice. Success summons demon; immediate binding contest follows.

**Banishing**: Opposed Lore roll against demon's Power. Success dismisses demon from reality (temporarily or permanently based on margin).

These mechanics make occult acts significant undertakings rather than casual spellcasting.

### Advancement

Characters improve through play, but advancement focuses on Humanity and relationships rather than power accumulation:
- Humanity can increase/decrease based on choices
- Stats improve slowly through significant events
- Demon relationships evolve
- Kicker resolution brings character arc to conclusion

This creates character development through dramatic experience rather than numerical progression.

## Setting and Themes

Sorcerer doesn't assume default setting, instead providing frameworks applicable to diverse contexts:

### Contemporary Horror (Default Assumption)

Modern world where sorcery exists secretly. Demons are supernatural entities accessed through forbidden knowledge. Sorcerers pursue power for desperate reasons, binding entities they barely understand.

**Themes**: Faustian bargains, corruption through power, moral compromise, isolation

### Sorcerer & Sword: Barbaric Fantasy

Sword-and-sorcery setting where demons are powerful entities bargained with by desperate heroes. Humanity represents civilization versus barbarism.

**Themes**: Civilization's price, barbarism's freedom, power's corruption, heroic tragedy

### Other Variations

The system supports:
- Cyberpunk (demons as AI/programs)
- Superhero (demons as power sources)
- Historical (demons as supernatural entities throughout history)
- Space opera (demons as alien entities/technology)

### Core Thematic Questions

Regardless of setting, Sorcerer explores:
- **What would you sacrifice for power?**
- **How far will you go to achieve your goals?**
- **What defines humanity in the face of inhuman power?**
- **Can you control what you've unleashed?**

### The Sorcerous Compact

Characters have crossed lines ordinary people won't. They've learned forbidden knowledge, bound supernatural entities, and accepted moral compromises. The game asks: was it worth it? Can you preserve any humanity while wielding inhuman power?

### Demons as Metaphor

Demons represent:
- Addiction (Need/Desire as compulsion)
- Toxic relationships (reluctant cooperation with entities that harm you)
- Power's corruption (effective tools with catastrophic costs)
- Externalized darkness (your bound demons reflect your choices)

This metaphorical richness allows diverse interpretations serving different thematic goals.

## Cultural Impact

**Narrativist Design Proof-of-Concept**: Sorcerer demonstrated GNS Theory's narrativist agenda could produce mechanically functional, thematically coherent games.

**The Forge Foundation**: Provided practical example validating The Forge's theoretical discussions, encouraging indie designers to pursue narrative-focused projects.

**Indie Publishing Model**: Demonstrated PDF sales and print-on-demand viability, establishing template for indie game distribution.

**Kicker Innovation**: The Kicker mechanic became standard feature in narrative games, ensuring player-driven story momentum.

**Humanity Mechanics**: Moral degradation tracking influenced countless games exploring ethical choices and soul-loss.

**Currency Dice**: The tactical dice-carrying-forward mechanic influenced subsequent narrative systems seeking tactical depth.

**Relationship-Driven Conflict**: Demons-as-problematic-allies model influenced how designers approached companion/follower/resource mechanics.

**GNS Theory Application**: Served as primary case study for Edwards's theoretical work, influencing decades of design discourse.

**Supplement Model**: The thematically diverse supplements demonstrated core mechanics' flexibility across genres/tones.

**Designer Influence**: Directly inspired Baker (*Dogs in the Vineyard*), Czege (*My Life with Master*), and numerous other narrativist designers.

**Academic Attention**: Became subject of game studies scholarship examining narrativist design and indie gaming emergence.

**Community Building**: Adept Press forums and The Forge discussions built community around narrativist gaming philosophy.

## Reception and Criticism

**Praise**:
- Elegant mechanics perfectly serving narrative priorities
- Humanity system creating genuine moral tension
- Kickers ensuring player-driven story
- Demon relationships generating ongoing drama
- Flexible framework supporting diverse settings
- Theoretical coherence between design and execution
- Successful indie publishing model
- Influential mechanical innovations

**Criticisms**:
- **Presentation**: Early editions' layout and clarity issues
- **Theoretical Density**: Assumed familiarity with narrativist theory, creating barriers
- **Simplicity**: Some players wanted more mechanical depth
- **Setting Vagueness**: Lack of default setting confused players expecting defined world
- **Narrow Appeal**: Narrativist focus didn't suit gamist/simulationist preferences
- **Humanity Subjectivity**: Group-defined Humanity sometimes created inconsistency
- **Demon Management**: Some found demon relationship tracking burdensome

**Community Response**:
- Devoted following within narrativist gaming community
- Extensive theoretical discussion on The Forge
- Multiple derivative games and hacks
- Strong cult classic status
- Limited mainstream penetration

**Influence vs. Adoption**: Sorcerer influenced more games than it was actually played—many designers studied it without running campaigns.

## Legacy and Influence

**Narrativist Movement Foundation**: Established narrativist design as viable creative agenda with successful implementation.

**Indie Publishing Blueprint**: Provided model for direct-to-consumer indie RPG distribution.

**The Forge's Flagship**: Served as proof-of-concept for The Forge's design theories and community.

**Mechanical Innovations Spread**: Kickers, Humanity tracking, currency dice all became standard narrativist tools.

**Derivative Games**: Inspired *Dogs in the Vineyard*, *My Life with Master*, *Sorcerer's Crusade*, and others.

**Theoretical Foundation**: Remains primary reference for GNS Theory discussions.

**Designer Education**: Studied by aspiring designers learning narrativist principles.

**Supplement Framework**: Demonstrated how core systems support diverse applications through focused supplements.

**Continued Relevance**: Regular updates and active community maintain contemporary significance.

**Academic Subject**: Subject of scholarly analysis in game studies examining indie gaming emergence.
