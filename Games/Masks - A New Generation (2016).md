# Masks - A New Generation

**Publisher**: [[Magpie Games]]
**Designer**: [[Brendan Conway]]
**Year**: 2016
**System**: Powered by the Apocalypse (PbtA)
**Genre**: superhero, modern
**Status**: in-print

## Historical Context

Masks: A New Generation emerged as one of the most thematically focused and emotionally resonant Powered by the Apocalypse games, proving that superhero gaming could explore teenage identity, growth, and relationships as compellingly as punching supervillains.

By the mid-2010s, PbtA had proven itself across numerous genres—horror (Monsterhearts), urban fantasy (Urban Shadows), mystery (Monster of the Week), cyberpunk (The Sprawl). Superhero gaming, however, remained dominated by mechanically complex systems like Mutants & Masterminds and Champions focused on power simulation rather than character drama. These systems excelled at modeling what powers could do but struggled with the emotional and identity questions central to great superhero stories, particularly those about young heroes.

Brendan Conway recognized an opportunity. The best teenage superhero fiction—Young Justice, Teen Titans, Runaways, Ms. Marvel—focused on identity formation, adult expectations, peer relationships, and figuring out who you are while the world tries to tell you who you should be. These stories weren't primarily about power levels or combat tactics but about growing up under extraordinary circumstances. PbtA's focus on character drama and relationships seemed perfect for this territory.

Conway's design philosophy centered on a crucial insight: teenage superheroes are fundamentally about identity in flux. Adults have established identities; teens are still forming theirs, influenced by peers, adults, society, and their own experiences. This fluidity should be mechanical, not just narrative. Instead of fixed ability scores, characters should have shifting self-perceptions that change based on how others see and treat them.

This insight led to Masks' defining innovation: Labels. Rather than Strength or Intelligence, characters have Danger, Freak, Savior, Superior, and Mundane—how they see themselves in these aspects. When someone tells you you're dangerous, your Danger might increase. When an adult dismisses you as just a kid, your Mundane might rise while your Savior drops. Your abilities don't change, but your confidence in different aspects shifts, affecting how you approach situations.

Development occurred through extensive playtesting and iteration. Conway shared drafts on Google+ and Story Games, gathering feedback from the narrative gaming community. The playbook designs underwent particular scrutiny—each needed to represent a distinct teenage hero archetype while providing meaningful character growth opportunities.

The Kickstarter launched in May 2016, seeking $6,000. It raised over $126,000 from 1,700+ backers—tremendous success demonstrating hunger for emotionally sophisticated superhero gaming. Backers weren't just funding a game; they were supporting exploration of teenage identity through the superhero lens.

Masks released in late 2016 to widespread critical acclaim. Reviews praised how the game made superhero stories about character rather than combat, how Labels mechanized identity fluidity, and how playbooks captured specific teenage experiences. The game felt simultaneously like Teen Titans and like being an actual teenager—figuring out who you are while everyone has opinions about who you should be.

The game's impact proved substantial. It demonstrated PbtA could handle superhero gaming by focusing on what made superhero stories emotionally compelling rather than what made powers mechanically complex. It showed that innovative mechanics could emerge from thematic focus—Labels existed because the theme demanded them. It proved that seemingly narrow focus (teenage superheroes specifically, not superheroes generally) could create richer play than broad universality.

By the late 2010s, Masks had become the go-to system for emotionally driven superhero play. It spawned supplements expanding playbook options, settings, and play frameworks. It influenced how designers thought about identity mechanics and character growth. It demonstrated that the best genre emulation comes from identifying what makes the genre emotionally resonant, then mechanizing those elements.

Masks represents PbtA design at its most thematically focused and mechanically innovative. It proved that constraints enable creativity, that emotional truth matters more than mechanical simulation, and that teenage superhero stories contain depth worth exploring through carefully crafted game design.

## Mechanical Innovations

Masks introduced several innovations that redefined how RPGs could handle identity, growth, and superhero action.

**Labels Instead of Stats**: Rather than traditional attributes, characters have five Labels—Danger (threatening, strong, reckless), Freak (strange, alien, powerful), Savior (noble, defending, capable), Superior (skilled, competent, arrogant), and Mundane (normal, human, insignificant). Labels represent how you see yourself in these aspects, ranging from -2 to +3. You roll+Label when taking actions reflecting that self-perception.

**Shifting Labels**: Labels change constantly. When adults tell you who you are, they can shift your Labels—increasing one, decreasing another. When peers Comfort or Support you, you can shift Labels yourself. This mechanical fluidity represents teenage identity formation where external influences and internal growth constantly reshape self-perception.

**Influence**: Some NPCs have Influence over you—they can shift your Labels. Adults often start with Influence. You can give teammates Influence, or they can earn it. Having Influence means your opinion matters to that character emotionally. You can reject Influence, but it costs resources or creates complications. This mechanizes whose opinions affect you.

**Conditions**: Instead of hit points, you mark Conditions—Afraid, Angry, Guilty, Hopeless, Insecure. These emotional states provide mechanical penalties and trigger specific moves. Clear Conditions by acting in accordance with them or through character growth. This makes emotional state mechanical and consequential.

**Team Mechanics**: The game emphasizes team dynamics. You have relationships with each teammate that provide mechanical benefits and complications. Moves like Defend, Team (working together), and Comfort and Support only work with teammates. The game assumes you're a team figuring things out together.

**Moment of Truth**: Each playbook has a Moment of Truth—a once-per-game dramatic turning point where you transcend your limitations and do something impossible. These moments are narratively defined rather than mechanically constrained, creating signature superhero moments where you become who you're meant to be, at least temporarily.

**Adult Moves**: NPCs (particularly adult heroes and villains) don't roll dice. They have moves the GM makes—Tell them who they are, Give them an order, etc. This asymmetry reflects power dynamics between adults and teens. Adults have authority and experience; teens have potential and growth.

**The Bull, The Beacon, The Legacy, etc.**: Ten core playbooks each represent a teenage superhero archetype:
- **The Bull** - Super-strong powerhouse struggling with control
- **The Beacon** - Normal kid with no powers but lots of heart
- **The Legacy** - Carrying family/institutional expectations
- **The Delinquent** - Rebellious troublemaker with attitude
- **The Janus** - Balancing superhero and civilian lives
- **The Nova** - Barely controlled immense power
- **The Outsider** - From somewhere else, learning human ways
- **The Protege** - Mentored by and compared to adult hero
- **The Reformed** - Former villain trying to be good
- **The Transformed** - Body changed into something inhuman

Each playbook has unique moves, special relationships, and growth arcs reflecting their specific identity struggles.

**Playbook Advancement**: Characters advance by resolving their core issues. The Legacy might finally accept or reject their family's expectations. The Reformed might prove they've changed. Advancement leads to either embracing adult hero status, rejecting superheroics, or evolving into new playbook representing growth.

**Take a Powerful Blow**: When you're hit hard, you roll+Conditions marked. More emotional trauma means you're more likely to lash out or lose control. This creates downward spirals where emotional damage makes you more likely to create more damage—very teenage.

**Directly Engage a Threat**: Combat uses conversation-based resolution. Describe how you engage the threat, roll, and negotiate outcomes based on fictional positioning and results. This keeps action dynamic while preventing blow-by-blow tactical combat from overwhelming character drama.

**Pierce the Mask**: When you try to pierce someone's emotional defenses, you can ask questions about their emotions and motivations. This makes emotional insight mechanical, encouraging players to engage with character psychology.

**The GM's Principles**: Like all PbtA games, Masks provides explicit GM guidance—"Make the city feel alive," "Give NPCs simple motivations," "Treat adults and teens differently." These principles teach GMing the game's specific themes.

## Cultural Impact

Masks' cultural impact demonstrated how thematic focus could create commercially and critically successful niche games.

**Superhero Gaming Evolution**: Masks changed conversations about superhero RPGs. Instead of "how powerful is your character?" the questions became "who is your character?" and "who are they becoming?" This shifted superhero gaming toward character-driven stories.

**Identity Mechanics**: The Labels system influenced how designers thought about character attributes. What if stats represented self-perception rather than objective capability? What if they changed based on relationships and experiences? Several subsequent games explored similar territory.

**Teenage Stories Legitimized**: Before Masks, teenage protagonists often seemed less serious than adults. Masks demonstrated teenage stories—with their identity struggles, relationship drama, and growth—could be as compelling and sophisticated as adult narratives. This influenced broader RPG design.

**LGBTQ+ Representation**: Masks explicitly supported LGBTQ+ characters and themes. The Janus playbook's civilian/hero split resonated with closeted experiences. Identity fluidity paralleled gender identity exploration. The game became popular in queer gaming communities for enabling these stories.

**Emotional Gaming**: Masks normalized mechanics for emotions, relationships, and psychological states. Conditions, Influence, and Label-shifting made feelings mechanical and consequential. This contributed to broader industry acceptance of emotion-focused mechanics.

## Design Philosophy

Masks' design philosophy centered on theme-driven mechanics and emotional authenticity.

**Theme First**: Every mechanic serves the teenage superhero theme. Labels exist because teenage identity is fluid. Influence exists because teens care what others think. Conditions are emotional because teenage drama is emotional. The theme drives every design decision.

**Identity Is Fluid**: The game embraces that teenage identity is unformed and changing. Your Labels shift constantly. Your playbook can change. Who you are at campaign start differs from who you become. This fluidity is feature, not bug.

**Relationships Matter**: The game assumes relationships are central. Moves involve teammates. Influence mechanics track whose opinions matter. Team dynamics create mechanical benefits and complications. Isolation is mechanically punished; connection is rewarded.

**Adults Are Different**: NPCs, particularly adults, use different mechanics. They don't roll dice. They have Influence more easily. They tell you who you are. This asymmetry reflects that being a teenage hero means navigating adult expectations and authority.

**Powers Are Secondary**: Your specific powers matter less than how you use them and what they mean for your identity. Two characters with super-strength tell different stories based on their playbook and Labels. The game cares more about what powers mean than what they do.

**Emotional Truth Over Simulation**: Masks prioritizes emotional authenticity over mechanical simulation. Combat is resolved quickly through conversation because the game cares more about how fighting makes you feel than tactical positioning. This focus keeps spotlight on character drama.

**Growth Through Struggle**: Advancement comes from engaging with your character's core issues—confronting expectations, dealing with your past, figuring out who you are. You grow by facing your problems, not by accumulating XP through generic actions.

**Failure Is Growth**: Failed rolls don't mean incompetence—they mean complications, consequences, and opportunities for growth. Failure drives character development and story progression. The game embraces failure as necessary for satisfying arcs.

**Conversation-Based Play**: Like all PbtA, play is conversation. No initiative systems, no combat rounds. Describe actions, resolve with moves, respond to results, continue conversation. This keeps play fluid and character-focused.

**Genre Expectations**: The game assumes players want teenage superhero stories. It doesn't try to be universal or handle adult heroes, cosmic threats, or gritty street-level stories. This focus enables depth in its specific niche.

## Setting and Themes

Masks uses Halcyon City as default setting—a city where superheroic activity has existed for generations.

**Halcyon City**: A major metropolitan area with extensive superhero history. Multiple generations of heroes have protected it. Teen heroes operate in the shadow of legendary adults while forging their own identities. The city feels lived-in and history-rich.

**Generational Heroes**: The setting emphasizes superheroic generations:
- **The Golden Generation** - World War II-era heroes, mostly retired or dead
- **The Silver Generation** - Cold War heroes, now establishment figures
- **The Bronze Generation** - Modern adult heroes, currently active
- **You** - The new generation, still proving yourselves

This generational structure creates built-in mentors, expectations, and legacy issues.

**The Protectorate**: Adult superhero team serving as establishment heroes. They handle major threats but also represent the authority and expectations young heroes struggle against. They're not villains, but they're not always helpful either.

**Familiar But Open**: Halcyon City provides familiar superhero setting touchstones without extensive detail. There are villains, hero teams, civilian authorities, and complicated politics, but specifics emerge through play. The setting supports rather than constrains stories.

**Themes**:

**Identity Formation**: The central theme. Who are you? Who do others think you are? Who do you want to be? How do these different identities conflict and align? The entire game explores this through Labels, playbooks, and advancement.

**Adult Expectations**: Adults have opinions about who you should be—follow in family footsteps, join the Protectorate, stay out of trouble, use your powers responsibly. These expectations conflict with your own desires and your peers' influence.

**Peer Relationships**: Your team matters more than anything. These relationships provide support, complications, growth opportunities, and emotional grounding. You might disappoint adults, but disappointing your friends hurts worse.

**Power and Responsibility**: Classic superhero theme filtered through teenage lens. You have incredible abilities but limited life experience. You want to help but might make things worse. You have power but not always wisdom.

**Belonging and Isolation**: Finding your place—on the team, in the city, in the world. The Outsider doesn't understand human customs. The Transformed looks monstrous. The Beacon has no powers among super-powered peers. Everyone struggles with belonging.

**Growth and Change**: You're not static. You change through play—Labels shift, playbooks might change, you grow toward adulthood or other futures. The game is about becoming, not being.

**Heroism Despite Doubt**: You're heroes, but uncertain ones. You doubt yourselves, make mistakes, struggle with your powers and identities. Heroism means trying despite doubt, not fearless confidence.

**Family and Legacy**: Biological family, found family, institutional legacies, mentor relationships—these connections shape you while you determine how much they define you. The Legacy struggles with family expectations. The Protege deals with mentor comparisons.

**Masks We Wear**: The title's double meaning—superhero masks hiding civilian identities, and the masks everyone wears hiding their true selves. The game explores both literal and metaphorical masks.

**Greatest Achievement**:
Masks proved that understanding what makes a genre emotionally compelling—then mechanizing those elements—creates better genre emulation than simulating surface features. Teenage superhero stories aren't about power levels; they're about identity, belonging, and growth. By making those themes mechanical through Labels, Influence, and playbook advancement, Masks captured teenage superhero fiction's essence better than any system focused on powers and combat.

The game demonstrated that constraints enable creativity. By focusing exclusively on teenage heroes rather than superheroes generally, Conway created space to explore teenage experiences deeply. This narrow focus paradoxically made the game more universally resonant—everyone's been a teenager figuring themselves out, even if nobody's actually fought supervillains.

Masks represents PbtA design philosophy at its most refined—identify the genre's emotional core, create mechanics serving that core, trust the framework to generate appropriate stories. It proved that careful thematic focus combined with innovative mechanics could create experiences previously impossible in the hobby.

For players discovering Masks, it offers something rare: a game that takes teenage experiences seriously while embracing superheroic fun, that mechanizes identity exploration while supporting action-packed stories, that makes you feel like Teen Titans while also feeling like being an actual teenager. This combination of thematic authenticity and entertaining play ensures Masks' legacy as one of PbtA's greatest achievements.
