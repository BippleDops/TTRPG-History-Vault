# Blades in the Dark

**Publisher**: [[Evil Hat Productions]]
**Designer**: [[John Harper]]
**Year**: 2017
**System**: Forged in the Dark (FitD)
**Genre**: fantasy, crime
**Status**: in-print

## Historical Context

Blades in the Dark emerged from the intersection of indie narrative design and traditional gaming sensibilities, creating a system that would spawn its own design movement rivaling Powered by the Apocalypse's influence.

Designer John Harper brought substantial pedigree to the project. His previous works included Agon, Lady Blackbird, and contributions to various indie games. Harper understood both story game principles and traditional RPG structures, positioning him perfectly to synthesize both approaches into something novel.

The game originated from Harper's desire to run Doskvol-style heist fiction—Victorian-era criminals pulling elaborate scores in a haunted industrial city. Existing systems felt inadequate. D&D focused on dungeon-crawling heroics. PbtA games worked for many genres but lacked the specific procedures heist fiction required. Harper needed mechanics supporting planning-without-planning, flashbacks, crew development, and the escalating heat of criminal enterprise.

Development occurred largely publicly through Google+ and Harper's blog. He playtested extensively, sharing rules iterations and design insights. This transparency built community investment before the game's release. Backers weren't just funding a product; they'd watched it grow from concept to completion.

The Kickstarter launched May 2015, seeking $3,000. It raised over $330,000 from 4,200+ backers—one of indie RPG's most successful crowdfunding campaigns at the time. This demonstrated the appetite for sophisticated narrative systems with structured gameplay loops.

Publication came through Evil Hat Productions in 2017 after substantial development refinement. Evil Hat, known for supporting innovative designs through Fate and other systems, provided professional production values, distribution networks, and promotional reach that amplified the game's impact.

Upon release, Blades received extraordinary critical acclaim. Its innovations in action resolution, downtime structure, and crew-level play influenced designers across the industry. More significantly, the system's hackability spawned the Forged in the Dark movement—dozens of games adapting Blades' framework to different genres, much as PbtA games adapted Apocalypse World.

By 2020, FitD had become one of indie gaming's dominant frameworks. Games like Scum and Villainy (space opera), Band of Blades (military horror), and Beam Saber (mecha) demonstrated the system's versatility. Harper released the FitD SRD under Creative Commons, encouraging derivative works and ensuring the framework's continued evolution.

Blades represented indie gaming's maturation. It proved narrative-focused systems could support complex, procedural gameplay. It demonstrated that indie games could achieve production quality rivaling corporate publishers. It showed that careful mechanical design could enhance rather than restrict emergent storytelling. In these respects, Blades in the Dark marked a watershed moment in RPG design history.

## Mechanical Innovations

Blades in the Dark introduced numerous innovations that became FitD hallmarks and influenced broader game design.

**Position and Effect**: The game's core innovation. Before rolling dice, the GM establishes position (controlled, risky, desperate—how dangerous is the action?) and effect (limited, standard, great—how much will you accomplish?). This negotiation makes consequences and stakes explicit before dice hit the table. Players make informed decisions about risk versus reward, and everyone understands what success and failure mean for the specific situation.

**Flashback Mechanics**: Players can declare flashbacks to establish preparation they "already did." Want to have bribed the guard earlier? Spend stress and roll a flashback. This eliminates tedious planning sessions—you plan through play rather than before play. The system trusts players not to abuse it while providing narrative tools for competent character portrayal.

**Stress and Trauma**: Characters have stress tracks representing mental/physical strain. Players spend stress to resist consequences, activate special abilities, or push themselves. Fill your stress track and take trauma—permanent psychological damage like "paranoid," "haunted," or "ruthless." Accumulate four traumas and the character retires. This creates meaningful resource management with narrative consequences beyond hit points.

**Action Dice Pool**: Roll d6s equal to your action rating. Take the highest single die result: 1-3 is failure with consequences, 4-5 is partial success (you do it but face complications or reduced effect), 6 is full success. Multiple 6s (critical success) grant enhanced effect. The pool system scales elegantly from zero dice (roll two, take lowest) to elite skills.

**Devil's Bargains**: The GM can offer devil's bargains—add a die to your pool in exchange for a complication the GM will introduce. Players choose whether to accept, adding tactical decision-making and dramatic tension. The mechanic ensures every roll potentially introduces story complications.

**Resistance Rolls**: When consequences occur, players can resist by rolling dice equal to the relevant attribute and spending stress equal to 6 minus the highest die result. You can always mitigate consequences, but it costs resources, creating push-your-luck dynamics.

**Crew Sheet**: The criminal crew has its own character sheet tracking crew type (assassins, smugglers, cultists, etc.), reputation, turfs, upgrades, and heat. The crew advances alongside individual characters, creating organizational play alongside personal advancement.

**Downtime System**: After each score, the game enters structured downtime. Players spend downtime actions to recover stress, work on long-term projects, gather information, reduce heat, or pursue vice. This formalizes the score-downtime-score rhythm essential to heist fiction.

**Heat and Wanted Level**: Criminal actions generate heat. Accumulate enough heat and you hit wanted levels, attracting Bluecoats (police) attention. This mechanizes escalation—successful criminals attract increasing pressure, forcing difficult choices about lying low versus pursuing opportunities.

**Entanglements**: When heat reaches certain thresholds or during downtime, roll for entanglements—complications from previous scores. Maybe the Lampblacks want revenge, or you attract a rival crew's attention, or your patron demands a favor. Entanglements ensure consequences ripple through the campaign.

**Clocks**: Progress clocks track ongoing situations. A four-clock might track an investigation; an eight-clock might represent a rival gang's scheme. Fill segments through successful actions or when complications occur. Clocks provide visual progress tracking and pacing tools for multiple simultaneous situations.

**Factions**: Doskvol contains forty faction groups, each with ratings representing their strength and influence. Actions affect faction standings—help the Bluecoats, they like you more; hurt them, they become enemies. The faction game creates dynamic political landscapes responding to player actions.

**Load**: Before scores, players declare their load (light, normal, heavy) affecting their action ratings. During play, they reveal specific equipment they brought as needed, up to their load limit. This eliminates equipment list tedium while maintaining meaningful choices.

**Cohorts**: Crews can recruit cohorts—gangs or expert NPCs providing capabilities. A gang of thugs, a ghost-hunting expert, or a boat crew can be deployed as crew assets, expanding the crew's capabilities and creating attachments the GM can threaten.

**Harm**: Injury is tracked in levels (lesser, moderate, severe, deadly) with narrative descriptors. Take level 2 harm: "Battered." Take level 3: "Broken Leg." Harm provides penalties until healed, and severe harm can progress to worse without treatment. It's deadlier than D&D but more forgiving than one-hit-you're-out systems.

**Free Play, Score, Downtime Loop**: The game structure alternates between free play (gathering information, roleplay, setup), scores (the missions—structured scenes with engagement rolls determining starting position), and downtime (recovery and advancement). This loop provides rhythm and pacing while supporting the genre's demands.

## Cultural Impact

Blades in the Dark's impact extended far beyond its own success, influencing indie design and establishing a new framework tradition.

**Forged in the Dark Movement**: Like Apocalypse World before it, Blades spawned a design movement. The FitD SRD enabled derivative games across genres:
- **Scum and Villainy** - Space opera heists
- **Band of Blades** - Military horror survival
- **A Torch in the Dark** - Weird West
- **Beam Saber** - Mecha war drama
- **Runners in the Shadows** - Cyberpunk heists
- **Heart: The City Beneath** - Nightmare dungeon delving
- Dozens more across every conceivable genre

This framework diversity demonstrated FitD's versatility and Harper's success in creating hackable structures.

**Position and Effect Influence**: The position/effect framework influenced even non-FitD games. Its explicit stake-setting before rolls addressed long-standing RPG problems around unclear consequences. Many systems adopted similar stake-negotiation mechanics.

**Flashback Normalization**: Flashback mechanics appeared in numerous subsequent games. The technique solved the planning-paralysis problem many heist-style games faced. Harper demonstrated that trusting players with narrative tools enhanced rather than undermined gameplay.

**Structured Campaign Frameworks**: Blades showed how procedural frameworks support rather than restrict emergent storytelling. The score-downtime loop, faction system, and crew advancement provided structure that generated stories organically. This influenced how designers approached campaign-level game design.

**Indie Production Quality**: The game's physical production—particularly the deluxe edition with leather cover, gold-edged pages, and ribbon bookmarks—demonstrated indie publishers could match or exceed corporate production values. This raised expectations for indie RPG presentation.

**Fiction-First Meets Crunchy**: Blades bridged indie narrative gaming and traditional procedural play. It offered extensive mechanics while maintaining fiction-first principles. This demonstrated the approaches weren't incompatible, influencing games seeking middle ground between OSR crunch and story game freeform.

**GM Guidance Evolution**: Like Apocalypse World, Blades provided extensive GM frameworks—guidelines for running scores, setting positions, creating clocks, managing factions. This continued the trend of teaching GM craft explicitly rather than assuming knowledge.

**Twitch and Actual-Play**: Several popular actual-play streams featured Blades, including RollPlay: Blades by itmeJP and The Bloodletters by LA By Night cast. The game's heist structure created natural dramatic arcs perfect for episodic viewing.

**Design Conversation**: Blades contributed vocabulary to design discussions. Terms like "position and effect," "flashback mechanics," and "clock systems" entered common usage, enabling more precise design communication.

## Design Philosophy

Blades in the Dark's design philosophy synthesized narrative gaming principles with traditional procedural structures.

**Fiction First, Mechanics Support**: Harper insisted mechanics should support and emerge from fiction, not drive it. You don't "make an Action roll"—you describe actions that trigger rolls. The mechanics formalize and resolve fictional positioning rather than replacing it.

**Explicit Stakes**: The game makes stakes explicit through position and effect. Everyone knows what they're risking and what success achieves before rolling. This transparency prevents table disputes and enables informed player decision-making.

**Fail Forward**: No roll stalls the story. Failed rolls introduce complications or lead to worse situations, but always move things forward. The game structurally prevents "you try the door, it's locked" dead-ends.

**Player Agency and Competency**: Flashbacks, resistance rolls, and load systems assume player characters are competent criminals. You're not struggling to pick basic locks—you're dealing with sophisticated security while managing complications. Mechanics support portraying capable characters facing challenging situations.

**Meaningful Choices**: Every system creates genuine choices. Spend stress to resist or accept consequences? Accept a devil's bargain? Go for risky effect or controlled safety? Choices have mechanical and narrative weight, preventing optimal strategies from emerging.

**Procedural Emergent Story**: The structured score-downtime loop, faction systems, and entanglement tables generate stories procedurally. The GM doesn't prepare plots—they prepare situations, threats, and factions, then let interactions create stories.

**Resource Management as Drama**: Stress, heat, coin, crew status—these resources create dramatic tension. Pushing too hard risks trauma; playing too safe leaves you poor and weak. Managing resources becomes inherently dramatic.

**Death Is Rare But Meaningful**: Characters rarely die outright, but trauma accumulation eventually forces retirement. This creates investment in characters who might last entire campaigns while maintaining genuine stakes.

**Transparency**: Blades embraces mechanical transparency. Players see position and effect. They know devil's bargains before accepting. The GM makes mechanics visible rather than hiding them behind screens. This builds trust and enables tactical play.

**Genre Emulation**: Every mechanic serves the heist-crime genre. Flashbacks enable competent planning. Heat mechanizes escalation. Crews represent organizations. The system doesn't try to be universal—it focuses on doing one thing exceptionally well.

## Setting and Themes

Doskvol, the game's default setting, is integral to Blades' design rather than interchangeable backdrop.

**The Shattered Isles**: The world suffered a cataclysm centuries ago that shattered reality. The sun went dark. Demons and ghosts plague the world. Humanity survives in lightning-barrier-protected cities powered by leviathan blood harvested from demon-infested seas. Doskvol is one such city—industrial, corrupt, and haunted.

**Electroplasmic Technology**: The setting's industrial revolution runs on refined leviathan blood—electroplasm—which powers lightning barriers, industrial processes, and various technologies. This creates a Victorian-industrial aesthetic with weird supernatural edges.

**Ghosts and Spirits**: Death is complicated in Doskvol. Ghosts linger unless properly disposed of via cremation or spirit trafficking. The Spirit Wardens, the city's ghost police, ensure proper death rites—for a price. This makes murder messy; bodies are evidence, but so are ghosts.

**Factions**: Forty detailed factions populate Doskvol—criminal gangs, noble houses, labor unions, religious cults, elite social clubs, government agencies. Each has agendas, relationships, and resources. Players navigate this complex political landscape, making allies and enemies through their actions.

**Districts**: Doskvol's districts have distinct character—Crow's Foot (criminal heart), Brightstone (wealthy district), Charterhall (university and libraries), Dunslough (industrial hellscape). Each offers different opportunities and challenges.

**Industrialization's Cost**: The setting examines industrialization's human toll. Factories exploit workers. Aristocrats profit while laborers suffer. The city's wealth comes from dangerous leviathan hunting and spirit exploitation. Themes of class, exploitation, and survival pervade.

**Moral Ambiguity**: You play criminals. Maybe you're Robin Hood figures stealing from oppressors, or maybe you're brutal gangsters. The game doesn't judge, letting tables explore their own moral boundaries. Every crew type offers different ethical territories.

**The Score**: The heist itself is central. The game provides frameworks for different score types—burglary, espionage, smuggling, assassination, occult operations, demolition. Each requires different approaches and creates different complications.

**Supernatural Crime**: The occult is commodified in Doskvol. You might steal a demon's true name, smuggle ghosts to the spirit trade, or infiltrate the Dimmer Sisters' sorcerous order. Magic is treated like any other resource—exploitable, dangerous, valuable.

**Themes**:
- Survival in oppressive systems
- The price of ambition
- Loyalty versus self-interest
- Working-class struggles
- Supernatural horror as daily reality
- Community in adversity
- The cycle of violence and revenge

## Reception and Legacy

Blades in the Dark achieved critical and commercial success while influencing game design broadly.

**Critical Acclaim**:
- Won 2016 ENnie Gold for Best Rules
- Won 2016 ENnie Gold for Best Game
- Won 2016 ENnie Silver for Product of the Year
- Extensively reviewed with near-universal praise
- Featured in major gaming publications as innovative design
- Academic game studies examined its mechanical innovations

**Commercial Success**:
- $330,000+ Kickstarter from $3,000 goal
- Sold tens of thousands of physical copies
- Extensive international translations (French, German, Spanish, Italian, Polish, more)
- Multiple print runs and special editions
- Sustained sales years after release

**Framework Legacy**: The Forged in the Dark framework became one of indie gaming's dominant design templates alongside PbtA. The FitD SRD enabled community development, ensuring the framework's evolution beyond Harper's original vision.

**Actual-Play Success**: Multiple successful actual-play series featured Blades:
- RollPlay: Blades garnered millions of views
- The Bloodletters reached substantial audiences
- Numerous smaller streams built communities around the game
- The game's heist structure proved excellent for episodic content

**Design Influence**: Position and effect, flashback mechanics, structured downtime, crew-level play, and clock systems influenced games across genres and styles. Designers studied Blades as exemplar of how mechanical precision supports narrative play.

**Community Engagement**: Active communities developed around Blades:
- Extensive fan-created content
- Multiple fan-created games and hacks
- Vibrant Discord communities
- Fan-created tools, character keepers, and resources
- International player communities

**Teaching Material**: The game became studied in game design courses and indie design communities as an example of successful iteration, clear design philosophy, and framework development.

**Professional Validation**: Evil Hat's involvement brought professional distribution and production while maintaining the game's indie roots. This demonstrated successful indie-professional partnerships.

**Accessibility Through Design**: Despite mechanical sophistication, Blades proved accessible. The structured procedures—engagement rolls, score structure, downtime phases—provided clear frameworks that helped new players engage with narrative gaming.

**Criticism and Refinement**: The game faced some criticisms:
- Learning curve for players accustomed to traditional systems
- GM burden for managing forty factions, multiple clocks, and complex situations
- Stress economy sometimes led to slow play as players calculated optimal actions
- The setting's darkness not everyone's preference
- Some found the structure too prescriptive for their improv preferences

These criticisms generally focused on preference rather than design flaws, reflecting that Blades served specific purposes exceptionally well rather than trying to be universal.

**Continued Development**: Harper and Evil Hat supported the game post-release with supplements:
- **Scoundrel's Pack** - Additional materials and assets
- Various setting expansion materials
- Community content through licensing

**Industry Impact**: Blades demonstrated that indie designers could create mechanically sophisticated systems that achieve commercial and critical success while spawning their own design movements. It proved narrative gaming could support procedural complexity. It showed framework thinking—creating adaptable systems—could build communities around design approaches rather than just individual games.

By establishing the Forged in the Dark framework alongside Powered by the Apocalypse, Blades ensured indie gaming had multiple robust frameworks supporting diverse design explorations. The game's influence continues growing as new FitD games reach audiences and its innovations propagate through the broader design community.

Blades in the Dark represents indie gaming's sophistication—professionally produced, mechanically refined, thoughtfully designed, and culturally influential. It proved John Harper's design vision and demonstrated that careful attention to structure, procedure, and player experience could create something genuinely innovative that advances the medium.
