# Feng Shui

**Publisher**: Daedalus Entertainment (1996), Atlas Games (later)
**Designer**: [[Robin D. Laws]]
**Year**: 1996
**System**: Custom (Action Movie)
**Genre**: Hong Kong Action Cinema, Time Travel
**Status**: in-print

## Historical Context

Feng Shui, designed by Robin D. Laws and published by Daedalus Entertainment in 1996, revolutionized how tabletop RPGs approached action and cinematic combat. Subtitled "The Roleplaying Game of Action Adventure," it aimed to recreate Hong Kong action cinema—John Woo gun fu, Jackie Chan physical comedy, Tsui Hark wire-fu, and classic martial arts films—at the gaming table. The game asked a fundamental question: why did RPG combat feel like tactical simulation rather than action movie excitement? Laws' answer was comprehensive: redesign everything around cinematic pacing, dramatic description, and over-the-top action.

The mid-1990s RPG market was dominated by tactical combat systems. D&D, GURPS, Shadowrun—all featured detailed position-tracking, careful action economy, and realistic consequences. These systems created engaging tactical puzzles but rarely felt like action movies. Characters spent rounds getting into position, combat dragged across multiple sessions, and realistic damage systems punished heroic recklessness. Laws recognized fundamental disconnect between action movie logic and simulation-focused game design.

Hong Kong action cinema provided perfect reference. John Woo's heroic bloodshed films featured protagonists sliding across floors while dual-wielding pistols, diving through windows in slow motion, and emerging victorious from impossible odds. Jackie Chan's films combined martial arts, physical comedy, and improvised prop-fighting. Tsui Hark's wuxia fantasies showed warriors flying through air, wielding impossible weapons, and battling supernatural foes. These films operated on different logic than realistic combat—style over tactics, momentum over caution, description over positioning.

Laws had already demonstrated innovative thinking with Over the Edge's freeform traits. With Feng Shui, he tackled action specifically. The design challenge was substantial: create system that rewarded cinematic description, maintained action-movie pacing, encouraged over-the-top stunts, and made every character feel like action hero. Traditional mechanics seemed inadequate.

The breakthrough came through multiple interconnected innovations. The shot counter replaced traditional initiative, tracking everyone's position in action sequence like movie scenes. Stunt mechanics rewarded creative description with mechanical bonuses. Character archetypes from genre films (Killer, Martial Artist, Cop, Ex-Special Forces, Scrappy Kid, etc.) enabled instant recognizable character creation. And streamlined combat resolution kept action flowing rather than bogging down in tactical minutiae.

The setting proved equally crucial. Rather than generic modern world or standard fantasy, Laws created the Secret War—factions battling across time to control chi flow through four critical Junctures (69 AD ancient China, 1850s Shaolin temple era, contemporary Hong Kong 1996, dystopian 2056 future). This time-travel framework justified mixing genres, archetypes, and action styles. Your party might include ancient sorcerer, 1850s kung fu master, modern cop, and cybernetic future soldier—all fighting together against supernatural conspiracies.

Daedalus Entertainment published Feng Shui in 1996 to strong critical reception. Action-movie enthusiasts and Hong Kong cinema fans embraced it immediately. Critics praised the innovative shot counter, cinematic combat mechanics, and how faithfully the system recreated genre conventions. The game won Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Game of 1996, validating Laws' approach.

The timing proved fortunate. Mid-1990s saw Hong Kong cinema gaining Western cult following through video releases. Films like Hard Boiled, Drunken Master II, and Once Upon a Time in China found audiences outside Asia. The Matrix (1999) would soon bring Hong Kong action aesthetics to mainstream cinema. Feng Shui rode this cultural wave, providing tabletop experience for fans wanting to participate in action movie narratives.

Atlas Games acquired the line and continued supporting it with supplements exploring different Junctures, adding character types, and expanding the Secret War mythology. The support demonstrated publisher commitment while building devoted community.

Feng Shui's influence extended beyond its own player base. The shot counter influenced initiative thinking in numerous games. Mechanics rewarding cinematic description appeared in Spirit of the Century, Fate, and other narrative-forward games. The demonstration that RPGs could successfully emulate specific media genres influenced licensed games and genre-focused designs.

Robin D. Laws himself built on Feng Shui's innovations in subsequent designs. His work on HeroQuest, Gumshoe, and other systems showed continued interest in matching mechanics to genre expectations. Feng Shui established his reputation as designer who understood how mechanical choices create genre feel.

Second edition released in 2015 through Kickstarter, updated for modern sensibilities while maintaining core innovations. The successful funding demonstrated sustained interest two decades after original publication.

Feng Shui proved that tabletop RPGs could successfully recreate specific cinematic experiences through genre-focused mechanical design. It showed that initiative could be more than turn order, combat more than tactical positioning, and character creation could start with archetype recognition rather than point allocation. These lessons influenced action-oriented game design broadly while creating devoted following for its specific Hong Kong action niche.

## Mechanical Innovations

Feng Shui introduced revolutionary mechanics specifically designed to create action-movie experiences:

**The Shot Counter**: Feng Shui's most famous innovation replaced traditional initiative. The shot counter works like film sequence tracking:
- Start at Shot 0
- Character actions cost different shot amounts based on complexity
- After acting, advance your position on shot counter by action cost
- Next person to act is whoever's currently at lowest shot number
- This creates dynamic action flow where quick actions let you act again soon, slow actions leave you vulnerable

This mimics action editing—quick jabs and shots let characters stay active; big elaborate moves create dramatic pauses. The counter creates cinematic rhythm impossible with round-based initiative.

**Stunt Mechanics**: Characters can describe elaborate stunts for mechanical bonuses. Slide across bar while firing dual pistols? Swing from chandelier to kick opponent? Use enemy as weapon? Each stunt grants bonus dice if description is sufficiently cinematic. This mechanically rewards the elaborate action descriptions central to genre films.

**Archetype-Based Characters**: Rather than point-buy or class-level systems, Feng Shui provides archetypal character templates straight from Hong Kong cinema:
- **Killer**: Professional assassin (John Woo heroes)
- **Martial Artist**: Kung fu master (Jackie Chan, Jet Li characters)
- **Cop**: Dedicated detective (Hong Kong action cop films)
- **Ex-Special Forces**: Military veteran (action veterans)
- **Scrappy Kid**: Young street fighter (young Jackie Chan roles)
- **Karate Cop**: Japanese martial artist officer
- **Old Master**: Wise aged kung fu teacher
- **Sorcerer**: Ancient magic user
- Many more covering genre staples

Pick archetype, customize slightly, and play. This speeds character creation while ensuring genre-appropriate characters.

**Mooks and Named Characters**: Enemies divide into mooks (faceless minions defeated easily) and named characters (worthy opponents requiring real effort). Mooks go down in single hits; named characters fight like player characters. This recreates action movie logic where heroes mow through dozens of unnamed henchmen but struggle against rival protagonists.

**Positive/Negative Dice**: The core resolution uses positive dice (your competence) versus negative dice (opposition/difficulty). Roll both, subtract negative from positive, compare to target number. This creates dynamic rolls where both luck and skill matter.

**Schticks**: Special abilities called "schticks" (Cantonese slang) represent signature moves and talents. Gun schticks include "Carnival of Carnage" (mow through mooks dramatically), "Hair-Trigger Neck Hairs" (never surprised). Martial arts schticks include "Flying Windmill Kick," "Claw of the Tiger," etc. These abilities are explicitly genre moves from films.

**Fortune Dice**: Characters earn Fortune dice for acting appropriately cinematic—making one-liners, attempting impossible stunts, dramatic descriptions. Fortune dice add to important rolls, creating mechanical reward for genre-appropriate behavior.

**No Hit Locations**: Damage is abstract wound points rather than detailed hit locations. This keeps combat fast and simple, avoiding tactical positioning minutiae that would slow cinematic pacing.

**Unified Resolution**: Same basic mechanic (positive vs. negative dice) handles combat, skills, social interaction, and challenges. This prevents system-switching that breaks action flow.

**Improvised Weapons**: Special rules encourage using environment as weapons. Bar stools, bottles, motorcycles, anything becomes weapon with improvised weapon rules. This recreates Jackie Chan-style prop fighting.

**Vehicle Combat**: Fast, simple vehicle rules let car chases, boat pursuits, and motorcycle fights flow as smoothly as personal combat. Vehicles use similar mechanics to characters, maintaining system consistency.

**Fast Healing**: Characters recover quickly between fights, ensuring they're fresh for next action sequence. This matches action movie logic where heroes recover from brutal fights to face next challenge soon after.

**Chi Powers**: Magic and supernatural abilities use chi system based on Asian mysticism. Sorcerers, transformed animals, and supernatural characters use chi for special effects, grounding powers in setting-appropriate framework.

## Cultural Impact

Feng Shui's cultural impact demonstrated how genre-focused design could create distinctive gaming experiences:

**Action Movie Gaming Pioneer**: Feng Shui was first major RPG explicitly designed to recreate action cinema. While previous games featured action, Feng Shui made cinematic action fundamental design goal. This pioneered genre-emulation approaches influencing subsequent licensed games and genre-focused designs.

**Hong Kong Cinema Introduction**: For many Western gamers, Feng Shui was introduction to Hong Kong action cinema. The game's bibliography pointed players toward John Woo, Jackie Chan, Tsui Hark, and other filmmakers. This educational role helped spread appreciation for Hong Kong cinema among gaming communities.

**Shot Counter Influence**: The shot counter influenced initiative thinking in numerous subsequent games. Designers studying Feng Shui recognized that initiative could create pacing and drama rather than merely ordering actions. This influenced various action-focused games and combat systems.

**Stunt Reward Mechanics**: Mechanics rewarding cinematic description with mechanical bonuses appeared in Spirit of the Century, Fate, and many narrative games. Feng Shui demonstrated that rewarding description could encourage the play behaviors designers wanted.

**Archetype-Based Design**: The success of archetype templates influenced character creation in numerous games. Spirit of the Century, Fate Accelerated, and other games adopted similar "pick recognizable archetype, customize slightly" approaches.

**Mook Rules**: The mook/named character distinction appeared in D&D 4th Edition (minions), 13th Age (mooks), and numerous other games. Feng Shui proved this video game logic worked wonderfully in tabletop, creating satisfying combat against numerous weak opponents.

**Critical Recognition**:
- Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Game (1996)
- Widespread critical praise for innovation
- Featured in discussions of cinematic gaming
- Studied for genre-emulation mechanics
- Recognized for cultural bridge-building

**Community Development**:
- Devoted fanbase sustained the game
- Active online communities shared resources
- Convention play introduced new audiences
- Actual play demonstrated system's cinematic potential
- Fan supplements expanded content

**Second Edition Success**: The 2015 Feng Shui 2 Kickstarter raised over $200,000, demonstrating sustained interest two decades later. Backers included original fans and new players discovering Hong Kong action gaming.

**Design Influence**: Robin D. Laws' reputation as innovative designer solidified through Feng Shui. His subsequent work on HeroQuest, Gumshoe, and other systems built on lessons from genre-focused mechanical design.

**Cultural Timing**: Feng Shui's 1996 release positioned it perfectly for mid-1990s Hong Kong cinema cult following. The game rode cultural wave of increasing Western appreciation for Asian action films.

**Matrix Effect**: The Matrix (1999) brought Hong Kong action choreography to mainstream cinema, creating renewed interest in wire-fu and gun-fu. Feng Shui was positioned perfectly to serve gamers wanting to recreate Matrix-style action.

**Wuxia Gaming**: Feng Shui introduced Western audiences to wuxia (martial arts fantasy) genre, demonstrating how RPGs could incorporate flying warriors, mystical kung fu, and ancient Chinese fantasy elements.

**Time Travel Framework**: The Junctures setting showed how time travel could justify genre-mixing, allowing historical, contemporary, and science fiction elements in single campaign. This influenced genre-crossing game design.

## Design Philosophy

Feng Shui's design philosophy prioritized genre emulation over simulation:

**Genre Fidelity Over Realism**: Every design choice served genre emulation. Real-world combat logic was irrelevant; action movie logic was paramount. This commitment to genre over simulation created distinctively cinematic play.

**Mechanical Reward for Description**: The game mechanically rewarded players for describing actions cinematically. Stunt bonuses, Fortune dice, and GM encouragement made elaborate description mechanically advantageous, not just color.

**Pacing Through Mechanics**: The shot counter creates action-movie pacing. Quick actions keep you active; elaborate moves create dramatic moments. This mechanical pacing recreates how action films edit sequences.

**Archetypes Enable Instant Recognition**: Using film archetypes let players immediately understand characters without explanation. "I'm playing the Killer" instantly communicated character type, enabling fast buy-in and shared reference.

**Accessibility**: Simple core mechanics (positive vs. negative dice) made the game approachable. Complexity came through options and schticks, not baroque core system.

**Every Character Is Action Hero**: Unlike games where some characters are support or non-combat, Feng Shui assumed everyone was action-movie protagonist. Every archetype had combat capacity and cool moves.

**Encourage Awesome**: The game explicitly encouraged over-the-top action. Be ridiculous. Attempt impossible stunts. Make dramatic declarations. The system rewarded rather than punished action-movie logic.

**Setting Serves Action**: The Secret War and Junctures existed to justify constant action and genre-mixing. Setting served gameplay rather than constraining it.

**Simple Resolution, Complex Options**: Basic roll mechanic was simple, but schticks, stunts, and tactical options created depth. This balanced accessibility with interesting choices.

**Fail Forward**: Like action movies where setbacks lead to dramatic reversals, Feng Shui encouraged treating failures as complications rather than stopping points.

**Collaborative Cinematic Vision**: The game worked best when entire group committed to cinematic action. This collaborative buy-in created shared aesthetic everyone reinforced.

## Setting and Themes

Feng Shui's Secret War across Junctures created distinctive time-travel action setting:

**The Four Junctures**: Critical points in history where chi energy concentrates:
- **69 AD** - Ancient China, mythic past with sorcerers and supernatural beings
- **1850** - Shaolin temple era, golden age of kung fu
- **1996** - Contemporary Hong Kong, modern action
- **2056** - Dystopian future, cybernetic oppression

Controlling these Junctures lets factions reshape reality and history.

**The Secret War**: Hidden conflict between factions battling to control chi sites:
- **Ascended**: Supernatural creatures disguised as humans seeking domination
- **Lotus**: Eunuch sorcerers from ancient China pursuing magical power
- **Architects**: Future technocrats imposing totalitarian order
- **Dragons**: Freedom fighters resisting all tyrannies
- **Jammers**: Chaos-loving anarchists disrupting everyone's plans
- **Guiding Hand**: Shaolin monks protecting traditional values

**Chi Sites**: Locations where mystical energy concentrates, called Feng Shui sites. Controlling these sites grants power over time and reality. The Secret War focuses on capturing and holding chi sites across Junctures.

**Netherworld**: Mystical realm connecting Junctures, allowing time travel. Portals exist at certain locations, enabling movement between eras. This explains how ancient sorcerers, historical kung fu masters, modern cops, and future soldiers fight together.

**Genre Mixing**: The Junctures justify combining genres—ancient sorcery, historical martial arts, contemporary action, cyberpunk future—in single campaign. Your party is inherently cross-genre.

**Hong Kong as Nexus**: Contemporary Hong Kong serves as primary setting, reflecting the city's status as Hong Kong action cinema capital. The city's cinematic geography becomes play space.

**Action Movie Logic**: The setting operates on action movie rules:
- Conspiracies are real and dangerous
- Ordinary people can be action heroes
- Ancient mysticism exists alongside modern technology
- Good guys can win against impossible odds
- Style matters as much as substance

**Time Travel Freedom**: Unlike hard science fiction time travel, Feng Shui's mystical time travel avoids paradoxes and complications. Just get to portals, travel to different Junctures, fight enemies. Simple.

**Themes**:
- **Heroism Against Tyranny**: Fighting oppression across time
- **Style and Spectacle**: How you succeed matters as much as success
- **Cultural Heritage**: Protecting traditions and values worth preserving
- **Friendship and Loyalty**: Companions bonding through shared dangers
- **Freedom vs. Control**: The central conflict between oppressive order and chaotic liberty
- **Impossible Odds**: Facing overwhelming opposition with courage and skill

**Cinematic Geography**: Settings are action movie sets—temples with training grounds, nightclubs with hidden passages, corporate towers hiding laboratories, ancient tombs with death traps. Geography serves action sequences.

**Endless Action**: The Secret War never ends, ensuring perpetual conflicts, conspiracies, and action. This sustains ongoing campaigns without requiring elaborate metaplot.

## Reception and Legacy

Feng Shui achieved critical acclaim and lasting influence as definitive action movie RPG:

**Critical Acclaim**:
- Origins Award for Best Roleplaying Game (1996)
- Extensive positive reviews praising innovation
- Recognition as genre-emulation pioneer
- Featured in game design discussions constantly
- Academic attention for cinematic mechanics
- Cited as essential action gaming experience

**Commercial Success**: While never mainstream blockbuster, Feng Shui sustained publication across two editions and numerous supplements. Devoted fanbase kept the game commercially viable.

**Two Editions**:
- **First Edition** (1996): Revolutionary original
- **Second Edition** (2015): Modernized, refined, expanded

**Extensive Support**: Supplements explored different Junctures, added character types, expanded Secret War factions, and provided adventure frameworks. This demonstrated sustained publisher and player interest.

**Kickstarter Validation**: Feng Shui 2's successful 2015 Kickstarter ($200,000+) proved sustained market demand two decades after original publication. The funding enabled quality second edition production.

**Design Influence**: Feng Shui influenced numerous subsequent games:
- **Spirit of the Century**: Pulp action using stunts and aspects derived from Feng Shui thinking
- **Fate**: Stunt mechanics and rewarding description
- **D&D 4th Edition**: Minion rules based on mooks
- **13th Age**: Mook mechanics and cinematic combat
- Various action-focused games studying Feng Shui's approaches

**Robin D. Laws' Legacy**: Established Laws as major designer. His subsequent work on HeroQuest, Gumshoe systems, and other designs built on Feng Shui's genre-focused approach.

**Hong Kong Cinema Gateway**: Introduced Western gamers to Hong Kong action cinema, creating cultural bridge and spreading appreciation for John Woo, Jackie Chan, Tsui Hark, and others.

**Shot Counter Legacy**: The shot counter influenced how designers thought about initiative, pacing, and action sequencing. While not universally adopted, it demonstrated alternatives to round-based turn order.

**Archetype Success**: Demonstrated that archetype-based character creation could work beautifully, influencing Spirit of the Century, Fate Accelerated, and numerous other games.

**Community Longevity**: Active communities sustained the game through online forums, convention play, and fan materials. The devoted following kept Feng Shui relevant across decades.

**Actual Play**: The game's cinematic nature made it excellent for actual play demonstrations at conventions. Watching Feng Shui created excitement that reading about it couldn't match.

**Criticisms and Limitations**:
- **Narrow Focus**: Genre-specific design meant limited appeal beyond action enthusiasts
- **Learning Curve**: Shot counter confused some players initially
- **Balance Issues**: Some archetypes and schticks were stronger than others
- **Setting Complexity**: The Secret War could feel overwhelming
- **Cultural Representation**: Some criticism about Western designers handling Asian cultural elements

**Cultural Sensitivity**: Modern discussions note concerns about cultural appropriation and representation in Feng Shui's use of Asian cultural elements. Second edition attempted more thoughtful approaches.

**Modern Relevance**: In era of increased licensed gaming and genre-focused designs, Feng Shui's approaches remain relevant and influential.

**Academic Recognition**: Game studies scholars examined Feng Shui for genre emulation mechanics, cultural representation issues, and how mechanical design creates specific play experiences.

**Convention Staple**: The game became popular convention one-shot choice. Single-session action-packed Feng Shui games showcased system strengths perfectly.

**Design Lessons**: Feng Shui taught important lessons:
- Mechanics can create genre feel
- Initiative affects pacing dramatically
- Rewarding description encourages desired behavior
- Archetypes enable quick character recognition
- Genre fidelity matters more than realism
- Simple cores with complex options balance accessibility and depth

**Influence on Licensed Games**: Feng Shui's success at genre emulation influenced how licensed RPGs approached adapting specific media properties. The demonstration that mechanics should serve genre influenced Star Wars, Marvel, and other licensed designs.

**Enduring Community**: Decades after publication, communities still play, discuss, and create Feng Shui content. This longevity demonstrates genuine lasting appeal beyond initial novelty.

**Second Edition Reception**: Feng Shui 2 received warm reception for updating while maintaining core innovations. The modernization proved the original design remained sound, requiring refinement rather than revolution.

**Final Assessment**: Feng Shui stands as definitive action movie RPG and important innovation in genre-focused design. Its mechanical innovations—particularly shot counter, stunt rewards, and mook rules—influenced subsequent games significantly. More broadly, it demonstrated that tabletop RPGs could successfully recreate specific cinematic experiences through genre-appropriate mechanical design.

The game proved that matching mechanics to genre created authentic feel simulation-based approaches couldn't achieve. For action gaming, Feng Shui remains touchstone—studied, played, and referenced as exemplar of how to make tabletop combat feel like action cinema rather than tactical simulation. Its influence extends beyond its own play to broader understanding of how mechanical choices create genre experiences, making it genuinely important contribution to RPG design thinking.
